# Chapter 2: Studying Social Life — Sociological Research Methods
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term                                 | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Quantitative research**            | Research that translates the social world into **numbers** that can be treated mathematically; often tries to find cause-and-effect relationships.                                        |
| **Qualitative research**             | Research that works with **nonnumerical data** — texts, field notes, interview transcripts, photographs, tape recordings; often tries to understand how people make sense of their world. |
| **Scientific method**                | A procedure for acquiring knowledge that emphasizes collecting **concrete data** through observation and experimentation.                                                                 |
| **Literature review**                | A thorough search through previously published studies relevant to a particular topic.                                                                                                    |
| **Hypothesis**                       | A theoretical statement explaining the relationship between two or more phenomena.                                                                                                        |
| **Variables**                        | Two or more phenomena that a researcher believes are related; examined in the experiment.                                                                                                 |
| **Operational definition**           | A clear and precise definition of a variable that facilitates its measurement.                                                                                                            |
| **Replicability**                    | The ability of research to be repeated and, thus, later verified by other researchers.                                                                                                    |
| **Correlation**                      | A relationship between variables in which they **change together** — may or may not be causal.                                                                                            |
| **Causation**                        | A relationship between variables in which a change in one **directly produces** a change in the other.                                                                                    |
| **Intervening variable**             | A third variable, sometimes overlooked, that explains the relationship between two other variables.                                                                                       |
| **Spurious correlation**             | The appearance of causation produced by an **intervening variable**.                                                                                                                      |
| **Deductive approach**               | Researcher formulates a **hypothesis first**, then gathers data to test it (theory → data).                                                                                               |
| **Inductive approach**               | Researcher gathers **data first**, then formulates a theory to fit the data (data → theory).                                                                                              |
| **Ethnography**                      | A naturalistic method based on studying people in their **own environment** to understand the meanings they attribute to their activities; also the written work that results.            |
| **Participant observation**          | A methodology associated with ethnography whereby the researcher both **observes and becomes a member** in a social setting.                                                              |
| **Rapport**                          | A positive relationship often characterized by mutual trust or sympathy.                                                                                                                  |
| **Field notes**                      | Detailed notes taken by an ethnographer describing their activities and interactions; later become the basis of analysis.                                                                 |
| **Autoethnography**                  | A form of participant observation in which the **feelings and actions of the researcher** become a focal point of the study.                                                              |
| **Thick description**                | Detailed data on interactions and meaning **within a cultural context**, from the perspective of its members (Geertz).                                                                    |
| **Reflexivity**                      | How the **identity and activities of the researcher** influence what goes on in the field setting.                                                                                        |
| **Grounded theory**                  | An inductive method of generating theory from data by creating categories and looking for **relationships among categories** (Glaser & Strauss 1967).                                     |
| **Representativeness**               | The degree to which a studied group is similar to, or represents, any part of the larger society.                                                                                         |
| **Validity**                         | The **accuracy** of a question/measurement tool; whether the researcher is measuring what they think they're measuring.                                                                   |
| **Reliability**                      | The **consistency** of a question/measurement tool; whether the same questions produce similar answers.                                                                                   |
| **Interviews**                       | Person-to-person conversations for gathering information by means of questions posed to respondents.                                                                                      |
| **Respondent**                       | A participant in a study from whom the researcher seeks to gather information.                                                                                                            |
| **Target population**                | The entire group about which a researcher would like to be able to generalize.                                                                                                            |
| **Sample**                           | The members of the target population who will actually be studied.                                                                                                                        |
| **Focus group**                      | Interviewing a number of participants together, allowing interaction among group members.                                                                                                 |
| **Informed consent**                 | Safeguard ensuring respondents are **freely participating** and understand the nature of the research.                                                                                    |
| **Closed-ended question**            | Imposes a limit on the possible responses.                                                                                                                                                |
| **Open-ended question**              | Allows the answer to take whatever form the respondent chooses.                                                                                                                           |
| **Leading questions**                | Predispose a respondent to answer in a certain way.                                                                                                                                       |
| **Double-barreled questions**        | Get at **multiple issues at once**; tend to receive incomplete or confusing answers.                                                                                                      |
| **Life history**                     | Interview approach asking for a **chronological account** of the respondent's life or some portion of it.                                                                                 |
| **Surveys**                          | Questionnaires administered to a sample of respondents selected from a target population.                                                                                                 |
| **Likert scale**                     | Survey format where respondents choose an answer along a **continuum** ("strongly agree" → "strongly disagree").                                                                          |
| **Negative questions**               | Ask respondents what they **don't** think instead of what they do.                                                                                                                        |
| **Pilot study**                      | A small-scale study carried out to test the feasibility of a larger study.                                                                                                                |
| **Probability sampling**             | Any sampling procedure that uses **randomization**.                                                                                                                                       |
| **Simple random sample**             | Probability sample in which every member of the population has an **equal chance** of being selected.                                                                                     |
| **Response rate**                    | The number/percentage of surveys completed and returned to researchers.                                                                                                                   |
| **Existing sources**                 | Materials produced for some other reason but usable as data for social research.                                                                                                          |
| **Unobtrusive measures**             | Research methods relying on existing sources; researcher does not intrude on the social setting or its subjects.                                                                          |
| **Comparative historical research**  | Uses existing sources to study relationships among elements of society in **various regions and time periods**.                                                                           |
| **Content analysis**                 | Identifying and studying specific **variables or themes** that appear in a text, image, or media message.                                                                                 |
| **Experiments**                      | Formal tests of specific variables and effects, performed where all aspects of the situation can be **controlled**.                                                                       |
| **Experimental group**               | Test group members who receive the experimental treatment.                                                                                                                                |
| **Control group**                    | Test group members who continue **without intervention** so they can be compared to the experimental group.                                                                               |
| **Independent variable**             | The factor that is **predicted to cause change**.                                                                                                                                         |
| **Dependent variable**               | The factor that is **changed (or not)** by the independent variable.                                                                                                                      |
| **Basic research**                   | The search for knowledge **without an agenda or practical goal** in mind.                                                                                                                 |
| **Applied research**                 | The search for knowledge that **can be used to create social change**.                                                                                                                    |
| **Bias**                             | An opinion held by the researcher that might affect the research or analysis.                                                                                                             |
| **Objectivity**                      | Impartiality; the ability to allow the **facts to speak for themselves**.                                                                                                                 |
| **Reactivity**                       | The tendency of people and events to **react to the process of being studied**.                                                                                                           |
| **Hawthorne effect**                 | A specific example of reactivity — the desired effect results not from the independent variable but **from the research itself**.                                                         |
| **Deception**                        | The extent to which participants are unaware of the project or its goals.                                                                                                                 |
| **Confidentiality**                  | The assurance that **no one other than the researcher** will know the identity of a respondent.                                                                                           |
| **Code of ethics**                   | Ethical guidelines for researchers to consult as they design a project.                                                                                                                   |
| **Institutional review board (IRB)** | A group of scholars within a university who review and approve research proposals and protect human subjects.                                                                             |

## 1. An Overview of Research Methods

- **Theory vs. methods:** theories make hypothetical claims; **methods produce data** that support, disprove, or modify those claims.
- Opens with **Dave Barry's joke** that sociology is unintelligible gibberish (e.g., "prematurated isolates" for crying children). Point: sociology draws from the "hard" sciences but is often dismissed as "soft" — yet research can be conducted and written clearly.
- **Quantitative** — numerical data, statistical analysis (rates, percentages, charts). *Example:* in 2019, 32% of male drivers in fatal crashes had BAC ≥ 0.08% vs. 21% of female drivers.
- **Qualitative** — nonnumerical data (texts, field notes, transcripts, videos, photos); interpretive rather than statistical analysis; focuses on meaning from the perspective of those studied. *Ethnographers have studied:* long-haul truck drivers (Viscelli 2016), fashion models (Mears 2011), low-income students at elite colleges (Jack 2019).

### The Scientific Approach (see [[Science]])
- **Scientific method** = standard procedure for acquiring and verifying empirical (concrete, scientific) knowledge; builds and tests theory that explains and predicts the world.
- Steps/skills include: **literature review → hypothesis → variables → operational definitions → replicability → correlation vs. causation → deductive/inductive approaches.**
  - *Correlation ≠ causation:* ice cream sales & drowning both rise in summer (classic spurious relationship).
  - **Intervening variable** explains an apparent relationship; **spurious correlation** = causation that *looks* real but is produced by the intervening variable.
  - **Deductive:** hypothesis first → gather data to test it.
  - **Inductive:** data first → build theory to explain the data.
- **Thomas Kuhn (1962/1970):** truth is relative and depends on the **paradigm** through which one sees the world. Paradigms = broad theoretical models of how things work.
  - *Example:* geocentric (earth-centered) view ruled until **Copernicus (1543)** proposed heliocentrism → a **paradigm shift**, "a major break from the assumptions made by the previous model."
  - Paradigm shifts occur when new data force new ways of looking at the world — **and methods are what generate data.**

### Which Method to Use? — The Woodstock Example
Choosing methods trades one set of advantages/disadvantages for another; guided by research goals, researcher competence, time, funding, and access to respondents.

| Method | Woodstock application | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Participant observation** | Ideal at the time (1969) — be in the thick of it, firsthand data | Opportunity long past |
| **Interviews** | Attendees recount experiences | Hard to recruit (attendees worldwide), memory decayed after 50+ years |
| **Surveys** | Mail/online questionnaires | Recruitment still hard; trash-bin mailings; **impostors** claiming attendance |
| **Existing sources** | Published firsthand accounts, film, photography | Accounts are necessarily **selective** |
| **Experiments** | Replicate defining features (mass gathering of strangers, no official presence → peace) | Woodstock '99 "failed" (fires, violence, arrests) — but labs can test altruism, cooperation, trust between strangers |

> **Rest of chapter:** six methods in detail — ethnography/participant observation, interviews, surveys, existing sources, experimental methods, social network analysis — all illustrated by research on **"family dynamics."**

## 2. Ethnography / Participant Observation

- One of the most engaging methods; a **qualitative** method centered on **fieldwork** — research in naturally occurring social environments ("real world"), studying day-to-day lives firsthand.
- **Participant observation** = become a participant *and* an observer; often used interchangeably with ethnography.
- Involves **deep immersion** (months to years) to develop a **member's-eye view** — know the social world from the inside out.
- **Ethnography** literally = "writing [graphos] culture [ethnos]" — also the name for the **written product** (often a book).

### The process
1. **Gain entry/access** to the field site (some groups hard to approach; some places off-limits — yet sociologists study an astounding variety of social worlds).
2. **Establish rapport** (trust and acceptance) with subjects.
3. **Collect data** — primarily daily **field notes**; some photos/videos, but observational skills are most critical.
   - **Jottings** (brief notes: keywords, quotes, cocktail napkins, text messages) → elaborate at day's end; sometimes **"head notes"** (memory alone) when writing isn't possible.
4. **Analyze** — look for patterns and themes; fit data into categories ("episodes of conflict," "common vocabulary"); identify relationships among categories → **grounded theory** (Glaser & Strauss 1967). Ethnographers use an **inductive approach**.

### Thick description (Clifford Geertz 1973)
- Coined to convey well-written field notes: more than photographic detail — **sensitivity to context and interactional details** (facial expression, tone of voice).
- Explores all possible meanings of a phenomenon (e.g., a blinking eye) within a cultural setting.
- Good ethnography = systematic, holistic, and lets the reader see the world **from another's perspective**.

### Reflexivity
- Researcher's own social statuses (gender, age, race, parenthood) shape **access** and **knowledge**.
- The researcher's **presence affects** the group being observed; personal feelings (respect, contempt, curiosity, boredom) can influence observations. Especially important for ethnographers due to close personal ties.

### Overt vs. covert research
- **Overt** (preferred): open about sociological intentions — eliminates ethical problems of deception. Most ethnographers, e.g., Edin & Kefalas.
- **Covert:** observe without members knowing. *Example:* **David Calvey (2018)** worked as a bouncer in British clubs for years, hiding his researcher identity (studying masculinity & physical culture of bouncers); witnessed violence/criminality without intervening; risked being exposed as a fraud.

### Classic study: Edin & Kefalas — *Promises I Can Keep* (2005)
- Studied **poor urban single moms** in East Camden, NJ (Edin moved her whole family there for 2.5 years; joined a church, taught Sunday school, volunteered; Kefalas tutored at a GED program for teen mothers).
- 162 Black, white, and Puerto Rican mothers (avg. age 25; single parents; <$16,000/yr).
- **Finding:** motherhood was a **stabilizing agent**, often the only positive factor in these women's lives — contrary to mainstream assumptions. The perceived low cost of early child-rearing + the high value of mothering outweigh the difficulties.
- Being women and mothers themselves helped create rapport and access.

### Advantages
1. Richly detailed stories; studies groups whose stories otherwise go untold — deviant groups (young gang members cultivating violent online personae — Stuart 2020) *and* elites (prep school students preserving privilege — Khan 2011).
2. Challenges taken-for-granted notions (single moms ≠ irresponsible).
3. Can reshape stereotypes **and influence social policy**.
4. Source of pioneering methodological innovations (reflexivity, researcher roles).

### Disadvantages
1. **Representativeness** problem — can a small study apply to society at large? (Edin & Kefalas's East Camden findings arguably generalize to single mothers in other cities.)
2. **Resource-intensive** (time, effort, personal commitment) and **difficult to replicate** — unique combinations of people, timing, setting, researcher role mean no one can repeat the same study. Ethnographers boost validity by documenting methods/roles/relationships and sharing raw data (field note excerpts).

> **📋 Data Workshop — "Watching People Talk":** Practice writing field notes using **thick description**. (1) For 5–10 min, *listen* to a conversation you can't see (bus, restaurant); (2) for 5–10 min, *observe* a conversation you can't hear (across the quad). Write 2+ double-spaced pages of detailed field notes each, describing participants, setting, and what you think is going on. *Prep-Pair-Share:* mark passages you can see/hear (*), circle evaluative words/summaries, and mark "want to know more" (?). *DIY:* 2–3 page essay on the fieldwork experience + attach notes.

## 3. Interviews

- Face-to-face, information-seeking conversations to gather **qualitative data** directly from **respondents** — done systematically and more scientifically than TV interviews.
- May be the sole method or combined with others. **Interviews are always conducted by the researcher** (surveys may be self-administered).
- First identify the **target population** (e.g., all parents with children under 18), then select a **sample** representative of it, to make generalizations.
- Interview projects are usually **smaller in scope** than surveys; **focus groups** (5–10 participants interviewed together, allowing interaction) can increase sample size.
- **Informed consent** required — especially because most interviews are audio/video recorded.

### Question-writing
- **Closed-ended** ("Are you for or against couples living together before marriage?") vs. **open-ended** ("What do you think about couples living together before marriage?").
- Avoid: **biased/leading questions**, overly complex questions, **double-barreled questions** (multiple issues at once), ambiguous/inflammatory language.
- *Example:* "How difficult is her life?" only elicits difficulties; better: "Tell me about the pluses and minuses of single parenthood."
- **Life history** approach: chronological account of the respondent's life.
- Afterward, interviews are **transcribed** and analyzed for patterns; computers or hand sorting.

### Classic study: Hochschild — *The Second Shift* (1989)
- Studied couples in two-career families: who does the housework/childcare after the workday?
- Interviewed 50 couples + 45 others in their domestic arrangements (babysitters, day-care providers, teachers).
- **Categorized couples as "traditional," "transitional," or "egalitarian"** based on division of household labor; quantified time spent on chores.
- Findings generalize beyond the sample to similar couples elsewhere.

### Modern study: Dawn Marie Dow — *Mothering While Black* (2019)
- Interviews with 60 middle-/upper-middle-class Black moms on balancing child-rearing with race and gender.
- Key theme: race had to be part of everyday calculus even amid class advantages — e.g., moms coordinated so their kids wouldn't be "the only brown girl" in ballet; protecting children (esp. Black boys) from racism while preparing them for it.

### Advantages
1. Respondents **speak in their own words** — reveals thoughts, feelings, beliefs inaccessible any other way (authentic portrait in *The Second Shift*).
2. Can **dispel preconceptions** and uncover overlooked issues.

### Disadvantages
1. Some respondents are **selective/self-presentational** — **triangulate** with other methods (Hochschild observed some families: actual household labor was *less egalitarian* than couples claimed).
2. **Representativeness** problem — small samples; can we generalize from 50 couples? (Hochschild compared her couples against a large national survey.)

## 4. Surveys

- Questionnaires administered to a sample from a target population. **Macro & quantitative:** large-scale patterns, statistics.
- **Earliest user:** **Karl Marx** — in the 1880s sent questionnaires to 25,000+ French workers on employer exploitation (heavily influenced his writing).
- *Example institution:* National Marriage Project at Rutgers ("The State of Our Unions"); Guttmacher Institute (May 2020): ⅓ of American women delayed childbearing plans due to pandemic difficulties.

### Questionnaire design
- Mostly **closed-ended**; common format: **Likert scale** ("strongly agree" → "strongly disagree"); some "don't know"/"doesn't apply"; **open-ended write-in** questions give qualitative data.
- Avoid **leading**, **negative**, and **double-barreled** questions; watch question **order** (mentioning divorce/infidelity early colors later answers).
- **Pretest** with a small group; run a **pilot study** before the larger administration.

### Sampling
- **Probability sampling** = randomization; ensures an unbiased, representative sample.
- **Simple random sample** = every member has an equal chance (like drawing names from a hat).
- **Response rate:** even ~50% returned is considered very good; claims can be made from 20–30% with proper sampling.
- Analysis: responses **coded** into numbers; software like **SPSS** and **Stata** for statistics.
- **Online surveys** (SurveyMonkey) promise ease/cost-effectiveness but raise sampling concerns — the *user*, not the software, must ensure reliability, validity, representativeness.

### Advantages
1. Best for gathering original data on **populations too large** to study otherwise; generalizable.
2. **Quick and economical**; vast amounts of data (online even more so).
3. Strong on **reliability** — same question → same kind of data each time.
4. Less interviewer/observer bias; anonymity makes respondents more candid on sensitive topics.

### Disadvantages
1. Lacks **qualitative depth** (can't qualify answers; one reason for divorce may be both financial *and* emotional).
2. Weak on **validity** — self-reports aren't always honest (shame about divorce).
3. **Sampling problems** with self-selection (surveying a domestic-violence support group → 100% "incidence"); worse online.
4. Can be used to **make claims/push agendas** rather than for science (SUV maker's "90% of families want a larger car").

> **📋 Data Workshop — "Media Usage Patterns":** The average American spends ~11 hrs/day on electronic media (Nielsen 2018). Design + administer your own survey about media usage: (1) decide what to study, (2) select a sample from a target population, (3) write/format the questionnaire, (4) administer it, (5) analyze and present findings. *Prep-Pair-Share:* small groups design the project together and pilot-test. *DIY:* survey 5–8 people; write a 3–4 page essay on the process and preliminary findings; attach the questionnaire.

## 5. Existing Sources

- Almost unlimited data already exists: **archival/historical records** (marriage licenses, building permits), **media** (books, magazines, TV shows, websites).
- Considered **unobtrusive measures** — no intrusion or disturbance of the people studied.
- Approaches:
  - **Social demography** — population size, composition, growth, distribution (data from government agencies; **U.S. Census Bureau** makes data freely available at Census.gov).
  - **"Social archaeology"** — digging through the social environment (the average American throws away 2+ lbs of garbage/day… what could trash reveal?).
  - **Comparative historical research** — relationships among elements of society across regions and time periods (literature, paintings, newspapers, photographs).
  - **Content analysis** — count/identify recurrent themes or specific variables (words, visual elements) in texts, images, media; analyze relationships among them.

### Classic study: Stearns — *Anxious Parents* (2004)
- Used child-rearing manuals, newspapers, journals to study the changing meanings of childhood in the 19th–20th centuries.
- Children went from **self-sufficient mini-adults** (whose labor was needed) to **vulnerable beings** needing protection — from polio, tooth decay, kidnappers, "errant dodgeballs," loss of innocence…
- Rising parental guilt/anxiety → may have predicted today's **"helicopter parent."**

### Content analysis study: Sabrina Strings (2019)
- Analyzed 18th-century newspapers, magazines, visual art, scientific/medical literature on Black women's bodies.
- **Finding:** contemporary ideals of thinness — and the thinness-health link — are **rooted in anti-Blackness**; fat phobia traces to Enlightenment-era beliefs that fatness = racial inferiority/"savagery." Fat phobia is both **racialized and gendered**.

### Advantages
1. Access to information researchers could never collect themselves (U.S. Census); data can be **pooled/shared** across researchers.
2. Access to social worlds in **different time periods** (frontier wives' letters/diaries from the early 1800s — Peavy & Smith 1998).
3. Same data can **replicate prior projects** — test reliability, track changes over time.

### Disadvantages
1. Original authors didn't have your questions in mind (no clear references to frontier women's sex lives in their letters).
2. Content analysis describes messages but **not how they're interpreted** (TV women's roles have lower status — but what effects on viewers requires more research).

## 6. Experimental Methods

- Closest to the classic **scientific method**; used for group power dynamics, racial discrimination, gender socialization — in labs, corporate boardrooms, even street corners.
- Two basic goals: (1) develop **precise tools** to observe/record/measure; (2) **control for all variables except the one under investigation**.
- Setup: randomly assign similar subjects to an **experimental group** (gets treatment) and a **control group** (doesn't). The treatment = **independent variable**; the outcome = **dependent variable**.
- Data analysis tends to be **quantitative** (isolating a variable and measuring its effect).

### Classic study: Thorne (1993) — the pink/blue baby experiment
- Adults played with the *same* baby dressed in pink or blue; subjects inferred gender from clothing color:
  - "Boy" (blue): handled less gently, louder voice — "Aren't you a big, strong boy?"
  - "Girl" (pink): held closer, spoke softly — "What a sweet little girl!"
- **Conclusion:** gender socialization begins in infancy; gender shapes perception and interaction from a very early age.

### Discrimination studies
- Same-qualification applicants differing only by race/gender interview for jobs, apply for mortgages, etc. (Pager 2007; Hanson et al. 2016; Quadlin 2018): **whites and men got better jobs/mortgage rates; women and minorities got inferior or none** — even with identical qualifications.

### Advantages
1. **Manipulate and control** the social environment; minimal outside interference; select exact participants.
2. **Best method for establishing causality** — e.g., detecting the **"motherhood penalty"** (Correll, Benard & Paik 2007: working mothers paid less/fewer promotions; fathers rewarded).
3. **Replicability** — can be repeated to gauge historical/cultural change. ⚠️ But the **"replicability crisis"**: recent attempts to reproduce influential experimental studies (esp. psychology) have failed — due to social-environment change, problematic sampling, ethical constraints.

### Disadvantages
1. Only for research that can be built and measured in a **controlled setting** — labs are artificial; results may not hold in the messy real world. Fix: **field experiments** (e.g., class-size efficacy — Baldassarri & Abascal 2017).
2. Usually relies on some level of **deception** → strict ethical guidelines, **post-participation debriefing** (explaining the experiment and working through the impact of deception). Famous intense cases: **Milgram's obedience studies; Stanford Prison Experiment.**

## 7. Social Network Analysis (SNA)

- A tool for **measuring and visualizing the structure of social relationships** between people.
- Method: questionnaires ask respondents who they look to for information, advice, support → build network diagrams.
- Used for adolescent risk behaviors, information diffusion, corporate behavior (Abrutyn & Mueller 2014; Pedulla & Pager 2019).

### Reading a network diagram (Figure 2.2: sixth-grade friendships)
- Dots = students (red girls, blue boys); **arrows** = social ties; more arrows in = more **"central"/popular**.
- **Bridges** = students linking groups/cliques (information flows through them).
- In the example: girls' friends are almost exclusively girls, boys' likewise; only girls identified boys as friends (**ties lack reciprocity** — arrows flow one way); everyone connected in ~3 steps (degrees of separation).

### History
- **Georg Simmel** (early 20th c.): social ties and how **group size** affects relationships among members — SNA predates the internet.
- **Stanley Milgram (late 1960s):** the "small world" phenomenon — everyone is connected by an average of **5½–6 steps** → "six degrees of separation" (Travers & Milgram 1969).
- Computer programs made SNA a large, diverse interdisciplinary field; today it maps **Instagram, Facebook, Twitter, Snapchat, LinkedIn** networks — e.g., studying terrorist communication patterns (Everton 2012), Occupy Wall Street organizing via Twitter (Tremayne 2014), and the spread of **Covid-19** (Weeden & Cornwell 2020).

### Advantages
1. Traces the route of anything — idea, disease, rumor, trend — through groups/communities; useful for **epidemiologists, political sociologists, market researchers**.
2. Often uses **"big data"** (data sets too large for typical programs) — enables corporations to spot trends and target audiences; creates new research fields.

### Disadvantages
1. Often quantitative → can **gloss over details and diversity** of social actors' experiences; combine with qualitative methods (**mixed methods**) — e.g., **Mario Small**, *Unanticipated Gains* (2019), charts child-care centers' effects on NYC families' networks.
2. **Big data is expensive**, and large data sets often come from sources assembled for other purposes (advertising) or **pose privacy threats**.

## 8. In the Future: Emerging Methods

### Action research — combining research and social change
- Combines social science research with **community problem solving and social change**; questions sociology's beliefs about ethics, bias, and the researcher's role.
- Research **with** people, not on people — subjects are active, collaborative, equal participants ("scholarship of engagement," Rajaram 2007).
- *Example:* Chicago's **COFI** — "Why Isn't Johnny in Preschool?" Community members trained in interviewing talked to 5,000+ parents about preschool barriers → designed outreach campaigns → **preschool attendance increased** in targeted neighborhoods (COFI 2009).

### "Netnography" (cyberethnography)
- Participant observation methods applied to **online communities** (gaming environments, social media, web-based locations); adapts real-life ethnography rather than inventing new methods.
- *Examples:* infertility (Lee 2017), prescription drug use (Del Fresno & Peláez 2014); **Brigitte Borm (2017)** studied Airbnb hosts' online discussions and how their definition of "home" changed after renting to strangers.

### GIS (geographic information systems)
- Computer software attaching social science data to **geographic locations** — not just *that* 13% of a city lives in poverty, but *where*.
- Overlay maps of illness on maps of pollutant exposure; studying diseases, matching populations with services, migration (Sianko & Small 2017); widely used to understand **Covid-19** (Franch-Pardo et al. 2020; Kuchler et al. 2021 — Facebook data showing areas with ties to early hotspots had more cases).
- *Example:* **Oakley & Burchfield (2009)** mapped Chicago public-housing residents before/after demolition-relocation: relocated residents didn't necessarily land in "better" neighborhoods — "The prospects of escaping high-poverty neighborhoods through relocation are very slim" (p. 606).

## 9. Issues in Sociological Research

### A. Nonacademic uses of research methods
- **U.S. Census Bureau:** surveys the total population every 10 years since **1790**; guides government decisions (schools, hospitals, stoplights).
- **Polls:** Gallup, Zogby, Roper — polls don't just reflect public opinion, they can **shape it**; learn who commissioned a poll and whether they have an agenda.
- **Business:** ethnographers study organizational culture; experiments/team games reveal how task-oriented networks form, leaders emerge, cooperation develops.
  - **Shelley Correll** identified the "motherhood penalty" experimentally → now designs **diversity, equity, and inclusion** interventions (2017).
- **Market research:** the most common nonacademic use — a multibillion-dollar industry. Cookie clicks, pop-up "yes" clicks, *Dancing with the Stars* votes = data. ⚠️ Few market studies meet rigorous scientific standards; the bottom line is selling products.

### B. Values, objectivity, and reactivity
**Values:**
- **Max Weber (1925/1946)** — "Science as a Vocation": coined **"value-free" sociology** — separate facts from personal values.
- Challenges: Marxists combine research with **praxis** (social action); symbolic interactionists like **David Matza (1969)** argue the very intention to change the world prevents understanding it.
- Debate: **basic research** (knowledge for its own sake) vs. **applied research** (knowledge put into action).
- **Bias** can infiltrate every stage — from project selection to sample, question wording, analysis, write-up.
  - *Controversial example:* **Mark Regnerus's New Family Structures Survey (2012)** — found "suboptimal" outcomes for children of gay/lesbian parents; critics cited: funding by conservative/religious foundations, unqualified reviewers, lumping all same-sex relationships together, including kids who never lived with a gay parent, not separating family structure from stability, and publication timed for Supreme Court "friend of the court" briefs. Raises the question of how religious values can shape research and policy.

**Objectivity:**
- Ideal from **Comte** on: study society rationally and objectively; facts should speak for themselves.
- But "facts" are fallible — racist, sexist, ethnocentric perspectives long passed for "truth" (scientific reality = experience of white European males).
  - *Example:* women's heart problems went undiagnosed because medical research used mostly male subjects — women's symptoms differ (Criado-Perez 2019).
- Subjectivity is unavoidable — and some (autoethnographers, postmodern thinkers, chaos theory/fuzzy logic) question whether objective reality exists at all.

**Reactivity (the Hawthorne effect):**
- **Elton Mayo (1949),** Western Electric's Hawthorne plant, Chicago (1927–1932): every change to lighting, rest breaks, even pay → productivity *rose*; returning to original conditions → productivity rose *again*.
- Conclusion: the manipulated variables weren't the cause — **the effect of being studied** was. Subjects respond to researchers' interest and may "give them what they want."

### C. Research ethics
- Social research risks are subtle (vs. drug trials): researchers often don't fully explain projects; ethnographers operate on levels of secrecy; **covert research** makes all interactions inauthentic.
- Ethics codes set out **principles**, not strict rules: seek **informed consent**; protect subjects from **harm**.
- Biggest harm = **breaching confidentiality**. Subjects are entitled to **"rights of biographical anonymity"** — crucial for vulnerable populations (children, undocumented immigrants, psychiatric patients) and controversial topics.
  - *Pseudonym failures:* **Lynd & Lynd's "Middletown"** (1929/1959, 1937) was revealed to be **Muncie, Indiana** — bad for its reputation.
  - **Carolyn Ellis (1995, 2007):** villagers in her fishing-village field site were upset by her book's depiction of them; felt betrayed; refused to speak to her again — she was shut out of a social world she'd been part of.
  - **Alice Goffman — *On the Run* (2014):** ethnography of young Black men in Philadelphia (6 years); acclaimed but controversial — politics of representation (can a privileged white woman speak for marginalized Black men?); anonymous 60-page critique alleged inconsistencies (Goffman: results of protecting sources' anonymity).

### 🌍 Global Perspective: The Nuremberg Code & Tuskegee
- **Nuremberg Code** (post-WWII tribunals; Nazi doctors tried for experiments on concentration camp inmates; 16 of 23 convicted): guidelines from doctors **Andrew Ivy & Leo Alexander** — research only for "fruitful results for the good of society," protect subjects from "all unnecessary physical and mental suffering and injury," and **informed, noncoerced consent** only.
- Meanwhile, the U.S. ran its own atrocity: the **Tuskegee Syphilis Study** (1932–1972): 399 poor Black men in Alabama left untreated for syphilis to observe the disease's natural progression — even after penicillin became the standard cure (1947). Exposed 1972; **President Bill Clinton's official apology in 1997**.
- Lesson: all scientific research — social and medical — must adhere to ethical standards.
- **American Sociological Association (ASA) Code of Ethics:** avoid bias, follow professional standards, protect respondents.
- **IRBs (institutional review boards):** university groups reviewing research proposals; can require changes or stop projects; funding may be revoked. ⚠️ Controversial: boards are often all medical/physical scientists — can they judge social research? Some argue for **discipline-specific** review (sociologists judging sociologists).

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Deductive = top-down** (theory → hypothesis → data); **Inductive = bottom-up** (data → theory).
- **Correlation ≠ causation:** watch for the **intervening variable** (spurious correlation).
- **Reliability = consistency** (same answer each time); **Validity = accuracy** (measuring what you think you measure).
- **Hawthorne effect = reactivity:** people change because they're being watched.
- Six methods to remember: **E**thnography, **I**nterviews, **S**urveys, **E**xisting sources, **E**xperiments, **S**ocial network analysis ("EIESES").
- Experiments = best for **causality**; ethnography = best for **depth/meaning**; surveys = best for **large populations**; existing sources = best for **past/unobtainable data**.
