# Science (as it relates to sociology)
## The Core Definition

**Science = the organization of objectively verifiable sense experience.**

1. **Data is empirical and objective.**
   - Science uses **specific methods** of acquiring data (systematic observation, measurement, experiments).
   - Findings must be verifiable by others — not just one person's experience or opinion.
2. **Data is organized into general laws of social action = theory.**
   - Raw observations are not science; they must be organized into **general laws/principles** that explain and predict.
   - In sociology, these laws of social action are called **theories**.

> In other words, science is **objective reality** — describing the world as it verifiably is, not as we believe or wish it to be.

---

## Science as a Process

- **Science is not a fixed body of facts — it is the process of renewing theories.**
- Existing theories are constantly tested against new empirical data; when the data no longer fits, theories are revised or replaced.
- This is what makes sociology a **science**: like the natural sciences, it subjects claims about the social world to systematic, empirical verification.

---

## Related Concepts

- **Empirical data** — information gathered through direct observation/experience (the foundation of scientific knowledge).
- **Objectivity** — conclusions rest on verifiable evidence, not personal bias or common sense.
- **Practical vs. scientific knowledge (Schutz 1962)** — everyday actors rely on "recipe" knowledge that works day-to-day but is not coherent, clear, or consistent; the **social analyst** must "place in question everything that seems unquestionable" and *verify* what the everyday actor accepts as truth (see [[Chapter 1]]).
- **Positivism (Comte)** — sense perceptions are the only valid source of knowledge; the model for sociology's claim to be scientific.
- **The social sciences** — disciplines that use the scientific method to examine the social world (anthropology, psychology, economics, political science, history, geography, communication).

---

## Theory

**Definition:** General laws of social action — **abstract propositions that explain the social world and make predictions about the future** (see [[Chapter 1]]). We use theory to predict behaviors.

- **Theory is the product of science.** The core definition of science states that empirical data is *organized into general laws of social action* — in sociology, those laws are **theories**. Science without theory is just scattered observations; theory without data is speculation.
- **The theory–data loop:** theories are constantly tested against new empirical data — when the data no longer fits, the theory is revised or replaced. This is what it means that *science is the process of renewing theories*.
- **Paradigm vs. theory:** a **paradigm** is a broader set of assumptions, theories, and perspectives that forms a whole way of understanding social reality; individual theories exist *within* paradigms.

### Levels of theory

- **Macro theory** — explains large-scale social structures (functionalism, conflict theory, Weberian theory).
- **Micro theory** — explains face-to-face, small-group interaction (symbolic interactionism).
- **Midrange theory (Merton)** — a *style of theorizing* that strikes a balance between micro and macro; integrates research questions + **empirical data** into smaller-scale theories that build cumulatively.

> **Key point:** A claim only counts as *scientific* theory if it is testable against empirical data — this is what separates science from opinion, belief, or common sense.
