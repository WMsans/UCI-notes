# Chapter 10: Social Institutions: Politics, Education, and Religion

## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Social institutions** | **Systems and structures within society that shape the activities of groups and individuals** in specific areas of social life. They function at the macro level to shape our everyday interactions — but at the micro level those same everyday interactions construct the institutions. |
| **Politics** | The **methods and tactics intended to influence government policy, policy-related attitudes, and activities** — managing a political entity and the administration/control of its internal and external affairs (from Greek *politikos*, "of or relating to citizens"). |
| **Government** | The **formal, organized agency that exercises power and control** in modern society, especially through the **creation and enforcement of laws**. |
| **Power** | (Weber) The **ability to control the actions of others** — to get others to do one's bidding. |
| **Authority** | The **legitimate right to wield power** — the legitimate, noncoercive exercise of power (Weber's subtypes: see [[Chapter 5]]). |
| **Authoritarianism** | A system of government **by and for a small number of elites that does not include representation of ordinary citizens**; citizens have no say in who rules, what laws are made, or how they are enforced. A **dictatorship** is one form. |
| **Totalitarianism** | The **most extreme and modern version of authoritarianism** — the government seeks to control **every aspect, public and private, of citizens' lives**, using surveillance, mass communication, modern weapons, and propaganda (Arendt 1958). Ex: North Korea under the Kims. |
| **Monarchy** | A government **ruled by a king or queen, with succession kept within the family**; monarchs are not popularly elected. **Absolute monarchs** have complete authority (Saudi Arabia, Brunei); **constitutional monarchs** have powers defined and limited by a political charter (Japan, Great Britain, Sweden). |
| **Democracy** | A political system in which **all citizens have the right to participate** — educated citizens elect officials to represent their interests; a philosophy emphasizing majority rule, basic human rights, civil liberties, freedom, and equality. |
| **Disenfranchised** | **Stripped of voting rights, either temporarily or permanently** — e.g., felons in prison, on parole, or (in some states) for life; >5 million Americans barred from voting, including 1 in 16 African Americans of voting age. |
| **Gerrymandering** | **Redrawing the boundary lines of state voting districts to advantage one political party over another** — packing opponents into few districts while spreading your own supporters across many. |
| **Power elite** | (C. Wright Mills) A **relatively small group of people in the top ranks of the economic, political, and military institutions** who make many of the important decisions in American society — insular, self-perpetuating, overwhelmingly white and male. |
| **Pluralist model** | A system of political power in which **a wide variety of individuals and groups have equal access to resources and the mechanisms of power**, kept in check by laws, policies, and the courts (Dahl 1961). |
| **Special interest groups** | Organizations formed expressly to **raise and spend money to influence elected officials and/or public opinion** — corporate organizations, lobbies, PACs, 527 groups (unlimited "soft money"). |
| **PACs** | **Political action committees** — tax-exempt organizations that raise money to support the interests of a select group or organization or to campaign for/against candidates and legislation. |
| **Super PAC** | Created by **Citizens United v. FEC (2010)**, which called unlimited corporate/union campaign spending a First Amendment right; Super PACs can accept unlimited contributions (issue ads) as long as they don't coordinate with the candidate. |
| **Opinion leaders** | **High-profile individuals whose interpretation of events influences the public** (Katz and Lazarsfeld 1955) — we let them shape what we believe. |
| **Simulacrum** | (Baudrillard) An **image or media representation that does not reflect reality in any meaningful way but is treated as real** — "the image has come to replace the real"; the simulation becomes as good as the original. |
| **Education** | The process by which a society **transmits its academic knowledge, cultural values, and societal expectations** to its members so they can function effectively; can be formal or informal. |
| **Tracking** | The placement of students in educational **"tracks" or programs of study (college prep, gifted, remedial, vocational)** that determine the types of classes they take — locking students out of AP courses, colleges, majors, and careers by age 16. |
| **Hidden curriculum** | **Values or behaviors that students learn indirectly over the course of their schooling** (Jackson 1968) — the "rules, routines, and regulations" that train a submissive, obedient workforce (Bowles and Gintis 1977). |
| **Credential society** | A society that **emphasizes the attainment of degrees and certificates as necessary requirements for the job market and social mobility** (Collins 1979) — as credentials grow, they inflate and are worth less. |
| **Charter schools** | **Public schools run by private entities** (parents' groups, educational corporations) with relative freedom from bureaucratic regulations — a compromise between public and private; risk "cherry-picking" students. |
| **School vouchers** | **Government payments to parents whose children attend failing public schools; the money helps pay private school tuition** — critics say they drain funds from public schools and fund religious education. |
| **Homeschooling** | The **education of children by their parents, at home** — ~1.7 million students (~3% of school-age population); homeschooled students' achievement scores significantly above public school averages (Ray 1997, 2008, 2013). |
| **Unschooling** | A homeschooling alternative that **rejects the standard curriculum in favor of student-driven learning** — children initiate their own direction through freely chosen activities, interests, and real-world experiences. |
| **Early college high schools** | Institutions where students earn **a high school diploma plus two years of college credit** toward a bachelor's degree — aimed at low-income and minority students. |
| **Dual enrollment** | Programs that allow high school students to **simultaneously enroll in college classes, earning credit for both** high school and college. |
| **Community college** | Two-year institution providing general education and facilitating transfer to a four-year university — 5.6 million students, **34% of all undergraduates** (2019). |
| **Religion** | Any **institutionalized system of shared beliefs (held on faith) and rituals (practices based on those beliefs)** that identify a relationship between the **sacred and the profane**. Sociologists don't evaluate truth claims — they study how religions shape and are shaped by society. |
| **Sacred** | The **holy, divine, or supernatural**. |
| **Profane** | The **ordinary, mundane, or everyday**. |
| **Denominations** | **Major subgroups of larger religions** (Protestantism within Christianity; Shia within Islam). |
| **Sects** | **Smaller subgroups** of a religion (Amish, Mennonites). |
| **Cults** | Usually very small, intense, close-knit groups focused on **individual leaders** (David Koresh and the Branch Davidians) or specific issues (Heaven's Gate UFO cult). |
| **Monotheistic** | Religions that **worship a single divine figure** — Judaism, Christianity, Islam. |
| **Liberation theology** | A movement within the Catholic Church to **understand Christianity from the perspective of the poor and oppressed, focused on fighting injustice** — Marxism + Christianity; Archbishop Óscar Romero of El Salvador. |
| **Religiosity** | The **consistent and regular practice of religious beliefs**, gauged by frequency of attendance at worship services and the importance of religious beliefs to the individual. |
| **Extrinsic religiosity** | A person's **public display of commitment** to a religious faith — attending services and related functions. |
| **Intrinsic religiosity** | A person's **inner religious life or personal relationship to the divine**. |
| **Fundamentalism** | The practice of **emphasizing literal interpretation of texts and a "return" to a time of greater religious purity** — the most conservative group within *any* religion (not a religion itself). Grew in response to the social changes of the 1960s–70s. |
| **Evangelical** | Conservative Christians who **emphasize converting others to their faith** — see conversion as a service; ~one-quarter of U.S. adults. |
| **Unchurched** | Those who consider themselves **"spiritual but not religious"** — adopt aspects of various traditions ("cafeteria" strategy: astrology, alternative healing, witchcraft) while affiliated with none. |
| **Secular** | Nonreligious; a secular society **separates church and state and does not endorse any religion**. |
| **Agnostic / atheist** | Agnostics are **skeptical about the existence of a god or gods** (4% of Americans); atheists are **nonbelievers** (3.1%). Only about a quarter of the religiously unaffiliated are atheist or agnostic. |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: The Pledge of Allegiance

- You've said it countless times in school — but it brings together questions about **three social institutions at once: politics, education, and religion**.
- **Written in 1892 as a public relations gimmick** for *Youth's Companion* magazine ahead of a nationwide Columbus Day celebration (Crawford 2015) — intended to help **assimilate southern and eastern European immigrants** whose patriotic loyalty was in question (Petrella 2017).
- **"Under God" was added in 1954** — President Eisenhower signed the change; Senator Joseph McCarthy called it "a clear indication that the United States was committed to ending the threat of 'godless' Communism."
- Since **1943**, the Supreme Court has ruled children **cannot be forced** to recite the pledge (initially on patriotic grounds).
- **2002:** California physician **Michael Newdow** (activist for church–state separation) sued when his daughter's second-grade class recited the pledge; the **Ninth Circuit** ruled "under God" violated the First Amendment. Lawmakers of both parties denounced the ruling — the Supreme Court overturned it **on a technicality** and never addressed the constitutional question.

> **Lesson:** social institutions are a sociological **paradox** — they shape our everyday interactions at the macro level, but our everyday interactions construct them at the micro level. Politics, education, and religion are grouped in one chapter because they **intersect in unexamined ways** (the pledge, school vouchers, sex education debates). Look for the **micro-macro link** throughout.

## 1. What Is Politics? Political Systems and Government

- **Government** = the formal, organized agency exercising power and control through lawmaking and enforcement. **Power** (Weber) = the ability to get others to do one's bidding; **authority** = its legitimate, noncoercive exercise (see [[Chapter 5]] on Weber's authority types).
- Sociologists ask of any government: **Who has power and who does not, what kind of power is exerted, and how far does it extend?**

### Total Power: Authoritarianism and Totalitarianism
- **Authoritarianism** denies ordinary citizens representation and control; power concentrated in elites who control military and economic resources. **Dictatorships** gain power by election, family succession, or military coup — often combined:
  - **Paul Kagame (Rwanda):** de facto control as defense minister (1994–2000); "elected" with **99% of the vote** in 2017 amid irregularities.
  - **Bashar al-Assad (Syria):** succeeded his father in 2000; won a fourth term in 2021 with a reported **95%** — the U.S. called the election illegitimate. Dictatorships can also be party/group-run (the **Taliban**).
- **Totalitarianism** = the most extreme form: the state controls **every aspect, public and private, of citizens' lives** (Arendt 1958). Example: **Kim Jong Un's North Korea** (succeeded Kim Jong Il, 2011) — among the world's worst human rights records, a stagnant isolated economy drained by its nuclear program; **~1,000 people per year risk their lives to defect** to South Korea or China. Propaganda disseminates ideology to shape thoughts, values, and attitudes.

### Monarchies
- **Sovereignty vested in a successive line of rulers within a family** (the Tudors, the Ming Dynasty, the Romanovs); some ruled by "divine right."
- **Absolute monarchs** ≈ dictators (surviving examples: **Saudi Arabia, Brunei**); **constitutional monarchs** are figureheads limited by a charter/parliament (**Japan, Thailand, Great Britain, Sweden**). Most monarchies were weakened or overthrown in the revolutions of the 18th–20th centuries (French 1789; Russian 1917).

### Democracy — With an Asterisk
- Originated in **ancient Greece**; citizens share in directing government, electing officials to represent them. A **philosophy** as much as a system: majority rule, human rights, civil liberties, freedom, equality.
- **Even democracies exclude:** U.S. women couldn't vote until **1920**; the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) gave Black men voting rights on paper, but **poll taxes, literacy tests, and "grandfather clauses"** blocked African Americans for nearly 100 years until the **1965 Voting Rights Act**; **American Indians were excluded from federal elections until 1924**; **D.C. residents couldn't vote for president until 1961**; convicted felons are still disenfranchised in some states even after serving their sentences.

## 2. The American Political System

The colonists created the **first modern democracy** in 1776 — but it's more complicated than "rule by the people." Focus: voting, theories of who governs, interest groups, and the media.

### 🗳️ Voting in the United States
- Turnout: **62%** in 2008 (Obama — highest in forty years; 51% of 18–29-year-olds); steady at **59% (2012)** and **60% (2016)**.
- **2020 — a critical turning point:** Covid-19 + protests over police killings of Black people. States expanded early and mail voting (five states already vote entirely by mail: Colorado, Hawaii, Oregon, Utah, Washington). Republicans opposed the changes, fearing higher turnout would hurt them — **turnout surged to 67%**, the largest jump between elections in U.S. history and the highest since 1900. **Biden beat Trump by over 7 million votes**; Democrats won the House and Senate; **Kamala Harris** became the first female, African American, and Asian American vice president.
- **The "Big Lie":** GOP loyalists embraced the claim that Trump actually won but fraud obscured it — dozens of meritless lawsuits, a propaganda campaign, culminating in the **deadly January 6, 2021 Capitol riot**. Research shows election fraud is **vanishingly rare** and the Justice Department found no evidence of widespread 2020 fraud — yet the Big Lie persisted, fueling state-level **voter suppression bills** (Georgia's new law limited ballot drop boxes and even **banned handing out food and water to people waiting in line**).
- **Who votes (and why others don't):** age, race, gender, sexual orientation, religion, geography, class, and education all matter. In 2020, **74% turnout among those over 65 vs. 57%** among 18–34-year-olds. Top reasons for not voting: "not interested" (18%), "did not like candidates or campaign issues" (15%), "too busy" (13%) — only **4% cited the pandemic**.
- **Obstacles to voting:**
  - **Felon disenfranchisement:** all states except **Maine and Vermont** disenfranchise incarcerated felons; 30 states disenfranchise parolees, 27 probationers, and 11 permanently disenfranchise some who completed their sentences. **More than 5 million Americans — including 1 in 16 African Americans of voting age — are barred from voting** (Uggen, Larson, and Shannon 2020).
  - **Registration:** you can't vote without registering; only **21 states + D.C. offer same-day registration**; elsewhere it can close 30 days before the election.
  - **The Georgia flip:** Senate wins by **Jon Ossoff and Rev. Raphael Warnock** came after a decade of organizing by **Stacey Abrams** and volunteers with her **New Georgia Project** and **Fair Fight** — registering and mobilizing voters to flip a once reliably red state.
  - **Election Day is a Tuesday** — not a weekend or holiday as in other democracies.
  - **Voter ID laws:** justified as combating fraud (which is extremely rare — Brennan Center 2021); **minorities, the elderly, and low-income Americans are disproportionately affected**; Democrats charge they're intended to suppress turnout of groups that vote Democratic.
  - **Gerrymandering:** the party controlling the state redraws districts to entrench itself — outcomes no longer reflect the actual party makeup of residents; boundaries can look absurd. Some states use **independent commissions**.

### 👑 Who Rules America? Two Theories of Power

**The Power Elite (conflict theory)**
- **C. Wright Mills (1956/1970):** power is held by a small, insular, self-perpetuating elite occupying the **top positions in the major economic, political, and military institutions** — their interests may not coincide with those of the people.
- **G. William Domhoff:** the elite overlap into a **network of influence** — same prep schools and Ivy League colleges, marry within the group, same neighborhoods, country clubs, churches, charities, serve on one another's boards.
- **Thomas Dye (2002):** only **5,778 individuals** hold these elite positions — **less than 0.0026% of the U.S. population**, overwhelmingly white and male (Feagin and Ducey 2017).
- **Implications:** debunks the rags-to-riches myth (the average person will never wield real power regardless of work ethic); the U.S. remains controlled by **white upper-class men**; those with the power to create change may act only when it serves their own advantage.

**Pluralism (structural functionalism)**
- **Robert Dahl (1961):** power is distributed among **a variety of organizations** (corporations, parties, professional, ethnic, and religious groups), each assumed to have **equal access**, with checks and balances preventing any one group from dominating.

### 💰 Interest Groups: Money in Politics
- **Special interest groups / advocacy groups:** corporations, lobbies, PACs, and **527 groups** (unlimited "soft money"). **Top lobbying spenders (2020, Table 10.1):** National Association of Realtors **$84.1M**, U.S. Chamber of Commerce **$81.9M**, PhRMA **$25.9M**, American Hospital Association, Blue Cross/Blue Shield, **Facebook $19.7M**, AMA, **Amazon $18.7M**.
- **Money wins elections:** campaign spending correlates positively with success; **incumbency** is the biggest advantage — in 2020, **96% of incumbent representatives and 84% of incumbent senators** won reelection. Billionaire backing helps (Charles Koch, George Soros).
- **PACs** represent select interests: BAMPAC (Black America's), AAPAC (Arab American), NOW PAC (feminist), union and trades PACs, even the NFL's Gridiron PAC (and briefly X-PPAC, the Extraterrestrial Phenomena PAC, folded 2018).
- **Citizens United v. FEC (2010):** unlimited corporate/union independent expenditures are a **First Amendment right** → the era of **Super PACs**, funded primarily by a small group of **ultrawealthy contributors**. In the 2020 cycle, Super PACs spent **more than $2 billion**; conservative groups outspent liberal ones about **3 to 2**.

### 📺 The Media and the Political Process
- The media act as a check on power — the "**Fourth Estate**" (fourth branch of government); the First Amendment guarantees a free press because the framers saw a watchdog press as essential to democracy.
- **Politics and the media shape each other.** Media coverage has driven the civil rights movement, Vietnam, Watergate, 9/11, Hurricane Katrina — and social media powered **Black Lives Matter and MeToo** (see [[Chapter 9]]).
- **The partisan trust gap:** in 2016 about **three-quarters of both Republicans and Democrats** believed the media served as a watchdog; by 2020, **73% of Democrats vs. just 10% of Republicans** trusted the media to report news fairly (Brenan 2020). Trump supporters distrust "mainstream media" in favor of **Breitbart, Newsmax, OAN**.

**Campaigns and Elections: Style over Substance?**
- Media coverage now emphasizes **human interest, personalities, spectacles, and planned events** over policy background (Kellner 2005; J. Thompson 2012). **Pundits** (commentary + news: Maddow, Hannity, Maher, Hayes, Zakaria...) and **opinion leaders** (Katz and Lazarsfeld 1955 — e.g., Dan Rather) mediate what we believe.
- **The 1960 Kennedy–Nixon debates** — first televised: Kennedy prepared for the cameras (makeup, clothes, demeanor) and looked fresh and energetic; Nixon relied on his command of issues and looked sweaty and tired. **TV audiences thought Kennedy won; radio audiences thought Nixon won.** Image has mattered ever since.
- **Sound bites** condense politics into seconds; **celebrity politicians** know how to work the camera — Ronald Reagan ("the Great Communicator"), Bill Bradley, John Glenn, Al Franken, Jesse Ventura, Arnold Schwarzenegger, Sonny Bono, Clint Eastwood.
- **Donald Trump:** reality-TV celebrity whose "outsider/authentic" image was carefully promoted; mainstream media treated him as entertainment while giving him **free airtime**; "alt-right" outlets promoted him to disaffected white working-class voters — a case study in **style over substance**.

> **📋 Data Workshop — Satirical News Shows** (content analysis): Baudrillard's **simulacrum** — "the image has come to replace the real"; we can no longer distinguish the real from the unreal and no longer need the real. *The Daily Show with Trevor Noah, Last Week Tonight with John Oliver, Full Frontal with Samantha Bee*, SNL's "Weekend Update" mirror real news formats and production values but aren't held to journalistic standards. Studies show satirical news reinforces viewers' preexisting liberal or conservative attitudes as much as serious news does (Knobloch-Westerwick and Lavis 2017). Analyze one episode: how it resembles real news, what stories it covers (real vs. invented), how a viewer knows what's true, underlying messages, influence on young people's politics. *Prep-Pair-Share* or 3-page essay.

**Social Media and Politics**
- **Trump tweeted an average of 18 times a day** — over 25,000 tweets from inauguration to his permanent ban in January 2021, declaring tweets official presidential statements; retweeted racist dog whistles and fringe outlets (OAN, Infowars).
- **Filter bubbles** (Eli Pariser 2012): search engine algorithms deliver customized results based on your history and social circles → fragmentation into separate intellectual streams and political **"echo chambers"** where you only encounter agreeable sources — sowing division and enabling abuse.
- **Russian meddling (2016, 2020):** false accounts and inflammatory posts on Facebook, Twitter, YouTube; targeted ads encouraging Hillary supporters to "text or tweet rather than show up" (voter suppression); religious memes to court churchgoers (Jamieson 2018).
- **Fake news:** fraudulent stories spread by shares and retweets (e.g., "anti-Trump protesters paid by the Democratic Party"); conspiracy theories on 4chan spawned the cult-like **QAnon** (child-trafficking rings, stolen election), linked to the **January 6 insurrection**. Trump then flipped "fake news" to **delegitimize mainstream outlets** — undermining the watchdog function the framers intended.

### 🪧 Patriotism and Protest
- After 9/11, critics of U.S. intelligence were branded "unpatriotic" and silenced — until the 9/11 Commission **confirmed the problems they had raised**. Nationwide anti–Iraq War protests began in 2002.
- **March for Our Lives (2018):** after the Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School shooting (17 killed in Parkland, Florida), student survivors created **NeverAgain**, organized on Facebook and Twitter; the March 24, 2018 demonstrations drew **1.2–2 million people across 880+ demonstrations** — among the largest youth-led protests since the Vietnam War.
- **Black Lives Matter:** emerged 2013 after the killings of Trayvon Martin and Tamir Rice; surged after the murder of **George Floyd** by Minneapolis officer Derek Chauvin — demonstrations nationwide and worldwide. Saying victims' names — **Floyd, Breonna Taylor, Ahmaud Arbery, Daniel Prude, Rayshard Brooks** — became part of the rallying cry; protesters point to a long history of **racial profiling, police brutality, and inequities in policing** (see [[Chapter 8]]).
- **Is criticism unpatriotic?** Protesters argue dissent is the *most* patriotic act; opponents say "my country, right or wrong." Either way, **dissent and its tolerance are crucial elements of an open society** — and constitutionally protected.

### 🔗 Politics: The Micro-Macro Link
- Political institutions shape daily life: **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964)** protects you from job discrimination; the **Department of Education** and your **FAFSA** determine your financial aid and possibly where you attend college.
- But we build these institutions ourselves: voting, petitions, demonstrations bring individual influence to bear on the larger structure — micro-macro connections made every day. Democratic processes also require **an educated polity** → the bridge to education.

## 3. What Is Education?

- **Education** is the central means by which a society transmits **knowledge, values, and expectations** to its members — formal and informal.
- The framers knew an informed public was essential to democracy. U.S. public education is under **state and local control** (federal role grew in the late 20th century). Today: **88% of K–12 students attend public school, 9% private, 3% homeschooled** (Dept. of Education 2018).

### A Brief History of Modern Education
- **Ancient Greece (8th century B.C.E.):** philosophy, mathematics, music, gymnastics. **Middle Ages:** the church educated (monasteries/cathedrals, mainly training priests until ~1200 C.E.); 13th-century lay education = apprenticeship or chivalry; universities taught **law, theology, and medicine** — only for royals, aristocrats, and those tied to church and monarchy.
- **The Enlightenment (18th–19th c.):** Voltaire, Locke, Franklin emphasized **reason, logic, and science over religious tradition** — education's value soared; the founders considered basic education a government responsibility. Schooling became **legally mandatory** (Massachusetts first, **1852**); curricula diversified (academic + vocational). By **1929** schools had spread to every state and territory.
- **Mass education:** before 1900, **less than 2%** of Americans finished high school; by **2019 the graduation rate was 86%** and the dropout rate just **5%** (down from >10% in 2000) — especially improved among Black and Hispanic students.

### Education and the Reproduction of Society
- Beyond transmitting knowledge, school teaches us to **follow rules, respect authority, and become efficient, obedient workers** — and teaches us **our place**: **tracking** ("gifted" vs. remedial/vocational) locks students out of calculus, AP classes, certain colleges, majors, and careers **by age 16** (Useem 1990).

### 📉 Education and Inequality
- Education improves life chances — but **unequally**: 2019 median earnings by attainment — high school **$35,000**, associate's **$40,000**, bachelor's **$55,700**, master's+ **$70,000**; 2020 unemployment — high school **9%**, bachelor's **5.5%**, advanced degree **3.8%**.
- **Unequal access:** decades of **residential segregation + property-tax funding** mean wealthy districts have more resources; even within the same district, **experienced, higher-paid teachers cluster at lower-need schools** — students in high-poverty schools are **doubly disadvantaged** (Mathewson 2020).
- **College gaps widen:** high-income young adults are **nearly 5× more likely** to earn a bachelor's by 24 than the lowest-income group (**62% vs. 13%**); by race (2020, over 25): Asian **61%**, white **41%**, Black **28%**, Hispanic **21%**; by gender, women 25–29 lead men **44% vs. 35%** (see [[Chapter 9]]).
- **Micro-inequities** in the classroom: boys get more teacher attention; fewer Black and Hispanic students in gifted than remedial classrooms; kids with disabilities sidelined — individual experiences produced by **macro-level structural forces**. They teach as powerfully as the official curriculum: **educational attainment is as much about social stratification ([[Chapter 7]]) as individual ability**.

### 🫥 The Hidden Curriculum
- **Hidden curriculum** (Jackson 1968): lessons learned **indirectly** through schooling's structure and routines.
- **Bowles and Gintis, *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1977):** schools train a labor force with the skills, personalities, and attitudes a corporate economy needs — the official curriculum promises mobility, but the hidden curriculum of **"rules, routines, and regulations"** produces a **submissive, obedient workforce** ready to take orders and perform repetitive tasks. Schools look a lot like factories: no control over curriculum, must obey, little intrinsic satisfaction — preparing students to accept the same as workers.
- The hidden curriculum also **reproduces inequality** by defining what is "normal, right, good": textbooks still have gaps and exclusions; even multicultural curricula are undermined when the **staff doesn't mirror the lessons** (mostly white, male, or heterosexual administration) — changing *what* is taught without changing *how* it's taught changes nothing (Christakis 1995; Falconer and Byrnes 2003).

### 📚 Classic Studies of Education (one per perspective)

**Symbolic interactionist — Rosenthal & Jacobson, *Pygmalion in the Classroom* (1968)**
- Administered an IQ test (falsely telling teachers it predicted academic "bloomers"), randomly designated an experimental group, and told teachers those students would develop rapidly. A year later the experimental group's IQ gains were **significantly greater** than the control group's. **Teacher expectations unintentionally shape student performance** — a self-fulfilling prophecy (compare labeling theory, [[Chapter 6]]).
- Critiqued on methodological grounds (IQ tests, small samples), but supported broadly: student labeling is often **arbitrary and biased**, reinforcing class, ethnic, ability, and gender inequalities (Murdock-Perriera and Sedlacek 2018) — meaning **changing classroom interaction could improve outcomes for underprivileged students**.

**Conflict — Jonathan Kozol, *Savage Inequalities* (1991)**
- Ethnography of public schools (D.C., Chicago, San Antonio...): because schools are **funded by local property taxes, children in poor neighborhoods are trapped in poor schools** — reinforcing inequality. High-spending districts have **twice as many art, music, and foreign language teachers, 75% more PE teachers, 50% more nurses, librarians, counselors, and psychologists, 60% more administrators**.
- Wealthy-district parents thought educational inequality was "something dating maybe back to slavery or... official segregation" — not ongoing. Kozol found urban schools lacking basic resources: hundreds of classrooms without teachers in Chicago, thousands of children without classrooms in New Jersey. **A structural functionalist might reply that schools were never meant to provide equal chances.**

**Structural functionalist — Randall Collins, *The Credential Society* (1979)**
- In a **credential society**, degrees certify status and job readiness; as demand grows, credentials **inflate** — students need ever-higher achievement, stalling the promise of mobility. Class inequalities are reproduced and little can be done about it: holding class background constant, **better teachers, facilities, and funding show no effect**.
- Schools exist to reproduce the class structure — providing middle-class credentials to middle-class children; lucrative professions use credential systems to **limit applicants** and keep a large population in low-wage work. Collins's radical fix: **abolish compulsory schooling requirements and make formal credential requirements illegal** — employers couldn't ask about education any more than race or gender.

### 🔮 The Present and Future of Education
- Perceived crisis: low literacy, poor test scores, insufficient funding, crumbling infrastructure, low teacher pay, overcrowding, campus crime. **A Nation at Risk (1983)** called the system so bad that if "an unfriendly foreign power" had imposed it, "we might well have viewed it as an act of war." Four decades later the **achievement gap** persists.
- **Poverty is the biggest predictor of achievement:** **45% of Black students and 44% of Hispanic students** attend high-poverty schools vs. **14% of Asian and 8% of white** students; only ~half of high-poverty high schoolers go to college. **Covid-19 magnified this:** wealthy students moved easily online; high-poverty students lacked devices, broadband, and supervision — falling further behind (Perry, Aronson, and Pescosolido 2021).

**Reform trends:**
- **Common Core (2010):** state-led, voluntary standards to raise achievement (45 states adopted at peak); support fell to ~50% by 2016 amid fights over standardized testing and "federal mandate" perceptions; DeVos disavowed it, yet it persists in ~40 states in some form.
- **Charter schools:** public schools run by private entities, freer from regulation — designed for innovation (IDEA in D.C. teaches construction trades; Nuestro Mundo in Madison is a Spanish immersion school closing the Hispanic test-score gap). **Challenges:** fundraising struggles create disparities; freedom from regulation enables **"cherry-picking"** (denying ESL, special-needs, or behaviorally challenging students to look more successful) (Mommandi and Welner 2018).
- **School vouchers:** public money follows the child to private school (covering 75–90% of tuition); **little evidence they increase achievement** (Usher and Kober 2011); critics say they **drain public schools** and fund religious education (church–state issue); the Supreme Court has ruled them constitutional.
- **Homeschooling & unschooling:** ~1.7 million students (3%); homeschooled students score significantly above public school averages even with uncertified parents and light regulation (Ray 1997, 2008, 2013) — but social development questions remain (underscored by pandemic schooling). **Unschooling** rejects the standard curriculum entirely for student-driven, interest-led learning; adults who were unschooled report high career satisfaction; critics worry about isolation and motivation.
- **Early college high schools & dual enrollment:** diploma + two years of college credit, aimed at low-income and minority students (e.g., Portland Community College's Many Nations Academy for American Indian youth — the group with the lowest graduation rate); participants are significantly **more likely to attend and graduate college**, minorities most of all (Berger et al. 2014). Dual enrollment eases the transition, raises achievement, and shortens time to a degree (Karp 2012; Allen and Dadgar 2012).
- **Community colleges:** **5.6 million students — 34% of all undergraduates** (2019); far more than transfer springboards — vocational/technical training, worker retraining, enrichment; California's system offers 200+ fields. **Community college baccalaureate programs** let students earn bachelor's degrees on campus. **America's College Promise (Obama, 2015)** and similar state/local programs pursue **free community college** — shown to boost first-time enrollment, reduce debt, and increase bachelor's completion; Jill Biden (a community college professor) pushed Biden's proposal to treat free community college as national infrastructure.

> **💼 On the Job — A College Degree: What's It Worth?**
> - **Collins's credential inflation warning:** job skills are learned on the job; college provides a **credential** — and credentials inflate (jobs once requiring high school now demand bachelor's, etc.).
> - **The case for college:** the **wage premium averages ~$30,000/year** (Jaschik 2019) → **>$1 million extra over a lifetime**; 14% return on investment vs. 7% stocks, 3% bonds; the earnings gap keeps widening (Pew 2014b). Mary Daly (SF Fed): strong evidence of a lasting "earnings advantage."
> - **The cracks:** break-even takes **~9 years** for affordable public colleges, **~17 years** for private/for-profit; STEM starting salaries **$62–70K** vs. social sciences/humanities/business **$53–57K** (sociology majors average ~$56,000 — and we know what "average" means!). Skeptics: Nobel laureate James Heckman ("if not college ready... it's not worth it"); Peter Cappelli (many graduates end up in jobs that don't need degrees).
> - **Nonmonetary returns:** critical thinking, media literacy, communication skills; broadened perspective (dorms, study abroad); lifelong networks; college grads are **healthier and more likely to vote and volunteer**.

- **For-profit colleges:** operate to maximize profit; mixed record — access for nontraditional students, but **high debt, dismal graduation rates, weak job placement**. Students at for-profits are **10% of college students but half of student-loan defaults** (Shiro and Reeves 2021); they **target students of color** with predatory recruitment (Bonadies et al. 2018). ITT Technical Institute, Education Corporation of America, and **Trump University** were forced to close; the Pentagon banned GI Bill funds at for-profits. Yet during the pandemic, for-profit enrollment **rose 13%** while public four-year enrollment for 21–24-year-olds fell >20% (Cellini 2020).
- **Online learning:** from correspondence courses to the internet — access for those who can't come to campus, but **notoriously high attrition** and a missing personal dynamic. The pandemic's **Zoom school** exposed everything: teachers retooling lessons, students without tech or internet, parents supervising while working, therapists unable to reach special-needs students — and existing **inequities worsened** (Garcia and Weiss 2020).

### 🔗 Education: The Micro-Macro Link
- Macro-level change (digital technology) shapes your everyday student experience — ebooks, online course materials, video homework. **What you learn and how you learn it are shaped by larger social forces.**
- Education isn't the only institution teaching society's knowledge and values — **religion** is another. The three institutions keep intersecting (vouchers, the pledge).

## 4. What Is Religion?

- **Sociological definition:** any **institutionalized system of shared beliefs (held on faith) and rituals** that identifies a relationship between the **sacred** (holy, divine, supernatural) and the **profane** (ordinary, everyday).
- **Types of religious groups:** **denominations** (major subgroups: Protestantism in Christianity, Shia in Islam), **sects** (smaller: Amish, Mennonites), **cults** (small, intense, leader-focused: Branch Davidians; Heaven's Gate).
- Sociologists **do not evaluate the truth** of beliefs — they study how religions shape and are shaped by institutions, culture, and individual behavior.

### Theoretical Approaches to Religion

**Weber: The Protestant Ethic**
- **Max Weber (1904–05/1930):** Protestants' emphasis on **hard work and frugality** facilitated accumulation of wealth — seen as a sign of **God's favor**. The **"Protestant work ethic"** (Weber's term) contributed to the triumph of **capitalism** in Europe and the U.S. (connects to [[Chapter 7]] on class).

**Structural Functionalism — religion's functions**
1. **Shapes everyday behavior** with morals, values, rules, norms ("Thou shalt not kill"; Buddhist reconciliation of strife; Qur'anic dietary rules) — plus penance/rehabilitation (Catholic confession; Ramadan fasting; Yom Kippur atonement).
2. **Gives meaning to life** — answers "How did we get here?", "What is our purpose?", "Why do bad things happen to good people?" — explaining the inexplicable and making the terrible tolerable.
3. **Provides community** — group activity and identity, cohesive organizations, congregations of like-minded others.

**Conflict Theory — religion, inequality, and change**
- **Religion can subjugate:** the three monotheistic religions all treat women and men unequally (Orthodox Judaism's separation of the sexes; Catholicism's and many Protestant sects' ban on women clergy; traditional Islam's polygyny for men only). Nonsexist religions (Wicca) are marginalized; some sects refuse gay clergy; the LDS Church barred African Americans from temple marriage until **1978** (see [[Chapter 8]], [[Chapter 9]]).
- **Religion can liberate:** abolitionism was entwined with Methodist and Baptist reformers; the civil rights movement **began in southern Protestant churches** led by ministers including **Rev. Martin Luther King Jr.**; **liberation theology** (Marxism + Christianity) fought exploitation in Africa and Latin America — **Archbishop Óscar Romero** of El Salvador was assassinated while saying Mass in 1980 (beatified 2015); Poland's **Solidarność** (Catholic shipyard workers + Pope John Paul II) broke down communist restrictions on religion and unions and became the crucible of democratic change in the Eastern bloc.
- This complexity explains Americans' contradiction: quasi-religious national ideology coexisting with the belief that church and state should be separate.

**Symbolic Interactionism — doing religion**
- Religious meaning is **constructed in interaction**. **Orit Avishai (2008), Orthodox Jewish women:** far from "doormats," they construct ways of "doing religion" that preserve **personal agency and self-determination** within their faith's value system. Similar findings for Muslim and Christian women (Bartkowski and Read 2003; Gallagher 2004; Mahmood 2004): women frame their roles as **individual choices**, their work as central to family and community, their obedience as **triumph over problems** — agency, empowerment, and resistance in everyday interaction (compare Garfinkel's "Agnes" and "doing gender," [[Chapter 9]]; doing the self, [[Chapter 4]]).

### 🇺🇸 Religion in America
- **89% of Americans believe in God; 53% say religion is very important; 36% attend services weekly** (Pew 2015c; Theodorou 2015) — with big demographic differences. **~1 in 4 American adults is religiously unaffiliated** (more than a third of those under 30) — the highest ever recorded. The unaffiliated skew **young, white, male, and western/northeastern**.
- **Composition (Figure 10.1):** Christians ~**70%** of the U.S.; non-Christian religions <6% (Jewish 1.9%, Muslim 0.9%, Buddhist 0.7%, Hindu 0.7%); unaffiliated ~**23%** (agnostic 4%, atheist 3.1%). **Worldwide:** Christian 31%, Muslim 24% (**Islam has overtaken Christianity as the fastest-growing religion**), unaffiliated "nones" 16%, Hindu 15%.

### Religious Trends
- **The shrinking white Christian population:** less than half of Americans now identify as white and Christian — down from **8 in 10 in 1976**. Protestant and Catholic populations are shrinking while becoming **more diverse**: **more than a third of U.S. Catholics are Hispanic**.
- **Fundamentalism:** not a religion but a **traditionalist interpretive approach** applicable to any — literal readings of the Bible, Torah, or Qur'an and a return to founding principles. Gained popularity in response to the social changes of the 1960s–70s by offering **certainty in the face of change**. Between 1990 and 2001, self-described "fundamentalist Christians" **tripled** and "evangelical Christians" **more than quadrupled** (Kosmin, Mayer, and Keysar 2001); ~**one-quarter of U.S. adults are evangelical**. Evangelicals emphasize conversion, embrace media technologies, and are **less likely to support church–state separation**.
- **Unchurched spirituality:** the unaffiliated have **tripled since the 1990s**, but unaffiliated ≠ unbeliever — only a quarter are atheist/agnostic, and ~16% still consider themselves religious. Many seek a **"spiritual but not religious"** path via a **"cafeteria" strategy**: mixing astrology, alternative healing, twelve-step programs, even witchcraft (Fuller 2002) — rejecting organized religion's sexism, racism, homophobia, antisemitism, and conformity.

> **💞 In Relationships — Can a Relationship with God Improve Your GPA?**
> - Science can't prove or disprove God — but it can study religion's **role in everyday life**. A relationship with God resembles face-to-face interaction: talk, listen, receive comfort and support like from family and friends (Sharp 2010).
> - College students split into three worldviews: **32% religious, 32% spiritual, 28% secular** (Kosmin and Keysar 2013). Religious behavior (attendance) declines in college, but **spirituality increases**: 43% of first-years say integrating spirituality is "essential" or "very important" (Stolzenberg et al. 2020).
> - **UCLA longitudinal study** (14,500+ students, 136 colleges; Astin, Astin, and Lindholm 2010) measured **five qualities of spirituality**: **equanimity** (calm centeredness), **spiritual quest** (search for answers to big questions), **ethic of caring**, **charitable involvement**, and an **ecumenical worldview**.
> - Spirituality **positively affects** academic performance, psychological well-being, leadership, and college satisfaction. Study abroad, interdisciplinary coursework, and service learning fuel growth; students doing **"inner work"** (self-reflection, contemplation, prayer, meditation) show the greatest growth — which even **improves GPA**. So yes: a relationship with God, if it leads to spiritual growth, really can improve your GPA.

> **📋 Data Workshop — Measures of Religiosity** (interview research): 53% of Americans say religion is very important vs. 26% of Italians, 21% of Germans and Spaniards, 14% of the French (Theodorou 2015) — but people **say** more than they **do** (Holifield 2015; Vedantam 2010). Design a questionnaire (closed- and open-ended questions on affiliation, attendance, prayer, importance, comfort, influence on decisions), interview a convenience sample, and analyze patterns. *Prep-Pair-Share* or a 2–3-page essay with notes/transcripts attached.

### ⛪ A Secular Society?
- Church–state separation is a founding principle — **violated in practice constantly**: "In God We Trust" on the dollar; the 2015 Ten Commandments monument controversy at the Oklahoma capitol; federal money to "faith-based" charities; vouchers funding parochial schools.
- **The presidency:** no religious requirement in the Constitution — yet every president has been a man and a Christian. **John F. Kennedy** (Catholic) faced fears the pope would rule America in 1960; **Barack Obama** was dogged by false Muslim rumors both terms.
- **The Christian calendar rules everyday life:** schools, banks, and government close on Christmas (unobserved by >15% of Americans); universities organize breaks around Christmas and Easter but rarely give days off for **Yom Kippur, Passover, or Eid al-Fitr** — Jewish and Muslim students must make special arrangements, use vacation time, or forfeit exams. **Is every person equally free to practice their faith in America?**

### 🔗 Religion: The Micro-Macro Link
- Religion is a source of **conflict and misunderstanding** and a **wellspring of comfort and meaning**. Whatever the tradition, sociologists see shared patterns: meaning, rules to live by, explanations of the world, belonging, group identity.
- Religious beliefs and prejudices can **inflame or resolve global conflicts** and shape national politics — while religion is integral to many Americans' everyday lives. "In God We Trust" is the motto of the nation *and* of many of its people.

## Closing Comments

- Politics, education, and religion are **linked social institutions**: voucher policy (politics) affects students and neighborhoods (education) and benefits parochial schools (religion); the pledge binds all three; democratic citizenship requires educated voters whose values often come from religion.
- These institutions shape your life in ways you may not have realized — but you also shape them: **your vote changes the political landscape; your role as a student influences your college's culture; your membership in a congregation affects fellow worshippers.**
- Institutions affect individuals, and individuals can influence institutions — **this is the essence of the sociological imagination and the micro-macro link** (see [[Chapter 1]]).

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Social institutions = the micro-macro paradox:** they shape your everyday life (macro), but your everyday interactions construct them (micro). Chapter trio (politics + education + religion) chosen because they **intersect**: the pledge (PR stunt → assimilation tool → "under God" in 1954 → church–state lawsuits), vouchers, sex ed.
- **Government types:** authoritarianism (no citizen representation; Kagame 99%, Assad 95%) → totalitarianism (controls *everything*; Kim Jong Un, ~1,000 defectors/year) vs. monarchies (absolute: Saudi Arabia/Brunei; constitutional: figureheads) vs. democracy (Greece; ideal vs. reality — women 1920, Voting Rights Act 1965, Native Americans 1924, D.C. 1961).
- **2020 election:** 67% turnout — biggest jump ever, highest since 1900; Biden +7M votes; Harris first woman/Black/Asian American VP; the **"Big Lie"** → Jan. 6 insurrection despite zero evidence of widespread fraud; backlash = suppression laws (Georgia banned water for voters in line).

- **Who rules?** Conflict: **power elite** (Mills; Domhoff's network; Dye's 5,778 people = 0.0026% — debunks rags-to-riches). Functionalism: **pluralism** (Dahl — many groups, checks and balances). In practice: money — top lobbying spenders (Realtors $84M, Chamber of Commerce $82M); incumbents win 96%/84%; **Citizens United (2010)** → Super PACs ($2B in 2020, 3:2 conservative edge).
- **Media = the "Fourth Estate":** watchdog role; 1960 Kennedy–Nixon debate (TV vs. radio!); sound bites and celebrity politicians (Reagan → Trump); partisan trust gap (**73% of Democrats vs. 10% of Republicans** trust the media); **filter bubbles** (Pariser) → echo chambers → Russian meddling + fake news + QAnon; Trump flipped "fake news" against the press.
- **Protest is patriotic:** 9/11 criticism vindicated by the 9/11 Commission; **March for Our Lives** (NeverAgain, 1.2–2M, largest youth protests since Vietnam); **Black Lives Matter** (Floyd, Taylor, Arbery...). Dissent is constitutionally protected and essential to an open society.
- **Education reproduces society:** tracking locks in futures by 16; earnings rise with degrees ($35K → $70K) but access doesn't (62% vs. 13% BA by income; 41% white vs. 28% Black vs. 21% Hispanic); micro-inequities = macro inequality felt in the classroom.
- **The hidden curriculum** (Jackson; Bowles & Gintis): "rules, routines, and regulations" train obedient workers — schools as factories; also teaches who is "normal."
- **Three classic studies, three perspectives:** interactionist — **Pygmalion in the Classroom** (teacher expectations → IQ gains; labeling); conflict — **Kozol, Savage Inequalities** (property-tax funding = separate and unequal); functionalist — **Collins, The Credential Society** (credential inflation; radical fix: outlaw credential requirements).
- **Education's future:** A Nation at Risk (1983); poverty predicts achievement (45% of Black students in high-poverty schools; Covid worsened gaps); Common Core, charters (cherry-picking risk), vouchers (no achievement gains), homeschooling (3%, above-average scores) & unschooling, early college/dual enrollment, community college (34% of undergrads; free-college movement), for-profits (10% of students, **half of loan defaults**, predatory toward students of color), online learning (high attrition; Zoom inequality).
- **Is college worth it?** Yes: ~$30K/year wage premium, >$1M/lifetime, 14% ROI (vs. 7% stocks). But: 9–17 years to break even, STEM vs. humanities gaps, credential inflation (Collins). Nonmonetary: critical thinking, networks, health, voting, volunteering.
- **Religion = beliefs + rituals linking sacred and profane** (sociologists don't judge truth). Weber: **Protestant ethic → capitalism**. Functionalist: morals, meaning, community (+ penance: confession, Ramadan, Yom Kippur). Conflict: monotheisms are sexist/anti-LGBTQ/racist (LDS ban until **1978**) — yet religion also **liberates** (abolition, civil rights, liberation theology/Romero, Solidarność). Interactionist: **Avishai** — religious women construct agency; "doing religion."
- **U.S. religiosity:** 89% believe in God, 53% "very important," 36% weekly attendance; **~1 in 4 unaffiliated** (young, white, male, West/Northeast); 70% Christian (a third of Catholics are Hispanic); Islam is the world's fastest-growing religion; "white Christian" America is below 50% (was 8 in 10 in 1976).

College splits 32% religious / 32% spiritual / 28% secular.

Not quite:** "In God We Trust," Ten Commandments monuments, faith-based federal money, all-Christian presidents, Christmas-centric calendars (but no Yom Kippur or Eid off). **The takeaway of the whole chapter:** institutions shape you — but you vote, study, and worship *back*. Micro-macro, both directions.
