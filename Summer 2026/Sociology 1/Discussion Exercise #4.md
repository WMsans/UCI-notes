# Groups: Alienation, In/Out-Groups, and Groupthink

In short, alienation comes from feeling disconnected from the social world around us. Durkheim explained this through what he called anomie. He argued that group norms place limits on our desires. When those norms weaken, we lose the anchor that connects us to other people and to a sense of purpose. As a result, rapid social change such as the industrial revolution in his time could break down those bonds and leave people feeling lost. On the other hand, Putnam makes a related but what I consider more concrete argument in Bowling Alone, which states that Americans have become more alienated because they no longer join the kinds of everyday groups that used to create community, like bowling leagues, PTAs, and civic clubs. I personally agree more with Putnam. since his argument is easier to see in real life: people today have hundreds of online "friends" yet fewer people they actually eat dinner with or can call for help. The internet can create community, yet it often feels broader and shallower than the offline forms of belonging Putnam describes, and that shallowness is exactly what makes alienation feel so common.

In-groups and out-groups help explain why belonging feels good and why it can also go wrong. An in-group is a group we identify with, while an out-group is a group we see as different. The contrast is that in-groups give us trust and shared identity, but out-groups create opposed feeling including stereotyping and prejudice. Depending on the group we identify, the same behavior would result in completely different feelings in our mind. For instance, in a game jam (a hackathon, or competition for video game development), our team was the in-group and the other teams were the out-group. We wanted to win or at least not embarrass ourselves in front of "them", which made us feel united. However, it also made us care a lot about looking like a cohesive team. As a result, that pressure to appear united to outsiders end up caused the avoidance of arguing about the game design, missing the chance of polishing the concept.

The groupthink example would be how my team in that game jam polishes the game concept. In the beginning of the event, we decided as a team to make a game that everyone on the team would enjoy playing. At a glance, it may sounds like a inclusive decision, but it was indeed a groupthink trap. Our gaming preferences and expeirence were very different, scattered across metroidvania, roguelike, factory simulation, and top-down puzzle games. Groupthink happens when a highly cohesive group cares so much about reaching agreement that it suppresses doubts and stops thinking critically. In our case, everyone probably had some doubt that a single game could satisfy all those tastes and still be finished in the tight time strain of only few dozens of hours, but nobody wanted to be the person who killed the idea or slowed us down. During the discussion and later the whole development process, we kept nodding along since disagreeing felt like breaking the team's unity, especially with other teams watching. So we moved forward with an overambitious, unfocused concept, ending with a product was not the best we could have made. The groupthink caused unanimity to overrode the critical evaluation that is crucial to the game development, or any creative process.
