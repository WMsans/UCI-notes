# Chapter 9: Constructing Gender and Sexuality
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sex** | An individual's membership in **one of two biological categories—male or female** — based on chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive organs. |
| **Primary sex characteristics** | Biological factors—**chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive organs**—that distinguish males from females. |
| **Secondary sex characteristics** | Physical differences between males and females—**facial and body hair, musculature, bone structure**—that are **unrelated to reproduction** (many appear at puberty). |
| **Intersex** | Used to describe a person whose **chromosomes or sex characteristics are neither exclusively male nor exclusively female** — about 17 babies per 1,000 are born intersex. |
| **Gender** | The **physical, behavioral, and personality traits that a group considers normal, natural, right, and good** for its male and female members — what a society deems appropriately "masculine" or "feminine." |
| **Essentialists** | Those who believe gender roles have a **genetic or biological origin and therefore cannot be changed** — gender as an unambiguous, two-category system (the **gender binary**). Found in medicine, theology, biology. |
| **Gender binary** | A system of classification with **only two distinct, opposite, and separate gender categories**. |
| **Constructionists** | Those who believe that **notions of gender are socially determined**, such that a binary system is just one possibility among many; meaning of masculinity/femininity differs across societies and historical periods. |
| **Gender identity** | An individual's **self-definition or sense of gender** — our deeply held sense of ourselves as male, female, or some other gender; may or may not match the sex assigned at birth. |
| **Cisgender** | Term used when **gender identity and/or expression aligns with the sex assigned at birth** (*cis* = "same"). |
| **Transgender** | Term used when **gender identity and/or expression differs from the sex assigned at birth**; an umbrella term that can include other gender-nonconforming persons. Trans identity is **not dependent on medical procedures**. |
| **Gender expression** | An individual's **behavioral manifestations of gender** — clothing, grooming, behavior, body language, gestures, even names. |
| **Gender nonconforming** | Term used when **gender identity and/or expression differs from societal expectations about gender roles**. |
| **Nonbinary** | Individuals who **do not identify as exclusively men or women** — or identify as both, somewhere in between, or outside such categories altogether; not all nonbinary people consider themselves transgender. |
| **Sexuality** | The character or quality of being sexual — sexual behavior, desires, and fantasies. |
| **Sexual orientation / identity** | The **inclination to feel sexual desire toward people of a particular gender**. |
| **Heterosexuality** | Sexual attraction toward **members of the other gender** — the normative, dominant category ("straight"). |
| **Homosexuality** | Sexual attraction toward **members of one's own gender** (~4% of adults identify as gay or lesbian). |
| **Bisexuality** | Sexual attraction toward **members of both genders** (~3% of men, 4% of women). |
| **Asexuality** | The **lack of sexual attraction of any kind**; no interest in or desire for sex (~1% of adults). |
| **Queer theory** | Social theory about gender and sexual identity that **emphasizes the importance of difference and rejects ideas of innate identities or restrictive categories** (Butler 1993; Sedgwick 1993). |
| **LGBTQ** | Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer; extended forms: **LGBTQIA** (+ intersex, asexual — "A" is **not** for "allies"), sometimes "+" for allies and other identities. |
| **Gender role socialization** | The **lifelong process of learning to be masculine or feminine**, primarily through agents of socialization (families, schools, peers, media). |
| **Heteronormativity** | The belief that **heterosexuality is and should be the norm**. |
| **Social learning** | The process of **learning behaviors and meanings through social interaction**. |
| **Patriarchy** | Literally **"rule of the father"** — a **male-dominated society**; gender inequality invariably takes this form (little evidence a matriarchal society ever existed). |
| **Privilege** | **Unearned advantage accorded to members of dominant social groups** (males, whites, people who are physically able or heterosexual, etc.). |
| **Sexism** | The belief that **one sex, usually male, is superior to the other** — an ideology used to justify gender inequality and reinforce the status quo. |
| **Misogyny** | An ingrained prejudice against women; **dislike, contempt, or hatred of women**. |
| **Misogynoir** | (Moya Bailey 2010, 2021) The **unique form of anti-Black sexism that Black women experience** — sexism intersecting with racism (see [[Chapter 8]]). |
| **Prejudice** | An idea about the characteristics of a group that is **applied to all members and is unlikely to change** regardless of evidence against it — rooted in stereotypes (the "dumb blonde," the "promiscuous gay man"). |
| **Discrimination** | **Unequal treatment of individuals based on their membership in a social group**; usually motivated by prejudice; can happen on an individual or institutional level. |
| **Homophobia** | Fear of or discrimination toward **gay, lesbian, and bisexual people**. |
| **Transphobia** | Fear of or discrimination toward **transgender or other gender-nonconforming people**. |
| **Heterosexism** | Belief in the **superiority of heterosexuality and heterosexuals** — an ideological system analogous to sexism/racism (Herek 1990). |
| **Cisgenderism** | Belief in the **superiority of cisgender persons and identities** (Rothblum 1996). |
| **Coming out** | **Openly declaring one's true identity** to those who might not be aware of it; short for "coming out of the closet" — how LGBTQ persons have felt compelled to keep their identity secret. |
| **Hegemonic masculinity** | A masculine ideal that **promotes independence, aggression, competitiveness, and toughness** and **rejects anything feminine, homosexuality, and alternate gender expression** (Connell and Messerschmidt 2005); the standard against which all men are judged — at the top: upper-middle-class white cisgender heterosexual men. |
| **Toxic masculinity** | The **dysfunctional aspects of hegemonic masculinity** and the risks of pursuing its ideal — harms men too (suppressed emotions, competition, breadwinner pressure); cited as a negative factor in men's mental and physical health. |
| **Rape culture** | A set of beliefs, norms, and values that **normalizes sexual violence against women**. |
| **Microaggressions** | Everyday uses of **subtle verbal and nonverbal communications that convey denigrating or dismissive messages** to members of certain social groups (Sue 2010) — "smile!" to women, misgendering trans persons. |
| **Instrumental role** | (Parsons) The position of the family member who **provides material support** — task-oriented "breadwinner," authority figure. |
| **Expressive role** | (Parsons) The position of the family member who **provides emotional support and nurturing** — stabilizes the personality of the instrumental partner. |
| **Feminization of poverty** | The economic trend showing that **women are more likely than men to live in poverty** — caused by the gendered wage gap, more single mothers than single fathers, and rising child-care costs. |
| **Second shift** | (Hochschild) The **unpaid housework and child care expected of women after they complete their day's paid labor** — cooking, cleaning, laundry, child care, etc. |
| **Feminism** | Belief in the **social, political, and economic equality of the sexes**; also, the social movements organized around that belief — both a theoretical perspective and a social movement. |
| **First wave** | The earliest period of feminist activism — **from the mid-nineteenth century until women won the right to vote in 1920**; the **suffrage movement** (right to vote) was its defining cause. |
| **Suffrage movement** | The movement organized around **gaining voting rights for women**. |
| **Second wave** | Feminist activism of the **1960s and 1970s**, associated with **equal access to education and employment** (Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique*, NOW). |
| **Third wave** | The most recent period of feminist activism, focusing on **diversity, intersectionality, globalization, and the variety of identities women can possess** (since the 1980s–90s). |
| **Men's liberation** | A movement originating in the mid-1970s to **discuss the challenges of masculinity** — freeing men from oppressive gender roles. |
| **Men's rights movement** | An offshoot of men's liberation whose members believe **feminism promotes discrimination against men**; includes the fathers' rights movement; the **incel** movement shares a similar ideology focused on men's "right" to sex. |
| **Pro-feminist men's movement** | An offshoot of men's liberation whose members **support feminism** and believe sexism harms both men and women. |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: #MeToo

- **October 15, 2017:** the hashtag **#MeToo** exploded across social media. The term was coined in **2006 by civil rights activist Tarana Burke** as a plan of action to help survivors of sexual violence heal — she was laying the foundation for a powerful social movement.
- **Earlier that October:** the *New York Times* and *New Yorker* reported that **Harvey Weinstein**, one of the most powerful men in Hollywood, had been accused by multiple women of sexual harassment and assault. **Alyssa Milano** tweeted the phrase at a friend's urging — "If all the women and men who have been sexually harassed, assaulted or abused wrote 'me too' as a status, we might give people a sense of the magnitude of the problem" — and publicly credited **Tarana Burke** the next day.
- **The overlooked credit:** "All too often, the crucial social justice work of Black women goes unnoticed, while white women are seen as leaders of feminist movements."
- **The scale:** within 24 hours #MeToo was **tweeted 825,000 times** and appeared in **more than 12 million Facebook posts**.
- The reckoning spread from entertainment to **politics, academia, Silicon Valley, and beyond** — and down to everyday women and men speaking out. Dramatic evidence of how deeply ingrained sexual harassment and abuse are in our culture, and of the persistence of movements fighting sexism and gender inequality.

> **Lesson:** gender and sexual identities are about **what we do** in addition to **who we are** — pay attention to the processes that construct the meanings of genders and sexualities, and to the real consequences of gender and sexual inequality.

## 1. Sex and Gender

Sociologists differentiate the terms: **"sex" is biological; "gender" is social or cultural.** Gender identity and gender expression may differ from the sex assigned at birth — which raises the question of which set of attributes is most consequential to everyday life.

### What Is Sex?
- **Sex** = membership in one of two biological categories (male/female), based on **chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive organs** (primary sex characteristics) plus secondary sex characteristics (Table 9.1: females — XX, estrogen; males — XY, testosterone; physical differences like facial hair, musculature, bone structure, voice).
- **Intersex:** about **17 babies in 1,000** are born with variant chromosomal makeup and mixed or indeterminate sex characteristics (Fausto-Sterling 2000). In modern Western society, ambiguity is seen as so threatening that most parents quickly seek surgery (female is usually the "most viable and expedient choice").
- **Georgiann Davis (2015):** critiques classifying intersex as a **medical disorder** rather than a **biological variation** — labeling it an "abnormality" is often harmful and has profound life consequences. Many adult intersex people want the freedom to choose for themselves.

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Different Societies, Different Genders
- **Berdaches / "Two-Spirit" People:** described by 19th-century explorers among Native tribes — individuals **neither male nor female but somehow both**. Usually biological males who dressed as women and did "feminine" work (cooking, domestic labor), or biological females who hunted, trapped, and waged war. Male two-spirits documented in **nearly 150 Native cultures**, female two-spirits in almost half that number. Some were assigned the role from birth for reasons of **"demographic necessity"** (Trexler 2002) — a family with all girls might raise the next child as a boy/hunter. They were acknowledged as a **third gender** ("When the spirit people made men and women, they also made berdaches" — Roscoe 2000).
- **Hijras (South Asia):** a modern third-gender group — usually biological males who have all or part of their genitals removed, voluntarily, in their teens or twenties; dress and live as females, referred to as daughter, sister, grandmother, aunt. Validated in the epic Hindu texts (recognized by the deity Rama); their presence at weddings and births of male children is **auspicious**. In **2012**, Pakistan added a **third-gender option to national identity cards** (Frayer 2012).
- **Bacha Posh (Afghanistan):** "a girl who is dressed up and disguised as a boy" (Nordberg 2014). In a patriarchal, gender-segregated society, boy children are strongly preferred — wives who fail to produce a male baby bring shame on the family. A family with several girls and no boys may turn one into a **bacha posh**: raised as a boy, accepted as one, able to leave the house without an escort, go to school, drive, even get a job. Most are turned back into girls at puberty; some remain.
- **Not the same as Western "transgender":** a two-spirit or hijra is always referred to by that term (not "he"/"she"), and a bacha posh is always called bacha posh. **Gender is malleable** — the characteristics we think of as definitive (sex, gender, sexuality) may be viewed differently in other cultures and time periods, which means it should be possible to view them differently here and now.

### What Is Gender?
- **Gender** = the traits a group considers "normal, natural, right, and good" for males and females — our notions of "masculine" and "feminine." No society leaves it completely up to nature.
- **Essentialists:** gender is **immutable and biological** — an unambiguous two-category system (the **gender binary**); chromosomes, hormones, and genitalia determine identity; culture plays little to no role. Common in **medicine, theology, and biology**.
- **Constructionists:** gender is a **social construction** shaped by culture and historical period; the binary isn't the only way to classify gender, and the meaning of masculinity/femininity differs across societies.

### Gender Identity and Expression
- **Gender identity** — our deeply held sense of ourselves as male, female, or some other gender — may or may not correspond to the sex assigned at birth.
  - **Cisgender:** sex and gender identity match (*cis* = "same").
  - **Transgender:** gender identity does not align with the sex assigned at birth. Well-known trans women: **Caitlyn Jenner, Laverne Cox, Chelsea Manning**; trans men: **Elliot Page, Chaz Bono**. Trans identity is **not dependent on medical or surgical procedures**.
- **Gender expression** — external manifestations (clothing, grooming, behavior, body language, gestures, names) — can be changed in a **transition** to align with gender identity rather than assigned sex.
- **Gender nonconforming / nonbinary:** don't fit neatly into binary classifications — may include **genderqueer, gender fluid, bigender**; **not all nonbinary people consider themselves transgender**. Language is rapidly evolving — **use the term an individual prefers**.
- Sociologists now think of sex, gender, and sexuality not as binaries but as **existing on a spectrum**.

## 2. Sexuality and Sexual Orientation

- **Sexuality** = sexual behavior, desires, and fantasies; also used for **sexual orientation/identity** — the inclination to feel sexual desire toward and engage sexually with persons of a particular sex or gender.
- **Heterosexuality** (desire for the other gender) is the **normative and dominant category** — "straight" — with privileges attached.
- **Homosexuality:** minority category — **~4% of adults** identify as gay or lesbian (National Survey of Sexual Health and Behavior; Reece et al. 2010).
- **Bisexuality:** ~**3% of men and 4% of women** (Reece et al. 2010).
- **Asexuality:** lack of sexual attraction of any kind — **~1% of adults** (Bogaert 2004).
- **A more recent CDC (2015) study:** 97.6% heterosexual, **1.6% gay or lesbian, 0.8% bisexual** — the exact percentages are contested.
- **Alfred Kinsey (1940s):** human sexuality is far more diverse than assumed — people are **not exclusively heterosexual or homosexual** but fall along a wide spectrum. **The Kinsey Scale (Figure 9.1):** sexual orientation is best understood as a **fluid continuum** that can change over a lifetime — not binary categories.

### "Queering the Binary"
- "Queer" was a **pejorative term** for most of the 20th century (applied mainly to gay men; originally meaning "odd" or "peculiar").
- In the **1990s, queer theory** transformed the term: it **rejects the idea of a single gay/lesbian/bisexual/heterosexual/transgender identity**, emphasizing instead the **importance of difference** (Butler 1993) — being queer is about "possibilities, gaps, overlaps, dissonances, and resonances" (Sedgwick 1993, p. 8); **any kind of categorization is outmoded and limiting**.
- **Queer** now describes anything that **challenges prevailing binary notions of sex, gender, and sexuality** — wider than "gay" or "trans"; hence the **"Q" in LGBTQ**.
- **LGBTQIA** proposes adding **intersex** and **asexual** — "A" is not for "allies" (allies are not oppressed the same way); "+" sometimes added for allies and other identities.

## 3. Socialization: Sex, Gender, and Sexuality

**Gender role socialization** — the subtle, pervasive process of learning masculinity/femininity — begins early and continues throughout life, carried out by the four major agents of socialization: **families, schools, peers, and the media** (religion may also play a part). It also perpetuates **heteronormativity** (the assumption that heterosexuality is the only acceptable orientation).

### 👨‍👩👧 Families
- **Kara Smith (2005):** gender role socialization begins **even before birth** — knowing the fetus's sex changes how the mother talks to it (word choice, tone). After birth: gendered clothes, rooms, toys, and stories.
- **Social learning:** through interaction, babies respond to and internalize the expectations of significant others — consciously ("big boys don't cry") and subtly (observation, imitation, play). **By age two** children are aware of their own and others' gender; **by age three** they identify specific traits associated with each gender.
- Gender pervades family life: chores, discipline, where children can go, "that's not very ladylike" — echoed in children's literature, toys, and games; family members serve as role models into adulthood (career, mate choice, how we raise our own children).
- **Change:** **Tey Meadow's (2018)** ethnography of families of trans kids found parents today are more accepting of gender nonconformity — sometimes **actively facilitating it** (allowing preferred clothing, new names) — treating it as "a form of gender itself rather than a failure of gender," a significant departure from the past (when parents sought psychiatric "cures").
- **How families are formed matters:** laws historically ratified only heterosexual marriage, teaching that heterosexuality and biological parenting are "normal" — overlooking the many other ways real families are formed.

### 🏫 Schools
- Girls and boys are put in **same-sex groups** and given gender-stereotyped tasks early on (Love and Kelly 2011; Thorne 1993); by fifth grade gender norms are firmly established, even in co-ed schools.
- **Teachers (women and men) tend to favor boys:** boys receive more attention, instructional time, are called on more, posed more challenging questions, and get more praise for work quality — but are also punished more for misbehaving (Smith 1999).
- Girls earn higher grades in elementary school (Buchmann, DiPrete, and McDaniel 2008) — but their achievement is **discounted** (credited to hard work, not ability), and media spin this discrepancy as a "crisis for boys" (Rosin 2012; Sommers 2015). By middle school, girls lose academic self-esteem — uneasy competing with boys, embarrassed by success, uncomfortable in math/science.
- Textbooks still contain **sexist language and gender stereotypes**; women and minorities underrepresented as subjects and authors (Robson 2001). In the school's social structure, **women are concentrated at lower levels** (teachers/aides) while men hold administration — schooling reinforces stereotypes structurally.
- **For LGBTQ students:** school teaches that acceptance requires hiding identity — heteronormativity pervades school life (textbook images of traditional families, prom kings/queens); **binary restrooms are problematic for trans students**.

### 👥 Peers
- Peer groups are an increasingly important agent of socialization (see [[Chapter 5]]). By age three children prefer same-sex playmates; **preschoolers are 3× more likely** to play with same-sex playmates, **11× more likely in kindergarten** (Maccoby and Jacklin 1987). Evidence suggests same-sex peer groups **create** gendered behavior rather than reflecting it: activities with same-sex peers are more gender-typed (Fabes, Martin, and Hanish 2003), and children (especially boys) are **mocked by peers for crossing gendered borders** (Thorne 1993).
- **C. J. Pascoe's *Dude, You're a Fag* (2007):** boys police each other's masculinity and "lay claim to masculine identities by lobbing **homophobic epithets** at one another" — calling someone a "fag" inflates the bully's masculinity and demonstrates the consequences of deviating (see labeling theory, [[Chapter 6]]).
- **Sarah Miller (2016):** girls also bully — homophobic labeling, spreading sexual rumors, and **"slut shaming"** reinforce traditional gender and sexuality norms.
- Teen prestige: boys gain it through **athletic ability, humor, risk-taking, defying norms** (Kimmel 2009); girls through **social position and physical attractiveness** — pressure that can produce bullying, rebellious behavior, or eating disorders; dating pressures enforce heteronormativity into early adulthood.

### 📺 Media
- From movies, comic books, and popular music we learn "how to behave, how to be accepted, what to value, and what is normal" (Barner 1999). "By the time a child reaches kindergarten, she will 'know' more television characters than real people."
- Stereotypes: girls should be beautiful, caring, sensitive, reserved; boys assertive, strong, analytic.
- **Before the 1960s, sexual diversity was absent from TV.** The 1967 CBS documentary *The Homosexuals*: "the fact that somebody's homosexual... automatically rules out the possibility that he will remain happy for long."
- **The turning points:** *Ellen* (1997) — first lesbian lead in a prime-time series; *Will & Grace* (1999) — two gay male characters; cable: *Queer as Folk, The L Word, Queer Eye for the Straight Guy*, and later *RuPaul's Drag Race, Pose*; trans visibility: **Laverne Cox** in *Orange Is the New Black* (2013), *I Am Cait* (2015), *I Am Jazz*; *Euphoria* (**Hunter Schafer**), *The Fosters*, *Supergirl*; **Asia Kate Dillon** — first nonbinary actor playing nonbinary character Taylor Mason on *Billions*.
- **Not equal yet:** **no gay male kiss on network TV until 2000** (two lesbian kisses preceded it); **Michael Sam** (2014) — first openly gay NFL draftee — kissed his boyfriend on camera and scandalized the sports world; it took until **June 2021** for an active player (Raiders linebacker **Carl Nassib**) to come out. Trans characters are often still cast as **villains, victims, or sex workers**.
- **Contact hypothesis** (Pettigrew and Tropp 2011): more contact between groups reduces prejudice — so realistic media portrayals matter when media are people's *only* exposure to LGBTQ people.
- The media also **socialize families, peers, and educators**, giving them an even more overarching power.

## 4. Sexism in Its Many Forms

Gender and sexuality, like race/ethnicity, have been used to establish hierarchies of inequality that benefit the dominant group: **privileges, opportunities, and resources are distributed based on category membership.**

### Patriarchy
- Gender inequality in all past and present societies invariably takes the form of **patriarchy** — male domination. There is **little evidence a matriarchal (female-dominated) society ever existed**, though some are more pro-feminine: the **Vanatinai** of New Guinea, where women share equal access to prestige, power, and control over the means of production (Lepowsky 1993).
- **Male privilege** = unearned advantages: men may take up more physical space, aren't expected to smile at all times, are less likely to be interrupted while speaking.
- **Patriarchal account of origins:** biological differences in early societies — hunting and warfare (scarce, prized resources) went to men, who became powerful by controlling distribution; women handled the "home" territory. **This does not explain persistence** — physical strength is no longer required in most jobs, and women are not restricted by childbearing demands.
- **Sexism** — the belief that one sex (usually male) is superior — persists in all contemporary cultures as an ideology that justifies inequality. Its extreme form is **misogyny** (contempt for or hatred of women). **Moya Bailey (2010, 2021)** coined **"misogynoir"** for the unique anti-Black sexism Black women experience.
- Women occupy a **minority position** even though they are a numerical majority — earning less for the same job, passed over for promotion. #MeToo points to the need for more work toward gender equity.

### Prejudice and Discrimination
- **Prejudice** = entrenched attitudes/prejudgments about a group's presumed characteristics, applied to all members, unlikely to change — rooted in stereotypes (the "dumb blonde," the "promiscuous gay man") and hard to avoid since they permeate culture and institutions.
- **Discrimination** = behavior resulting in **unequal treatment** based on category membership (charged more for a car or home loan; a recruiter consistently favoring men). Individual acts that are **cumulative or reproduced across workplaces and industries** become institutional.
- **Silicon Valley sexism:** the **Google memo (August 2017)** — a 3,300-word internal manifesto by a male engineer arguing women's underrepresentation in tech is due to supposed biological differences (women's "higher anxiety," "lower stress tolerance"; men's "higher drive for status") (Swisher 2017). Similar charges against **Pinterest, Oracle, Twitter, Microsoft**.
  - **Pinterest settlement:** former COO **Françoise Brougher** received **$22.5 million** after being abruptly fired in April 2020 — her post "The Pinterest Paradox: Cupcakes and Toxicity": "Female executives, even at the highest levels, are marginalized, excluded, and silenced" even though the staff was largely female.
- **Homophobia** (fear of nonheterosexuals) and **transphobia** (fear of gender nonconformity) are not true "phobias" (psychological phenomena like agoraphobia) but **prejudices — learned cultural norms**. Critics prefer **heterosexism** and **cisgenderism** — analogous to sexism/racism, describing an ideological system that stigmatizes any nonheterosexual or noncisgender identity (Frank 2012; Kitzinger 1987; Herek 1990; Rothblum 1996).
- **Coming out** can feel unsafe in an anti-gay, anti-trans society — fear of social/emotional repercussions and of hate crimes. **2019 hate crimes: 17% motivated by anti–sexual orientation bias, 4% by gender/gender-identity bias** (FBI 2020b) — believed underreported.

### Hegemonic and Toxic Masculinity
- **Hegemonic masculinity** (Connell and Messerschmidt 2005): an ideal of independence, aggression, competitiveness, and toughness held out as **superior to other masculinities and to femininity**; rejects anything feminine, homosexuality, and alternate gender expression. Men must measure up and prove themselves — but the hierarchy puts **upper-middle-class white, cisgender, heterosexual men at the top**.
- **Toxic masculinity**: the dysfunctional aspects of the ideal — men must compete, be breadwinners and leaders, suppress all emotions except anger, reject empathy/nurturance. It constricts men's careers, hobbies, and relationships and **harms men's mental and physical health** (Iwamoto et al. 2018); it partly explains why men were **less likely to wear masks during the pandemic** (Galasso et al. 2020; Palmer and Peterson 2020).
- **The incel movement** ("involuntary celibate"): largely young, white, heterosexual men who feel entitled to sex and rage against women when denied. Grew in online forums (a now-banned subreddit had 40,000+ subscribers) with misogynistic, objectifying rhetoric. **Elliot Rodger** (2014) wrote a 140-page manifesto before killing **6 people and injuring 14** in Isla Vista, California (near UC Santa Barbara), then himself. Other incel-affiliated mass murders have followed; the movement has migrated to other platforms. Labeled a **terrorist organization/hate group** promoting **male supremacy** — a misogynist ideology asserting women's fundamental inferiority and blaming feminism for the "crisis of masculinity."

### 💞 In Relationships: Rape Culture and Campus Social Life
- **The Brock Turner case:** at Stanford, two students saw a man sexually assaulting an unconscious woman behind a dumpster outside a fraternity house, chased and pinned him until police arrived. **Turner** (Stanford freshman swimmer) was convicted of **three counts of felony sexual assault**; prosecutors asked for six years — the judge gave **six months in county jail** (fearing "collateral consequences"); he served **three months**. The victim, known for years as **Emily Doe**, identified herself as **Chanel Miller** and published *Know My Name* (2019).
- **The scale:** at least **1 in 5 women (and 1 in 16 men)** in college will be victims of sexual assault. College survey: **10%** of women physically forced to have intercourse, **15%** had someone try and fail, **11%** victimized while passed out, asleep, drugged, or incapacitated (Ford and England 2015).
- **Rape culture** = an environment normalizing sexual violence against women. Campus features that contribute (Armstrong, Hamilton, and Sweeney 2006):
  - The expectation that students **party** — and campus alcohol policies push parties **off campus into fraternity houses**, giving frat brothers control over organization, alcohol, and what happens "if something goes wrong." Most campus assaults happen at or after a party.
  - **Hookup culture** (Wade 2017a): quick, casual sexual encounters — enjoyed by many, but celebrated behaviors blur the line between hookup and assault: men encouraged "on the prowl," getting women drunk (**alcohol is the most commonly used date rape drug on campus**), groping, force.
  - **Poor institutional response:** colleges protect reputations (especially athletes and fraternity brothers); **fewer than one-third of campus sexual assault cases result in expulsion** (Kingkade 2014); survivors often don't report.
  - **Victim blaming:** women are advised how to avoid rape (don't party alone, don't get intoxicated...) — implying assault is her failure — while there is far less focus on educating men in rape prevention and intervening with other men.
  - **Title IX:** federal civil rights law protecting people from sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs; the Office for Civil Rights issued 2011 guidelines on handling assault allegations; backed by the threat of withdrawing federal funding — but awareness has grown alongside backlash about overreach.

### Gendered Language and Microaggressions
- Language reflects cultural values — the English language has long assumed **male as the default category**: generic "he," "mankind," "all men are created equal," "man-made" — making women feel excluded and demeaned.
- Heterosexual assumptions: using "gay" or "queer" as insults; the new acquaintance who asks a woman "what does your husband do?"; the "Ladies' Man" T-shirt for a nephew — small acts that reinforce heterosexist assumptions and make LGBTQ people feel invisible.
- **Microaggressions** (Sue 2010): subtle denigrating communications, intended or not. Examples: being told to **smile** (Serena Williams, after a hard-fought evening match: "It's 11:30. To be perfectly honest with you, I don't want to be here."); being interrupted; being labeled **shrill or bossy**; catcalls; **misgendering** trans persons (accidentally or deliberately — refusing to use a chosen name/pronoun).
- **Respect preferred language:** allow others to self-identify and affirm it — use chosen names and preferred pronouns ("he"/"she"/neutral "they"); LGBTQ organizations (GLAAD, HRC) post glossaries online.

## 5. Sociological Theories of Gender Inequality

### Structural Functionalism
- Functionalism holds that some **social roles are better suited to one gender** and that societies are more stable when norms are fulfilled by the appropriate sex.
- **Talcott Parsons (Parsons and Bales 1955):** two complementary roles within the family — the **instrumental role** (task-oriented "breadwinner," authority figure) and the **expressive role** (emotional support and nurturing). The expressive role stabilizes the instrumental partner's personality against the competitive world; women are "better suited" to expressive, men to instrumental — gender segregation upholds the traditional family (*The Adventures of Ozzie and Harriet* = the era's prototypical family).
- **Critiques:** rewards for the roles are far from equal — feminine work is devalued as unskilled and instinctive; evidence shows juvenile delinquency and crime rates are higher without adult supervision (expressive roles matter); families are often sources of **instability, violence, and abuse**. The perspective has fallen out of favor within sociology.

### Conflict Theory
- Men have historically had access to most material resources and privileges and seek to **maintain their dominant status** — gender inequality is exploitation, like race and class inequality.
- **Friedrich Engels (1884):** gender inequality is a **derivative of class inequality**, originating with **private property**. Capitalists benefited from patriarchal families in two ways: women **reproduce the labor force without compensation** and serve as an inexpensive **"reserve army" of labor**. Abolish private property → classes disappear → no powerful interests forcing women into domestic roles.
- **Zillah Eisenstein (1979):** all men benefit from gender inequality in the short term — without gender segregation they'd do more unpaid work or pay for it, face a larger more competitive job market, and lose power and prestige as "breadwinners." (Note the overlap with class analysis in [[Chapter 7]].)

### Symbolic Interactionism
- Gender is **socially constructed and maintained in everyday life** — we can barely interact with anyone without first determining their gender identity; we need to categorize and be categorizable. Trans/nonbinary people who don't fit existing categories experience difficulty in interaction.
- **Harold Garfinkel (1967/1984) and "Agnes":** a trans woman born with male genitalia, raised as a boy, undergoing sex-reassignment treatment at UCLA. She always knew she was a "120 percent natural normal woman" but had to learn to **"do being female"** — dress, makeup, body language, speech, deference to her male boss, expectations as future wife/mother — plus extra precautions (avoiding intercourse, skirts disguising her anatomy, avoiding swimming) until her surgical transition. **Even Garfinkel found her enactment of femininity convincing.** The case shows gender is something we **"do"** — and we all enact gender the same way Agnes did. (Here gender meets the social construction of the self from [[Chapter 4]] — the self is not an inner essence but something we do and present in interaction.)
- **The "chilly climate" in classrooms** (Lee and McCabe 2021): from 95 hours of observation in nine elite-college classrooms, men students took up more "sonic space," **speaking 1.6× as often as women**; they spoke without raising hands, interrupted professors and classmates, held ongoing side conversations; men used more assertive language while women were more hesitant and apologetic — reinforcing gender status hierarchies. **Professors can change the dynamic** (calling on students in turn, enforcing anti-interruption rules).

### Feminist Theory
- Flourished alongside the **women's movement**; related to conflict theory (focus on inequality) and interactionism (focus on lived experience). Applies assumptions about gender inequality to institutions — family, education, economy, media — to understand them anew and **"write women back in"** to scholarship where their lives and contributions were minimized or overlooked.
- **Judith Butler (1999), bell hooks (2003), Catharine MacKinnon (2005):** link gender inequality to other hierarchies — race/ethnicity, class, sexual orientation — arguing **gender and power are inextricably intertwined**.

> **Table 9.2 — Theory in Everyday Life (Male- and Female-Dominated Occupations):**
> - **Structural functionalism:** sex determines which roles men and women are best suited to — women are "naturally" more nurturing (nurses, teachers of young children); men "naturally" more logical (lawyers, computer programmers).
> - **Conflict theory:** the traditional division of labor gives men more resources and privileges, and they maintain dominance — male-dominated occupations carry more prestige and pay; women struggle to enter them, while men succeed more easily in female-dominated occupations.
> - **Symbolic interactionism:** gender is learned through socialization — girls and boys are encouraged toward "gender-appropriate" training, majors, and career goals, channeling them into gendered occupations.

## 6. Gender, Sexuality, and Life Chances

Predictions about a girl and boy born at the same time, same place, same background: who lives longer, goes to college, goes to prison, earns well or lives in poverty, marries/divorces/widows, becomes a single parent, is a violent-crime victim, joins the military? **Remember intersectionality** — gender and sexuality are intertwined with race and class ([[Chapter 7]], [[Chapter 8]]), and the categories work together to construct the complexity of a person's life.

### 👨‍👩‍👧 Families
- **Marriage:** men are more likely than women to report never having been married (**36% vs. 30%**) — reflecting stronger societal pressure on women to marry; racial disparity: **47% of Black women vs. 27% of white women** never marry. Women are more likely to be **widowed** (8% vs. <3%) — due partly to longer life spans (Census 2020d).
- **Divorce hits women harder:** ~**4 of every 5 custodial parents are mothers**; less than **half (46%)** receive the full child support due, nearly a third receive none (Grall 2020).
- **Poverty (2019):** **31% of single mothers** with children under 18 lived in poverty vs. **15% of single fathers** and just **5% of married-couple families** (Census 2021b).
- **The second shift:** working women remain primary caregivers — time off for sick children is seen as nonproductive (Weisshaar 2018; Wharton and Blair-Loy 2002); at home women still do a disproportionate share of unpaid work (Hochschild and Machung 1989). **During Covid-19**, when day cares and schools closed, working mothers cut back on paid work **far more than working fathers** (Landivar et al. 2020).
- **LGBTQ youth and family:** coming out can be hard; **parental rejection** is associated with higher depression, attempted suicide, and risk-taking (unsafe sex, illegal drugs) (Ryan et al. 2009; SAMHSA 2015). Largely because of family rejection, **LGBTQ youth are more than 2× more likely to experience homelessness** (Romero, Goldberg, and Vasquez 2020); youth with supportive families and **"families of choice"** have better self-esteem and health (Ryan et al. 2010).
- **Marriage equality:** **Obergefell v. Hodges (June 2015)** — the Supreme Court ruled same-sex couples have a constitutional right to marry, legalizing gay marriage nationwide. A majority of same-sex couples (**61%**) are now married (up from 38% before the ruling); **nearly 10% of all LGBTQ adults** are married to a same-sex spouse (Jones 2021).

### 🩺 Health
- **Life expectancy:** females born in 2018 are expected to live **81.2 years vs. 76.2 for males** (CDC 2021b). The male average is depressed by young men's risk of accidental death: men ages 20–24 are **~3× more likely to die from accidents, >4× more likely to commit suicide, ~7× more likely to be murdered** (Heron 2018).
- The gap is **the smallest in fifty years**: women's stress-related behaviors (working outside the home, smoking, drinking) increased risk — lung cancer was once "a man's disease," but women's relative risk is now nearly identical to men's; researchers call smoking **"the great equalizer"** (Thun 2013; NIH 2014; Perls and Fretts 1998).
- **Depression:** women are **almost twice as likely** to suffer from it (Burton 2012; Kessler 2003). Historically women were diagnosed far more often with depression and "hysteria" and denied rights, working conditions, and pay as a "mentally unfit" category — though women may also be more likely to report symptoms while men feel stigma (Byrne 1981; Martin, Neighbors, and Griffith 2013).
- **Sexual orientation and health:** living in areas with high anti-gay prejudice was associated with a **"life expectancy difference of roughly 12 years"** (Hatzenbuehler et al. 2014) — suicide/homicide plus psychosocial stressors linked to cardiovascular risk. Lesbian, gay, and bisexual high school students are more likely to use illicit drugs (heroin, cocaine, prescription opioids) and are at higher risk of mental illness and suicide (CDC 2021e).

### 🏢 On the Job: The Pandemic "She-cession"
- In every previous U.S. recession (including 2008), men suffered higher unemployment — but **for the first time ever, women faced greater job and income loss during Covid-19**. **March–December 2020: women lost 5.4 million jobs vs. 4.4 million for men** (Boesch and Phadke 2021); in **December 2020 alone the economy lost 140,000 jobs — all women's** (Ewing-Nelson 2021). **C. Nicole Mason** (Institute for Women's Policy Research) coined **"she-cession"** (Gupta 2020b).
- **Why:** past recessions hit male-dominated finance and manufacturing; Covid hit the **service sector**, which is disproportionately female:
  - **Leisure and hospitality:** ~2 in 5 jobs lost — women are 53% of workers.
  - **Education and health services:** women are **77% of workers** — 13% of all jobs lost when schools/day cares closed and nonessential procedures stopped.
  - **Public sector:** 58% women — reduced tax revenues caused job losses.
- **Intersection of gender and race:** women of color fared worst — **Hispanic women** had the worst outcomes of any group (overrepresented in leisure/hospitality and education/health services); **Black women** concentrated in the public sector (almost 18% of workers) also saw high unemployment.
- **Caregiving:** when schools and day cares shut, women took on remote-learning supervision and child care — in **September 2020 alone, more than 850,000 women dropped out of the labor force, nearly 4× the number of men** (Boesch and Phadke 2021).
- The she-cession may **stall or even reverse** women's workforce gains (Covington and Kent 2020).

### 🎓 Education
- **Fall 2019:** 16.6 million undergraduates — **9.4 million female**. Since the mid-1990s women have been the majority of college graduates; by 2000 more women earned master's degrees, by 2005 more doctorates. **2019: women earned 57% of bachelor's, 61% of master's, and 54% of doctorate degrees** (NCES 2020).
- **LGBTQ students:** the 2019 National School Climate Survey (Kosciw et al. 2020) — **more than 8 in 10 LGBTQ students experienced harassment or assault at school**: **81% verbally harassed, 34% physically harassed, 15% physically assaulted** in the past year because of sexual orientation or gender expression. Anti-bullying policies and stigma-awareness programs help (Rabow, Stein, and Conley 1999).

### 💼 Work and Income
- **Labor force (2020):** 68% of men vs. 56% of women (BLS 2021e). Mothers' participation rose from **47% (1975) to 71% (2020)**. Marriage has opposite effects: never-married women are more likely to work than married women; married men more likely than single men (the "head of household" assumption).
- **Occupational segregation (Table 9.3, 2020):** female-dominated — **98.8%** of preschool/kindergarten teachers, **94.4%** of speech language pathologists, **93.9%** of dental hygienists, **92.9%** of secretaries, **88.3%** of maids/housekeepers, **87.4%** of registered nurses, **85.8%** of paralegals; male-dominated — **5.6%** of aircraft pilots, **4.4%** of firefighters, **3.2%** of carpenters, **1.4%** of automotive mechanics; clergy **18.5%** women, chief executives **29.3%**.
- **The gender pay gap:** women earn **about 82 cents for every dollar a man earns** (Figure 9.2; Semega et al. 2020). Causes: (1) **occupational sex segregation** — "women's work" (education, health services) is undervalued and underpaid (Chodorow 2009); (2) **motherhood and the disproportionate housework/child-care burden** limiting paid work time; (3) **ongoing discrimination** and job-market inequalities. **Compounded by race:** Black women earn **68 cents** and Hispanic women **62 cents** for each dollar a white man earns (BLS 2020e) — the gap is largest for women of color.
- **Feminization of poverty:** 2019 — **11.5% of women lived in poverty vs. 9.4% of men** (Semega et al. 2020); women of color disproportionately represented among the poor.
- **LGBTQ discrimination:** where not prohibited by law, employers can still discriminate in hiring and pay — **11–28% of LGB workers** report losing a promotion because of sexual orientation; **27% of transgender workers** report being fired, not hired, or denied a promotion (Singh and Durso 2017). Per worker, the rate of LGBTQ discrimination is several times higher than for women. The **Employment Non-Discrimination Act (ENDA)**, which would add sexual orientation and gender identity to the protected classes, **has consistently failed to pass Congress**.

> **📋 Data Workshop — The Second Shift: Gender Norms and Household Labor** (interview): **Arlie Hochschild** coined "the second shift" — the unpaid work (cooking, cleaning, laundry, child care, home repair, yard work) done at home after the day's paid labor (Hochschild and Machung 1989). Women do most of it — racking up **an extra month of housework a year**. Analyze: Is there a gendered division of labor? Who does most of the daily work? Is it inevitable that one partner does more — what effects do inequities have on relationships? *Prep-Pair-Share* or a 3-page essay with quotations and interview attachments.

### ⚖️ Criminal Justice
- The social construction of masculinity as aggressive, dominant, and physical corresponds to crime statistics: men are more likely to **die violent deaths and be assault victims**; women are slightly more likely to be victims of personal theft and much more likely to be rape victims — and **far more likely to be victimized by intimate partners**: **about half of female homicide victims are killed by current or former intimate partners** (Petrosky et al. 2017).
- **Arrests (2019):** men are overwhelmingly represented in nearly all categories — murder, rape, sex offenses, theft, assault, drug charges. The **only two categories where women outnumber men: embezzlement and prostitution** (FBI 2020a). Because males are perceived as more crime-prone, they're kept under more police scrutiny. Of ~**1.5 million people in state and federal prisons, ~93% are men** (Carson 2020).
- **LGBTQ experiences:** bias-motivated attacks based on sexual orientation are the **third-highest hate-crime category** (after race and religion); **>20% of all hate crimes** are motivated by sexual-orientation or gender/gender-identity bias. **2019: 1,195 anti–sexual orientation hate crimes and 198 anti-transgender/anti–gender-nonconforming attacks** (FBI 2020b). Proportionally, **LGBTQ people are the most likely targets of hate crimes in America**; LGBTQ people (especially LGBTQ people of color) are overrepresented in jails and prisons and more likely to suffer verbal, physical, and sexual abuse there. **Transgender inmates are >10× more likely to be sexually assaulted than other prisoners; LGB inmates ~3× more likely** (Beck et al. 2013).

### 🔀 Intersectionality
- **Miliann Kang (2010)** — nail salons: the work of Asian immigrant women (mostly from Korea) shows gender + race + class + immigration status intersecting. The femininity of salon beauty rituals is **racialized**: "subservience" is seen as "natural" in Asian women, so customers feel entitled to make more demands — manicurists provided "massage, elder-care, counseling for teens, community outreach, therapy for stressed-out clients" beyond paid services.
- The manicure table is not just women sharing gendered experience: one African American customer framed her manicure in terms of **race, not gender** — "Black people on a whole have not been the ones who get pampered. There was a time when only white people could do this" — a symbol of racial progress. Male clients were viewed with suspicion — they came "just to hold a woman's hand" (Kang 2010).
- **The bottom line on life outcomes:** the female infant is likely to **live longer**; both are likely to marry, but she is more likely to divorce or be widowed; she is more likely to graduate high school and attend college (if she doesn't drop out in response to anti-LGBTQ harassment), but if he also attends he's more likely to graduate; with the same degree he'll probably earn more; the military is likelier for him, nursing for her; he is more likely to die a violent death, she to experience rape or intimate crime. If either is LGBTQ, higher risk of bias-motivated attacks. **Gender and sexual categories don't guarantee these experiences — but sociologists can predict them in the aggregate.**

## 7. Social Movements

Most societies throughout history have been **patriarchal and heteronormative**, so women and LGBTQ people have often struggled to attain and preserve basic human and civil rights.

### Women's Movements
- **Feminism** = belief in social, political, and economic equality of the sexes + the movements organized around it — both a theoretical perspective and a social movement. Goals are never static. **Rebecca West (1913):** "I only know that people call me a feminist whenever I express sentiments that differentiate me from a doormat or a prostitute."

**FIRST WAVE (mid-1800s–1920)**
- Began at the **Seneca Falls convention (1848)**, organized by **Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott** (~300 people); issued a **Declaration of Sentiments** — "all men and women are created equal" — demanding the right to vote. Stanton believed the vote is the fundamental right on which all others depend.
- The **suffrage movement** became the wave's defining cause; victory came in **1920** — after Stanton, Mott, and **Susan B. Anthony** had died. Of the 100 signers of the Declaration of Sentiments, only one — **Charlotte Woodward**, a young worker — lived to cast a ballot.

**SECOND WAVE (1960s–70s)**
- Associated with **equal access to education and employment**. Key events: **Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963)** — "the problem that had no name," the sense of limitation and dissatisfaction many women felt, buried "unspoken, for many years in the minds of American women" — the **National Organization for Women (NOW) (1966)**, and women's **consciousness-raising groups**.
- Women were restricted by cultural norms and actual laws; those who breached barriers were seen as "unfeminine" — **Elizabeth Dole** was told, entering Harvard Law School in 1962, that she was taking an opportunity away from a more deserving man.
- Achievements: **equal opportunity laws, legislation against sexual harassment and marital rape, greater public awareness** — plus backlash against feminist causes.

**THIRD WAVE (1980s–90s onward)**
- Focused primarily on **diversity**: criticized the first two waves for treating "women" as one (mainly white, middle-class) category and marginalizing women of color, lesbians, and working-class women. More focused on **intersectionality** and on personal identity and freedom from limiting categories; also concerned with **globalization**, women's rights worldwide, and environmental and animal rights. "You are the third wave, and you will help make a difference."

### Men's Movements
- The women's movement asked men to rethink gender roles — responses ranged from opposition to support.
- **Men's liberation (mid-1970s):** freeing men from oppressive gender roles; studies pointed to men's greater stress, poorer health, shorter life expectancy from pressures to succeed and inability to express emotion (Farrell 1975; Goldberg 1976); American men confused about being a "real man" (Kimmel 1987) — a "**crisis of masculinity**" (Bly 1990; Connell 1995; Faludi 1999). Popular among middle-class white heterosexual men.
- The movement **splintered into two factions**:
  - **Men's rights movement** (incl. fathers' rights): claims feminism discriminates against/oppresses men — that it created "a new kind of sexism" by privileging women. The **incel movement** shares a similar ideology, focused on men's "right" to sex.
  - **Pro-feminist men's movement:** men should support feminism for fairness and because men's lives are also constrained by sexism — sharing child care, contesting economic disparities and violence against women, valuing women's lives.

### LGBTQ Movements
- **Stonewall (1968):** police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York City; after a history of harassment, pent-up resentment erupted into a **week of violence** — a watershed moment that jump-started the modern, visible LGBTQ liberation movement.
- **Dana Rosenfeld (2003):** two cohorts — those who lived before the gay liberation movement (identity would have *discredited* them if public) and those during/after (making identity public was *celebrating an essential aspect of the self*). Post-Stonewall groups fought on many fronts: AIDS research, anti–hate crime legislation.
- **Marriage equality:** early victories via **domestic partnership** statutes and **civil unions**; in **2004 the first states legalized same-sex marriage** while others banned it.
  - Opponents: rhetoric of "protecting marriage/family" and religious grounds. Proponents: marriage is a state-sanctioned right; denial is discriminatory, creating second-class citizens — and legal marriage conveys benefits domestic partnerships don't: **Social Security, veterans' and disability benefits, inheritance**.
  - **Internal LGBTQ critique:** not everyone supported same-sex marriage — some saw it as **assimilationist**, accepting heteronormative structures (binary gender roles, monogamy) rather than critiquing them. **Lisa Duggan (2003) and Cathy Cohen (2005):** marriage advocacy creates **"hierarchies of worthiness,"** benefiting the most "socially acceptable" LGBTQ people — those who most closely mimic heterosexual unions. Others argue LGBTQ people should challenge marriage itself as a flawed institution (Sycamore 2008).
- **Obergefell v. Hodges (2015):** the Supreme Court legalized same-sex marriage nationwide, holding the right to marry is guaranteed by the **Fourteenth Amendment**; before the ruling it was legal in 37 states + DC and banned in 13; the U.S. joined 21 other nations in recognizing same-sex marriage.
  - **Public opinion flipped:** 2001 — **57% opposed, 35% in favor**; 2019 — **61% in favor, 31% opposed** (Pew 2019a). Young Americans show the highest approval; **white evangelicals and Black Protestants** the lowest.
- **Remaining issues:** discrimination in housing, education, employment, the military, health care, plus everyday microaggressions, harassment, and violence — though awareness is increasing; hate crimes based on sexual/gender identity are punishable under federal law, and a growing minority of states/territories outlaw discrimination based on sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression. **In 2021, Dr. Rachel Levine** was appointed assistant secretary for health — the **first openly trans federal official confirmed by the Senate**.

### 📡 In the Future: Human Trafficking
- **Modern-day slavery exists.** In 2017, U.S. law enforcement uncovered a "modern-day sex slave ring" — one of the most elaborate operations ever seen (Davey 2017): a multimillion-dollar operation running 8+ years, shuttling hundreds of women among **Chicago, Dallas, and Los Angeles**. Most came from impoverished communities in **Thailand**, promised a better life; instead they were required to work as prostitutes to pay off **"bondage debts" of up to $60,000**, sequestered and working all day, every day.
- Not isolated: **more than 8,200 sex-trafficking cases** reported to the National Human Trafficking Resource Center in 2019 alone. High-profile cases: **Jeffrey Epstein** (billionaire financier) and **Keith Raniere** (NXIVM cult leader), both sentenced to prison.
- **UN definition:** "the recruitment, transportation, transfer, harboring or receipt of persons, by means of the threat or use of force or other forms of coercion... for the purpose of exploitation" (UNODC 2021).
- **The scale:** ~**25 million victims worldwide** (U.S. State Dept 2021); sexual exploitation accounts for the majority of cases; armed groups increasingly use trafficking as a **tool of war**. **70% of victims worldwide are female; 23% are girls under eighteen.** **Michelle Bachelet** called trafficking "one of the fastest growing" and most lucrative crimes in the world; **only 1 in 100 victims is ever rescued** (Lederer 2012).
- **U.S. response:** DOS, DHS, and DOJ investigated **more than 1,800 cases in 2019**, securing **475 convictions**; advocates push for mandatory restitution to victims.
- **The path forward:** raising awareness (community members most often tip off law enforcement), resolving the social/economic problems that make people vulnerable (extreme poverty, sexism), coordinating international enforcement. **Nadia Murad** — kidnapped and held captive by the Islamic State, now a human-rights activist — was awarded the **Nobel Peace Prize in 2018**.

## Closing Comments

- **Sex, gender, sexual orientation, and trans identities are status categories that structure social inequality and shape individual lives.** They are different but interrelated, and we all experience their overlap — categorizing ourselves and others, making assumptions based on perceived categories.
- A sociological perspective reveals the **cultural and environmental influences on supposedly biological identities** and lets us identify and critique the stratification systems that result.
- Most important, it shows how **destructive sexism is for men and women** and how **crippling homophobia and transphobia can be** for the straight, cisgender majority as well as the queer minority.
- **Stereotypes are socially constructed; therefore, they can be socially deconstructed and socially reconstructed as well.**

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Sex ≠ gender:** sex is biological (chromosomes XX/XY, hormones, reproductive organs); gender is social/cultural (traits a group considers "normal, natural, right, and good" for males/females). **Intersex** = ~17 in 1,000 births.
- **Gender is a spectrum, not a binary:** essentialists (biology, two categories, can't change) vs. constructionists (socially determined — binary is just one possibility). Cisgender = identity matches assigned sex; transgender = doesn't; nonbinary doesn't fit the binary (not all nonbinary = trans). Gender expression ≠ gender identity; trans identity doesn't require medical procedures.
- **Third genders exist elsewhere:** two-spirits/berdaches (Native cultures, ~150), hijras (South Asia — Pakistan added a third gender to ID cards in 2012), bacha posh (Afghanistan — girl raised as a boy for family honor). Not the same as Western "transgender."
- **Sexuality is a fluid continuum (Kinsey),** not gay/straight binaries: ~4% gay/lesbian, 3–4% bisexual, 1% asexual (or CDC: 97.6/1.6/0.8). Queer theory rejects all categorization; LGBTQIA — "A" is asexual, not allies.
