# Social Inequality
Everyday class consciousness is the awareness of one's own social status and that of others. In other words, the ability to read class clues in appearance, speech, and possessions. When viewed from symbolic interactionism perspective, this ability explains how class is negotiated from moments to moments in daily life. Even though our social identities are structured by categories we did not choose, we still have to perform those identities in every interaction, while other people are also constantly interpreting those performances, through clothes, shoes, accents, and accessories. Sudnow found that we make these judgments instinctively; Goffman described clothing, speech, and cars as props in the presentation of self; Fussell built a scoring system out of living-room furniture. In this perspective, class is our background when interacting with others. 

Social reproduction, a concept from Bourdieu, is the tendency of social classes to remain relatively stable as class status is passed down from one generation to the next. The main mechanism is cultural capital, which is the tastes, habits, expectations, skills, and knowledge that help people gain advantages in society, all of which people largely inherit from their parents. For instance, educated parents help their children with homework, expect them to earn credentials, and teach them how to talk to teachers and interviewers. On the other hand, cultural capital also shapes how others perceive us, since socially skilled candidates tend to win job interviews and an accent can greatly improve relationship with people in their class. As a first-generation college student, this process is very real for me. Neither of my parents holds a four-year degree, so I entered college without the cultural capital that other students inherited. Specifically, no one at home knew what office hours were for, how financial aid worked, or how to network with professors. I had to acquire that knowledge on my own, and acquiring it takes time and effort that students with college-educated parents never had to spend. That gap is a form of social reproduction in action. 

The culture of poverty argument, proposed by Oscar Lewis in 1959, claims that poor communities can develop entrenched attitudes, including fatalism, resignation, a preference for immediate gratification, etc, all of which get passed down to children and keep them from trying to improve their situation. Critics reject this argument for several reasons. First, it blames the victims. It takes attitudes that poverty produces and treats them as the cause of poverty, ignoring the structural conditions leading to this, like underfunded schools, unstable jobs, or discriminatory housing. Second, it feeds the just-world hypothesis. People need to believe the world is fair, so we conclude that poor people deserves it, which makes us less willing to make changes in society. Finally, the argument lacks sociological imagination. It ignores the tax laws and policies that protect inherited fortunes while giving working families few breaks.
