# Chapter 5: Separate and Together: Life in Groups
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term                         | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Group**                    | A collection of **two or more people who share some attribute, identify with one another, and have ongoing social relations** (e.g., family, Star Trek fan club, soccer team, sorority, poker group).                                                               |
| **Crowd**                    | A **temporary gathering of people** in a public place; members might interact but do not identify with each other and will not remain in contact (Goffman 1971).                                                                                                    |
| **Aggregates**               | Collections of people who **share a physical location but do not have lasting social relations** (crowds, audiences, queues).                                                                                                                                       |
| **Category**                 | People who **share one or more attributes but lack a sense of common identity or belonging** (everyone 18 years old, all Korean American men, all Chevy truck owners).                                                                                              |
| **Primary groups**           | (Cooley 1909) Groups composed of the people **most important to our sense of self**; relationships are typically characterized by **face-to-face interaction, high levels of cooperation, and intense feelings of belonging** (families, close friends).            |
| **Secondary groups**         | Groups that are **larger and less intimate** than primary groups; relationships are usually organized around a **specific goal** and are often temporary (co-workers, college classes, labor unions, political parties).                                            |
| **Consequential strangers**  | (Blau and Fingerman 2009) People we don't think of as close but who nonetheless play **important, supportive roles** in our everyday lives — the barista, the gym regular, the checkout clerk; "social anchors" between primary and secondary ties.                 |
| **Social network**           | The **web of direct and indirect ties connecting an individual to other people** who may also affect the individual.                                                                                                                                                |
| **Social ties**              | **Connections between individuals**; can be **direct** (you and your friend) or **indirect** (you and your friend's cousin you've never met).                                                                                                                       |
| **Centrality**               | A network-analysis term meaning **an actor with the most ties in a given network**; centrality itself can confer influence regardless of wins/losses.                                                                                                               |
| **Anomie**                   | (Durkheim) **"Normlessness"**; the alienation and loss of purpose that result from weaker social bonds and an increased pace of change. Durkheim argued group norms place limits on desire; without them we become disconnected and may even be at risk of suicide. |
| **Virtual communities**      | Social groups whose interactions are **mediated through information technologies**, particularly the internet.                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Group dynamics**           | The **patterns of interaction between groups and individuals** — how groups form, change, disintegrate, achieve goals, or commit wrongs.                                                                                                                            |
| **Dyad**                     | (Simmel) The **smallest possible social group**: **two members**; relationships are intense but the group is fundamentally unstable because if one person leaves, it's over.                                                                                        |
| **Triad**                    | A **three-person social group**; slightly more stable than a dyad because a third member can **mediate conflict** between the other two.                                                                                                                            |
| **In-group**                 | A group that one **identifies with and feels loyalty toward**; members often feel distinctness from or hostility toward out-groups.                                                                                                                                 |
| **Out-group**                | A group toward which an individual feels **opposition, rivalry, or hostility**.                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Rite of passage**          | A ceremony marking an individual's **transformation from one type of group member to another** (e.g., hazing, military basic training).                                                                                                                             |
| **Degradation ceremony**     | (Garfinkel 1956) A ritual that **strips away an individual's prior identity** in order to prepare them for entry into a new group (shaved heads, uniforms, derogatory nicknames for recruits).                                                                      |
| **Reference group**          | A group that provides a **standard of comparison** against which we evaluate ourselves — including groups we aspire to join but are not yet members of.                                                                                                             |
| **Group cohesion**           | The **sense of solidarity or loyalty** that individuals feel toward a group to which they belong; the force that binds members together.                                                                                                                            |
| **Groupthink**               | (Janis 1971, 1982) In very cohesive groups, the tendency to **enforce a high degree of conformity**, creating a demand for unanimous agreement and suppressing critical reasoning.                                                                                  |
| **Social influence**         | **Peer pressure**; the impact of groups on others' decisions. Conformity generally happens because we want **acceptance/approval** and want to avoid **rejection/disapproval**.                                                                                     |
| **Compliance**               | The **mildest** kind of conformity: going along with something because you expect to **gain rewards or avoid punishments**, without actually changing your beliefs.                                                                                                 |
| **Identification**           | A **stronger** conformity induced by the desire to **establish or maintain a relationship** with a person or group; conforming to group wishes because you identify with the group.                                                                                 |
| **Internalization**          | The **strongest, most long-lasting** conformity: adopting the beliefs of a leader or group **as your own**; belief and behavior become one.                                                                                                                         |
| **Social loafing**           | The phenomenon in which **each individual contributes a little less as more individuals are added to a task**; a source of inefficiency in teams (Karau and Williams 1993).                                                                                         |
| **Social identity theory**   | A theory of group formation and maintenance stressing the need of members to **feel a sense of belonging**; the most efficient teams are characterized by the greatest shared social identity (Turner and Reynolds 2010).                                           |
| **Power**                    | The **ability to control the actions of others**.                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Coercive power**           | Power that is **backed by the threat of force**.                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Influential power**        | Power that is **supported by persuasion**.                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Authority**                | The **legitimate right to wield power**; most formal organizations institutionalize it in some recognized form.                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Traditional authority**    | (Weber) Authority based in **custom, birthright, or divine right** (monarchies, dynasties).                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Legal-rational authority** | (Weber) Authority based in **laws, rules, and procedures**, not in heredity or personality (modern presidencies, parliaments).                                                                                                                                      |
| **Charismatic authority**    | (Weber) Authority based in the **perception of remarkable personal qualities** in a leader; can be revolutionary.                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Instrumental leadership**  | (Parsons and Bales 1955) Leadership that is **task- or goal-oriented**.                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Expressive leadership**    | Leadership concerned with maintaining **emotional and relational harmony** within the group.                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Bureaucracy**              | A type of **secondary group designed to perform tasks efficiently**, characterized by specialization, technical competence, hierarchy, written rules, impersonality, and formal written communication (Weber).                                                      |
| **Rationalization**          | The application of **economic logic to human activity**; the use of formal rules and regulations to maximize efficiency without consideration of subjective or individual concerns.                                                                                 |
| **McDonaldization**          | (Ritzer 1993) The spread of **bureaucratic rationalization** into everyday life, with accompanying increases in efficiency **and dehumanization**.                                                                                                                  |
| **Honor killing**            | The **murder of a family member — usually female** — who is believed to have brought dishonor to the family or community; the killer is seen as a norm enforcer, not a norm violator.                                                                               |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: Hazing and the Power of Groups

- **FAMU "Marching 100" band bus (Nov 19, 2011):** drum major **Robert Champion** died after a brutal hazing ritual — being beaten with drum sticks, mallets, and straps while covered in a blanket. Hazing is meant to **test newcomers and transform them into group members**; the abuse is supposed to prove you can belong.
- **Aftermath:** 12 bandmates charged with manslaughter, 4 convicted; the band was suspended ~2 years; the band director was fired; the FAMU president resigned; Champion's family won a $1.1 million wrongful-death settlement and founded the **Robert D. Champion Drum Major for Change Foundation**.
- **The sociological puzzle:** some bandmates said Champion and others **volunteered** to be hazed to move into leadership roles. Who is responsible when group initiation turns illegal, injurious, or deadly — the group or the individual?
- **Hazing facts:** illegal in nearly every state and prohibited by most group charters, yet still common in fraternities, sororities, athletic teams, clubs, bands, church groups, police/fire departments, and the military; **150+ hazing deaths** among U.S. college students since 1970; alcohol is a major factor in ~80% of incidents (Nuwer 2018, 2019).
- **Why it matters:** like all primates, we are **social animals** — groups help us survive, but they can also make us do things we'd never do alone. The chapter explores **group dynamics** to understand both the benefits and dangers of group life.

> **Lesson:** Group membership shapes identity, behavior, and even life itself. Understanding groups helps explain the best *and* worst of social life — from solidarity and teamwork to hazing and groupthink.

## 1. What Is a Group?

### Sociological definition
- Everyday use: any collection of people with something in common. **Sociological use:** at least two people who share some attribute **AND** identify with one another **AND** have ongoing social relations.
- **Not groups in the sociological sense:**
  - **Crowds/aggregates** — tourists at an attraction, people watching a fire: they interact, but lack common identity and don't reassemble.
  - **Categories** — 18-year-olds, Korean American men, Chevy truck owners: share an attribute but don't regularly interact or feel connected.
- **Groups vs. aggregates/categories:** groups create **lasting social relations**.

### Primary and secondary groups
- **Primary groups** (Cooley 1909): intimate, face-to-face, cooperative, emotionally central — families and close friends. They are **"primary"** because they have the most profound effects on us: they provide emotional satisfaction, much of our socialization, and remain central to identity throughout life. They are our most important **"looking glasses"** and our **significant others**.
- **Covid-19 example:** during lockdown, primary groups — especially families and households — became the main source of support, socialization, and feedback.
- **Secondary groups:** larger, less intimate, formal, impersonal, usually organized around a task or activity — co-workers, college classes, athletic organizations, labor unions, political parties. Membership is often temporary and less emotionally satisfying.
- **Blurred line:** secondary groups often generate primary ties (friends you meet at work). Your soccer team may be both goal-oriented *and* personally meaningful — real life is messier than typologies.

### Consequential strangers
- (Blau and Fingerman 2009) We need more than close friends: familiar-but-not-close people — baristas, gym regulars, checkout clerks, manicurists — serve as **social anchors** in fragmented postmodern life.
- **Takeaway:** we function best with primary relationships, secondary relationships, **and** the continuum in between.

### Social networks
- **Social network** = web of direct and indirect ties connecting an individual to other people.
  - **Direct ties:** you ↔ your friend.
  - **Indirect ties:** you ↔ your friend's cousin via your friend; can include flows of goods, services, money, ideas, even ancient Greek philosophy across centuries.
- **"Six degrees of separation":** everyone is theoretically connected to everyone else within about six steps (100 friends → 10,000 friends of friends → 1 million at 3 degrees → 9 billion at 6).
- **Duncan Watts (*Six Degrees*, 2003):** network structure shapes action — e.g., we may change our vote if enough friends vote the other way. Network analysis helps explain fads, fashions, job information, and disease spread.

#### Winners, losers, and influence
- **Joseph Conti (WTO study):** the U.S. is involved in most trade disputes but usually loses; what matters is **centrality** — having the most ties — not winning/losing. Centrality itself is a source of powerful influence.

#### Jobs, inequality, and networks
- **Mark Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973):** weak ties — acquaintances connected to different networks — are crucial for job hunting. Homogeneous networks (same race, class, religion, etc.) limit access to new information.
- **Hoffman and Torres (2002):** women with more men in their networks hear about better job leads; women with more women in their networks hear about fewer quality jobs. Men's networks aren't affected the same way. Explanations: women are less likely to hear leads *and* may pass leads to men out of job insecurity.
- **Pedulla and Pager (2019):** Black and white applicants are equally likely to use networks and both benefit, **but white applicants benefit more**. Black candidates' networks are less likely to include someone at the target company and less likely to mobilize on their behalf → social networks can reproduce racial inequality.

#### Sexual health and networks
- **Christakis and Fowler:** all social networks have **connection**; **contagion** is what flows through social ties (obesity, smoking, substance abuse, STDs).
- STD example: people with many partners tend to have sex with others who have many partners, keeping STDs concentrated in "core groups."

#### Covid-19: testing, tracking, tracing
- Epidemiology is fundamentally about social networks. **Cornell study (Weeden and Cornwell 2020):** almost all undergraduates were separated by just **two other students**; campuses are "small worlds" → helped convince colleges to stay online in fall 2020.
- **Contact tracing** = interview infected people, trace contacts, notify them; repeat. U.S. public health departments were often overwhelmed and under-tested; South Korea did better using cell-phone data, cashless transactions, and CCTV (with more flexible privacy laws). Controlling for population, the U.S. had **47× more Covid cases** and **79× more deaths** than South Korea (Solano et al. 2020).
- Monitoring **central individuals** can provide advance warning of outbreaks.

## 2. Separate from Groups: Anomie or Virtual Membership?

- **Durkheim's insight:** group norms place **limits** on our individual desires. Without limits, we would endlessly chase unattainable things and become deeply unhappy.
- **Anomie** = normlessness; a feeling of being disconnected from group life. Durkheim believed group membership anchors us to the social world — **and keeps us alive**.
- Modern society's fragmentation worried Durkheim: rapid technological/cultural change could weaken social bonds, increase anomie, and even contribute to suicide.

### 📡 In the Future: What Happens to Group Ties in a Virtual World?
- **Virtual reality** (Oculus Rift, Facebook/Google/Sony/Microsoft) creates immersive 3D experiences; Zuckerberg imagined courtside seats, global classrooms, face-to-face doctor visits from home.
- **Potential benefits:** democratized access to arts/education/travel; empathy across cultures (UN VR film *Clouds over Sidra* in a Jordanian refugee camp); full-body interfaces can simulate touch.
- **Big questions:** Will VR level class-based access or replicate inequality? Will it bring us closer together or drive us further apart? Is doing something in VR the same as doing it in "meatspace"? How will VR affect classrooms, families, and relationships?

### The Putnam debate
- **Robert Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000):** Americans are less likely to bowl in leagues, join the PTA, Kiwanis, League of Women Voters, or play bridge — a decline in **civic engagement** and community bonds that keep anomie at bay.
- **Critics:** Putnam may romanticize the "good ol' days"; new technologies and online groups offer alternative ways to connect.
- **Digital-age worries:** the internet may make us lonelier, replacing deep face-to-face bonds with "broader but shallower" connections (Marche 2012). Even **Sherry Turkle** (2011, 2015), once an optimist, warns we prioritize technology over relationships.
- **Counter-evidence (Pew, Klinenberg, Fischer):** social media users may be *more* connected, trusting, and politically engaged; Americans are no more lonely or detached than before.
- **Virtual communities:** MMORPGs (World of Warcraft, Fortnite), online support groups, fan communities — technology offers new ways to belong.
- **Caveat:** social media as an *escape* from social pain can increase loneliness (Nowland, Necka, and Cacioppo 2017). During Covid lockdowns, staying in touch with **at least five close friends/family** reduced the loneliness increase; face-to-face was still better than mediated contact (Kovacs et al. 2021).
- **Historical echo:** Durkheim feared the Industrial Revolution would disconnect people; today we have the same fears about the Digital Age. Rapid technological change induces anxiety.

## 3. Group Dynamics

- **Group dynamics** = how groups form, change, disintegrate, achieve great goals, or commit horrendous wrongs. Key questions: How do groups affect the self? What binds members? How do groups influence members? When do groups excel? What makes leaders? When are groups destructive?

### Dyads, triads, and more
- **Dyad** (2 people): intense but **unstable** — if one person leaves, the group is gone.
- **Triad** (3 people): more stable because the third can **mediate conflict**.
- **Larger groups:** as people are added, not everyone can know everyone personally → groups become more **formal, rule-based, and hierarchical**.
- **Axiom:** smaller groups = more personal ties; larger groups = more rules and regulations.

### In-groups and out-groups
- **In-group** = we identify with it and feel loyalty; **out-group** = we feel opposition, rivalry, or hostility toward it.
- **Us vs. them intensifies loyalty and cohesion**, but also fuels prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination (class, race, gender, sexuality, religion, politics).
- **Robert Merton (1968):** the same behavior is positive in "us" and negative in "them" — *we* are laid-back, *they* are lazy; *we* are classy, *they* are snobbish; *we* are devout, *they* are zealots.
- At best, in/out dynamics produce solidarity; at worst, they enable slavery and genocide.
- **Rites of passage** transform outsiders into insiders. Hazing is an example; **degradation ceremonies** (Garfinkel 1956) strip old identity — military recruits get identical haircuts, uniforms, and demeaning nicknames ("plebe," "sprog," "yardbird") before earning group respect.

### Reference groups
- A group that provides **standards for self-evaluation** — peers, professional groups, celebrities, fictional characters.
- We compare ourselves to reference groups to ask: Am I good enough? Smart enough? Fast enough?
- Reference groups can be groups we **aspire to join** but don't yet belong to (future nurses/lawyers looking at their professions).

> **📋 Data Workshop — Analyzing Everyday Life: The Twenty Statements Test: Who Am I?**
> **TST** (Kuhn and McPartland 1954) measures how much self-concept is based on group membership.
> - Write **20 responses** to "Who am I?" in 5 minutes.
> - **Score into 4 modes:**
>   - **A-mode:** physical characteristics ("I am short," "I am blonde").
>   - **B-mode:** socially defined roles/statuses tied to group membership ("I am a student," "I am a daughter," "I am Catholic").

### Group cohesion
- **Group cohesion** = solidarity/team spirit; the force that binds members together. At least a minimum level is necessary for group survival.
- **Enhanced by:** shared values; shared demographics (race, age, gender, class); attraction to the group; successful cooperation toward goals (cliques, congregations, Packers fans, Elks lodges).

#### Groupthink
- **(Irving Janis 1971, 1982):** highly cohesive groups may demand **absolute conformity** and punish dissent → desire for unanimity overrides critical reasoning.
- Groups may feel invulnerable and morally superior; members suppress doubts.
- **Lincoln's "team of rivals"** = an antidote: advisers diverse enough to challenge groupthink.
- **Examples:**
  - **NASA Challenger explosion (1986):** scientists failed to take warnings seriously.
  - **Iraq War (2003):** Senate Intelligence Committee blamed groupthink for exaggerated WMD claims.
  - **2016 election:** media/polling groupthink predicted a Clinton win.
  - **Trump administration:** high turnover replaced staffers with those who agreed with the president (Lu and Yourish 2019).

## 4. Social Influence (Peer Pressure)

- **Social influence** = group impact on individual decisions. We conform to gain **positive sanctions** (approval) and avoid **negative sanctions** (rejection).
- We follow **prescriptions** (things we're supposed to do) and avoid **proscriptions** (things we're not supposed to do).
- Social influence affects school performance, brand loyalty, victim blaming, and much more.

### Three kinds of conformity (Kelman 1958)
1. **Compliance** — mildest; go along for rewards/avoid punishments; beliefs don't change (court-ordered AA attendee who doesn't buy in).
2. **Identification** — conform to establish/maintain a relationship; identify with the group and follow its behavior (AA attendee begins to identify and keeps coming).
3. **Internalization** — strongest; adopt the group's beliefs as your own; belief and behavior merge (AA member makes the 12 steps part of identity).

### Experiments in conformity

#### The Asch experiment (1951)
- Groups of 7–8 "students" judged line lengths; only one was a real subject; confederates gave unanimous wrong answers.
- **Results:** ~33% were "yielders" (gave in at least half the time); 40% yielded less often; only **25% were independents** who never caved.
- Subjects were distressed by the discrepancy — some assumed the group was right; others knew they were wrong but didn't want to appear different.
- **Lesson:** resisting peer pressure is hard, even when the evidence is clear.

#### The Milgram experiment (1961–63)
- Inspired by the Eichmann trial and "I was only following orders."
- Setup: "teacher" (real subject) was told to administer increasing electric shocks to a "learner" (confederate) for wrong answers.
- **Result:** **65% administered the maximum 450 volts**, despite hearing the learner's protests, banging, and silence; only one refused outright at low voltage.
- **Lesson:** ordinary people often obey authority, especially when they feel someone else is responsible. Milgram and colleagues were stunned — they expected only a few to comply.

#### The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)
- **Philip Zimbardo** (Milgram's high school classmate) randomly assigned psychologically healthy students to be guards or prisoners in a mock jail.
- Students quickly inhabited roles: guards became sadistic; prisoners showed trauma; the experiment was shut down after **six days**.
- **Lesson:** **situational dynamics**, not individual personalities, can determine behavior — the power of roles and authority.

#### Milgram revisited
- Some argue Asch/Milgram were products of a more conformist era; later Asch replications found **lower compliance** (Perrin and Spencer 1980, 1981; Bond and Sussex 1996) — history/culture matter.
- **Jerry Burger (2009)** created an ethical modified Milgram replication: obedience rates were **only slightly lower** than the original → authority still powerfully shapes behavior.
- **Modern relevance:** Abu Ghraib abuses; QAnon and the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack show people following anonymous "authority" into destructive action.

### 💞 In Relationships: Social Networking — You're Not the Customer, You're the Product
- Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, Instagram are among the most visited sites in the U.S.; Facebook alone has ~1.5 billion daily active users.
- **The aphorism:** "When something online is free, you're not the customer, you're the product."
- **Facebook emotional contagion experiment (2014):** Facebook and Cornell tweaked newsfeed algorithms for ~700,000 unwitting users, showing slightly more positive or negative posts; users exposed to happy content posted more positive words; those exposed to sad content posted more negative words.
- **Ethical uproar:** experimenting without consent; potential emotional harm; Facebook apologized.
- **OkCupid's Christian Rudder** defended such experiments: "If you use the Internet, you're the subject of hundreds of experiments at any given time." OkCupid told 30% matches they were 90% matches and found conversations increased.
- **Question:** what does it mean to give for-profit corporations so much power over our social networks?

## 5. Working Together: Teams and Leadership

### Teamwork
- Early research assumed groups were always more productive than individuals; later research showed **task type and group characteristics matter**.
- Groups usually outperform single individuals in total productivity, but **not necessarily** compared with the same number of people working alone.

#### Steiner's potential vs. actual productivity
- **(Ivan Steiner 1972):** actual group productivity can **never equal potential productivity** — there are always **process losses**:
  1. **Coordination/organization** — deciding who does what, delegating, structuring (worse as groups grow).
  2. **Social loafing** — individuals contribute less as group size increases because individual effort becomes harder to see.
- **Solutions:** recognize individual effort, make tasks interesting/rewarding, build strong **social identity** (feeling like a representative of the group, not just a member).
- **Amazon's "two-pizza rule"** (Jeff Bezos): teams too big to be fed by two pizzas are too large — too much miscommunication and bureaucracy.

### 💼 On the Job: Teamwork and the Tour de France
- The Tour de France is a 2,000+ mile, three-week race; historically plagued by doping scandals (Lance Armstrong stripped of seven titles).
- **No one wins the Tour alone:** winning riders are supported by eight teammates with specialized roles — sprinters, super-climbers, rouleurs, and riders with *combativité* — who block, draft, organize food/bathroom breaks, and sacrifice their own chances.
- **Lesson:** individual success is almost always buoyed by group effort — presidents, Oscar winners, Everest climbers, restaurant owners, and college graduates all rely on others.

### Power, authority, and style
- **Power** = ability to control the actions of others. Two forms:
  - **Coercive power** — backed by threat of force.
  - **Influential power** — supported by persuasion.
- **Max Weber's three types of authority:**
  1. **Traditional authority** — custom, birthright, divine right (monarchies/dynasties).
  2. **Legal-rational authority** — laws, rules, procedures (modern presidencies/parliaments).
  3. **Charismatic authority** — remarkable personal qualities; can defy rules/traditions.
  - Types can coexist (Barack Obama = legal-rational + charisma; King Hussein = traditional + charisma).
- **Leadership styles:**
  - **Instrumental** — task/goal-oriented ("get it done").
  - **Expressive** — relationship/harmony-oriented (emotional needs of members).
  - **Gendered expectations:** men are expected to be instrumental, women expressive. Biden's empathy is sometimes read as "weak"; AOC's instrumental drive is sometimes read as "pushy" — showing how leadership styles are judged through gender.

## 6. Bureaucracy

- **Bureaucracy** = a specific type of secondary group designed to perform tasks efficiently — universities, employers, internet providers, fast-food restaurants, churches.
- As groups grow bigger, they necessarily become more structured, hierarchical, and bureaucratic.

### Weber's six characteristics of bureaucracy
1. **Specialization** — assigned roles and tasks.
2. **Technical competence** — members are trained/qualified for their roles.
3. **Hierarchy** — supervision of subordinates by higher-ranking managers.
4. **Rules and regulations** — make operations predictable.
5. **Impersonality** — rules come before people; no special treatment.
6. **Formal written communication** — memos, emails, documents.

- **University example:** professors can't teach each other's subjects; groundskeepers, police, coaches, librarians each have specialized roles; layers of hierarchy from trustees to department chairs; regulations can block what you want to do; impersonality means you're treated "like a number" — because to the college, you *are* a student ID number.

### The McDonaldization of society
- **(George Ritzer 1993):** bureaucratic **rationalization** is spreading beyond organizations into everyday life — education, health care, banking, food, entertainment.
- **McDonald's model:** efficiency, calculability, predictability, control through nonhuman technology; workers' moves monitored; outcomes standardized.
- **Ritzer's later update (Ritzer and Miles 2019):** **Amazon is now more McDonaldized than McDonald's** — eliminating the store, self-service shopping, employees only for warehouse/delivery.
- **Irrationality of rationality:** hyperrational systems produce illogical results — obesity, environmental degradation, overconsumption, worker exploitation, compulsive shopping.
- **Weber's "iron cage"** = the way bureaucracy can trap individuals in dehumanizing rational systems.

### Responding to bureaucratic constraints
- **Bureaucracies are necessary:** tap water, electricity, roads, cars, insurance, food, clothing, media — all produced/distributed by bureaucracies.
- **Humanizing strategies:**
  - **Primary relationships inside bureaucracies** — coworkers celebrating birthdays, parties, after-work drinks.
  - **"Enlightened bureaucracies"** — Apple, Toyota, Google try to be inclusive, share responsibility, and create advancement opportunities.
  - **Google's People Operations:** data-driven HR found women weren't being hired (often interviewed by men) and new mothers were leaving at twice the average rate; they fixed the hiring process and increased maternity leave.
  - **Kaizen** (Japanese management technique): lower-level workers suggest improvements; managers implement them — used by Fidelity Financial.
- **Takeaway:** corporations are still chasing productivity and cost-cutting, but individual needs and organizational needs are **not necessarily mutually exclusive**.

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Group vs. Individual Norms — Honor Killings
- **Honor killing** = murder of a family member (usually female) believed to have brought dishonor to the family/community; common justifications: refusing arranged marriage, being a rape victim, dressing/acting immodestly, sex outside marriage.
- **Contrast with U.S. case:** Drew Peterson killed his ex-wife Kathleen Savio (and likely his fourth wife Stacy) for personal motives → Americans see family murder as a betrayal of family loyalty.
- In honor-killing cultures, the killer is seen as a **norm enforcer**, not a norm violator; the victim is seen as deserving death for dishonoring the family.
- **Scale:** UN estimates up to **5,000 women/year** worldwide; researchers think the true number is much higher; ~1,000/year in Pakistan alone (Human Rights Watch 2019). Correlated with low social resources and gender inequality.
- **Case:** **Qandeel Baloch** (2016), Pakistani social media star, was drugged and strangled by her brother, who said she brought dishonor through "shameful" Facebook photos. The brother was convicted and sentenced to life — a rare outcome.
- **Lesson:** the same family-loyalty value that makes Americans disgusted by Peterson can, in a different cultural context, justify honor killing — showing how **group norms define individual behavior and moral judgment**.

## 7. Closing Comments

- **Groups make life possible:** they provide necessities — food, clothes, homes, cars, and everything we use daily.
- **Groups make life enjoyable:** companionship, recreation, friends, families, entertainment.
- **Groups make life problematic:** bureaucracies squelch individuality; organizations create social/environmental problems; in/out-group dynamics breed conflict and prejudice.
- **We are at our best in groups and our worst.** Groups enable great achievements *and* horrible acts.
- **Sociology's job:** understand group life at both extremes and everywhere in between.

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Group ≠ crowd ≠ category:** groups have **identity + ongoing relations**; crowds/aggregates just share space; categories just share an attribute.
- **Primary = emotional core** (family, close friends); **secondary = task/impersonal** (class, coworkers). But real groups blur the line.
- **Social networks:** direct ties → indirect ties → "six degrees"; **weak ties** = job info; **centrality** = influence; networks spread **both opportunities and contagion**.
- **Anomie = normlessness** (Durkheim): too little group constraint → disconnection. Putnam says civic groups are declining; others say the internet creates new communities.
- **Group size:** **dyad** (intense, unstable) → **triad** (stable, mediator) → bigger = more rules, less personal.
- **In-group/out-group:** "us vs. them" creates solidarity *and* prejudice; Merton's double standard — *we* are laid-back, *they* are lazy.
- **Reference group = measuring stick**; TST: **B-mode** = institutional/group-based self; **C-mode** = impulsive/individual self.
- **Groupthink = conformity over critical thinking** (Challenger, Iraq War, 2016 media); fix = "team of rivals."
- **Conformity ladder:** **compliance** (rewards/punishments) → **identification** (relationship) → **internalization** (belief).
- **Classic experiments:** Asch (wrong answers to fit in), Milgram (65% to 450V), Zimbardo (guards/prisoners). Situations can override personality.
- **Free online = you are the product** — Facebook emotional contagion experiment; OkCupid match deception.
- **Teams:** groups outperform individuals but never reach **potential productivity**; **social loafing** = less effort as group grows; **social identity** = fix.
- **Leadership:** **power** (coercive/influential) + **authority** (traditional/legal-rational/charismatic) + **style** (instrumental/expressive); gendered judgments of style.
- **Bureaucracy (Weber's 6):** specialization, technical competence, hierarchy, rules, impersonality, formal written communication.
- **McDonaldization = efficiency + dehumanization**; Amazon is the new McDonald's.
- **Honor killing** shows **group norms can override individual life** — same family value can look like betrayal in one culture and justice in another.
- **Bottom line:** groups make life possible, enjoyable, *and* problematic — sociology helps us understand all of it.
