# Individual and Institutional Discrimination, and Cultural Appropriation

Individual discrimination is the action of a single person. For instance, a landlord refusing to rent an apartment to a Black applicant is individual discrimination. It is usually personal and face-to-face since it is done by a specific person acting on their own beliefs. On the other hand, institutional discrimination is embedded in the policies and norms of social institutions and it exists without hostile intentions of individuals. For instance, the Federal Housing Administration and private banks used to refuse to insure or issue mortgages in non-white neighborhoods, systematically locking Black families out of homeownership. In this case, no single federal employee had to consciously reject Black buyers for the unequal pattern to emerge, as the discrimination was built into the structure. 

Cultural appropriation is when members of a dominant group adopt elements of a minority group's culture, in aspects including clothing, hairstyles, music, religious symbols, language, but in a way that strips them of their meaning and repackages them as fashion, entertainment, or profit. The defining feature is that appropriation flows only one direction, from a group that has been marginalized to the group that marginalizes it. Its effects are harmful in several ways. First, the dominant group profits from a culture while the minority that created it stays marginalized and penalized for those same elements. For instance, cornrows and dreadlocks are treated as edgy style when white celebrities wear them, yet Black employees have been fired or told to change their hair for wearing the same hairstyles in the workplace. Second, appropriation flattens a culture into a stereotype, replacing its depth with a caricature. For instance, sacred items could become accessories. Third, the minority group becomes disowned from its own culture, as its traditions are not accepted by dominate culture until those same traditions are repackaged by outsiders.
