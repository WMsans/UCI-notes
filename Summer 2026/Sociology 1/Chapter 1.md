# Chapter 1: Sociology and the Real World
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term                         | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sociology**                | The systematic or scientific study of human society and social behavior — from large-scale institutions and mass culture to small groups and individual interactions. (Latin *socius* + Greek *logos* = study of society) |
| **Society**                  | A group of people who shape their lives in aggregated and patterned ways that distinguish their group from others.                                                                                                        |
| **Social sciences**          | The disciplines that use the [[Science\|scientific method]] to examine the social world (anthropology, psychology, economics, political science, history, geography, communication).                                      |
| **Sociological perspective** | A way of looking at the world through a sociological lens; thinking sociologically.                                                                                                                                       |
| **Beginner's mind**          | Approaching the world without preconceptions in order to see things in a new way.                                                                                                                                         |
| **Culture shock**            | A sense of disorientation that occurs when entering a radically new social or cultural environment.                                                                                                                       |
| **Sociological imagination** | A quality of the mind that allows us to understand the relationship between our **individual circumstances and larger social forces** — the "intersection between biography and history."                                 |
| **Microsociology**           | Level of analysis studying face-to-face and small-group interactions to understand how they affect larger patterns/structures of society.                                                                                 |
| **Macrosociology**           | Level of analysis studying large-scale social structures to determine how they affect the lives of groups and individuals.                                                                                                |
| **Theories**                 | Abstract propositions that explain the social world and make predictions about the future.                                                                                                                                |
| **Paradigm**                 | A set of assumptions, theories, and perspectives that makes up a way of understanding social reality.                                                                                                                     |
| **Positivism**               | The theory that sense perceptions are the only valid source of knowledge (Comte's "social physics"). See [[Science]].                                                                                                     |
| **Social Darwinism**         | Application of evolutionary theory and "survival of the fittest" to the study of society (Spencer).                                                                                                                       |

## 1. What Is Sociology?

### Definitions
- **Howard Becker (1986):** sociology = the study of people **"doing things together"** — neither society nor the individual exists in isolation; humans are essentially social beings.
- Our sense of self derives from membership in society; the accumulated activities people do together create the patterns and structures we call society.
- Sociologists study **how humans affect society AND how society affects humans** (both directions).

### Sociology vs. other social sciences (Figure 1.1)
| Compared to… | Sociology differs because it… |
|---|---|
| History | focuses on **contemporary** society (though both compare past & present) |
| Anthropology | looks at societies at **all levels** of development (anthropology: traditional/small indigenous cultures) |
| Economics / Political Science | covers a **range of social institutions**, not just one |
| Geography | cares about the **relationship of people to places** (geography: the places themselves) |
| Communication studies | examines communication at **both** social and interpersonal levels |
| Psychology | looks at the individual in relationship to **external social forces** (psychology: internal states of mind) |

> **Takeaway:** Sociology is exceptional among social sciences for its **comprehensive, integrative approach**.

## 2. How to Think Like a Sociologist

### Practical vs. Scientific knowledge (Schutz 1962) — see [[Science]]
- **Everyday actors** use "reciped"/practical knowledge — works for getting along day-to-day, but not necessarily coherent, clear, or consistent. (You can use a smartphone but can't explain how it works technically.)
- **Social analysts** must "place in question everything that seems unquestionable" (Schutz 1962, p. 96) — take the perspective of a **stranger** in the social world and *verify* what the everyday actor accepts as truth.
- **Both have strengths:** the analyst sees clearly what the actor glosses over; the actor understands implicitly what the analyst labors to grasp. Best result: combine both.

### Beginner's Mind (Bernard McGrane 1994)
- Borrowed from the Zen Buddhist tradition; the **opposite of expert's mind** (so filled with facts, assumptions, and opinions it can't learn anything new).
- Must **unlearn what we already know**; approach the world open and receptive.
- > "Discovery is not the seeing of a new thing — but rather a new way of seeing things" (1994, p. 3).
- Inner stillness → personal **"paradigm shift"** (a new model for understanding self and society).

**📋 Data Workshop — "Doing Nothing":** Stand in a busy public place and do nothing for 10 minutes (no phone, no daydreaming, nothing). Observe others' reactions and your own thoughts. Turns the ordinary world strange; shows how **identity is constructed through interaction** and how situation-meaning gets defined.

### Culture Shock (Peter Berger 1963)
- The sociologist is intense, curious, daring. > "People who like to avoid shocking discoveries… should stay away from sociology" (p. 24).
- Create culture shock *without traveling*: become **curious visitors to our own lives** — the familiar is just as exotic as a foreign culture, only we've forgotten.
- Radical nature of sociology: suspend preconceptions, question everything, especially our own taken-for-granted notions.

### The Sociological Imagination (C. Wright Mills 1959) 
Reference [[The Sociological Imagination]] notes. 
- A quality of mind all great social analysts possess: understanding the **intersection between biography and history** — the interplay of the micro world of self/psychology and the macro world of larger social forces. "This is sociology's task and its promise."
- We wrongly see our problems as private matters of character/chance; they may be caused by (or occur within) a specific cultural-historical context.
  - *Example:* Can't find a job → feels personal, but may result from outsourcing, downsizing, restrictive policies, technology changes, migration.
  - *Credit card debt:* psychological explanation = lack of self-control; **sociological explanation** = cultural norms promoting lifestyles beyond means + wages not keeping up with inflation.

### 💡 In Relationships: "It's Official: Men Talk More Than Women"
- **Common-sense stereotype debunked:** studies (Leaper & Ayres 2007) show men are actually slightly *more* talkative overall.
- **Context matters:**
  - Men talk more: with wives, with strangers
  - Women talk more: with children, with college classmates
  - Equal: with close friends and family
- Men use more **assertive** speech (persuasion); women use more **affiliative** speech (connecting).
- Men are more **dominant** in conversations — interrupt/cut off women more often.
- **Mansplaining** (Solnit 2008): explaining condescendingly with presumption of superior knowledge; reinforces gender stereotypes about power; in the OED + French (*mecspliquer*), German (*herrklären*), Icelandic (*hrútskýring*).
- **Conclusion:** talkativeness varies by situation → language differences influenced more by **social forces than biology**, including power dynamics. (Example: Pence interrupted Harris 10x in the VP debate vs. 2–4x the reverse.)

## 3. Levels of Analysis: Micro- and Macrosociology

- **Photographer analogy (Newman 2000):** zoom lens = micro, wide-angle lens = macro; both photograph the same subject.
- **Microsociology** — studies smallest building blocks of society to understand large-scale structure.
  - *Classic study:* Victoria Leto DeFrancisco, **"The Sounds of Silence: How Men Silence Women in Marital Relations" (1991)** — recorded couples' home conversations; husbands withheld "supportive responses" ("mm-hmm," "oh") for 4–8 seconds, even walking out mid-conversation → saw **gender and power (macro phenomena) manifested in everyday talk (micro level)**.
- **Macrosociology** — starts from large-scale structures to see how they affect groups/individuals.
  - *Classic study:* **Christine Williams** — "glass ceiling" (women in male-dominated fields hit limits on advancement) vs. **"glass escalator"** (men in female-dominated occupations experience unusually rapid upward mobility).
- **Different assumptions:** micro assumes larger structures are *shaped by* individual interactions; macro assumes structures *shape* those interactions. Think of them as a **continuum** — most sociologists sit somewhere in between.

## 4. Sociology's Family Tree & Roots

- Sociology has a "family tree" of real people living in particular times/places, related along intertwining lines.
- **Theories** = "approaches," "schools of thought," "paradigms," "perspectives" — guiding principles/abstract models that explain and predict the social world.
- **Early roots:** pre-disciplinary thinkers (philosophers, theologians, economists, historians, journalists) establishing **society as an object of scientific scrutiny** — itself revolutionary.

### 🌳 The Founders
| Person | Lifespan | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Auguste Comte** | 1798–1857 | French scientist; first program for the scientific study of society — **"social physics"**/positivism; coined "sociology"; 3 stages of human thinking (theological → metaphysical → positive/scientific); wanted positivist guidance toward social progress *and* order after the French Revolution's instability. |
| **Harriet Martineau** | 1802–1876 | English journalist/political economist; radical for her time (unions, abolition, suffrage); traveled to U.S. (1835), judged democracy on its *own* standards → *Society in America* (1837), *Retrospect of Western Travel* (1838); **translated Comte's *Introduction to Positive Philosophy* into English (1853)** — made his ideas accessible in England/America. |
| **Herbert Spencer** | 1820–1903 | Railway engineer turned journalist; established sociology in Britain/America; *First Principles* (1862) — societies evolve like biological organisms; coined **"survival of the fittest"** → **social Darwinism**; *The Study of Sociology* (1873), *The Principles of Sociology* (1897). |

## 5. Macrosociological Theory (classical sociology)

*Context:* Late-19th-century Europe — Industrial Revolution, French & American Revolutions → theorists tried to explain **social order, social change, and social inequality** on the grandest scale.

### A. Structural Functionalism
**Founder: Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)** — central figure; Jewish family, strong morality & work ethic; after Franco-Prussian War (1870–71) wanted applied science to stabilize France; became first professor of social science in France (Bordeaux 1887, later the Sorbonne).

**Durkheim's key works:**
- *The Division of Labor in Society* (1893): **solidarity** in all societies, but different types:
  - **Mechanical solidarity** — premodern/agrarian; bonds from shared traditions, beliefs, experiences
  - **Organic solidarity** — modern/industrial; bonds from difference, interdependence, individual rights
- *Suicide* (1897): used statistics to show suicide relates to **social factors** (religious affiliation, marital status, employment) — not just psychology; result of **anomie** ("normlessness," disconnection from modern life). Firm anchors (family, religion, workplace) → less anomie.
- *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912): religion = powerful source of social solidarity; universal definition via **sacred** (holy/divine) vs. **profane** (ordinary/mundane); practitioners "unite into one single moral community."

**Original principles (Comte + Spencer → Durkheim):**
1. Society = a **stable, ordered system** of interrelated parts (structures)
2. Each structure has a **function** that contributes to the stability/equilibrium of the whole
- Structures = social institutions (family, education, politics, economy, religion); dysfunction in one → change → new equilibrium (e.g., if families fail to discipline kids, schools/churches/courts pick up the slack).

**Offshoots:**
- **Talcott Parsons (1902–1979):** specified functions modern structures fulfill — adaptation, goal attainment, cohesion (e.g., family/school/religion socialize children; shared moral values provide cohesion).
- **Robert Merton (1910–2003):** **manifest functions** (obvious, intended) vs. **latent functions** (less obvious, unintended). *Education:* manifest = teach literacy, norms, values, laws; latent = keeps kids busy/out of trouble 8 hrs/day, 5 days/week, 12 years.

**Advantages & critiques:**
- ✅ Universal social theory; comprehensive model; brings order to a disorderly world.
- ❌ Conservative bias: only *dysfunction* creates change → can't easily explain civil rights, antiwar, women's liberation movements; static rather than dynamic model; little room for individual action.
- ❌ Inequality problem: if poverty/racism/sexism persist, they must serve a function. **Herbert Gans (1971)** reviewed the functions of poverty: the poor do "dirty work," provide a market for used goods, give the non-poor symbolic value (compassion for "deserving" poor, fear of "undeserving" poor).
- ❌ **Biggest problem: circular reasoning** — an institution's mere persistence shouldn't count as explanation for its existence.

### B. Conflict Theory
**Founder: Karl Marx (1818–1883)** — German political economist; "conflict theory" ≈ "Marxism" in social science usage. ⚠️ Note: the "communism" of Cuba/North Korea = totalitarian dictatorships, nothing like Marx's ideal; Marx's ideas remain a powerful analytic tool.

**Marx's key concepts:**
- Studied capitalism — private for-profit operation of industry; *Manifesto of the Communist Party* (1848); *Das Kapital* (ed. Engels, 1890).
- Society split into two classes over the **means of production** (anything creating wealth: money, property, factories, businesses):
  - **Bourgeoisie** = owners of the means of production
  - **Proletariat** = workers who must sell their labor power
- **Alienation** — workers' dissatisfaction producing goods owned/controlled by someone else.
- **Ideology** — ruling class's values permeate society, rationalizing the status quo.
- **False consciousness** — oppressed deny the truth; accept prevailing ideology (e.g., "Religion is the opiate of the masses" — a critique of using religion to focus workers on the afterlife rather than demanding better conditions).
- **Class consciousness** — recognition of inequality → revolutionary action; only path to change.
- **Praxis** — practical action; intellectuals should act on what they believe ("not just describe the world but change it"). Marxian ideas fueled 20th-century social movements (civil rights, antiwar, women's, gay rights, animal rights, environmentalism).

**Offshoots:**
- **Critical theory (Frankfurt School / neo-Marxism, 1930s–60s):** Adorno & Horkheimer coined **"culture industries"** (mass communications/pop culture as ideological tools); Marcuse warned consumerism → decline of personal freedom & democracy.
- **Critical race theory** (out of 1970s–80s legal scholarship): relationship among race, racism, and power; racism deeply embedded in American institutions (institutional racism) → perpetuates white privilege, marginalizes people of color; scholars also work to end oppression. (Chapter 8 will return.)
- **Feminist theory:** gender inequality across institutions (family, education, economy, media); Butler (1999), **bell hooks** (2003), MacKinnon (2005) link gender with race, class, sexual orientation. (Chapter 9 returns.)
- **Queer theory:** sexual categories (homo/hetero/bi/trans) = **social constructs**, not innate; no category is fundamentally deviant or normal; some reject strict categories entirely (Garber 1997). (Chapter 9 returns.)
- Paved way for study of race via **W.E.B. Du Bois** → civil rights movement.

**Advantages & critiques:**
- ✅ Praxis → real social change; explains macro discrimination *and* micro interactions (boss/employee); challenges the status quo.
- ❌ Overlooks society's genuinely orderly, stable, shared-value aspects.

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Eurocentrism and Sociological Theory
- Social theory is often **Eurocentric** — privileges European/Western histories, cultures, values; replicates global power hierarchies inside academia (an irony for scholars of inequality).
- **Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)** — Arab Muslim philosopher/politician, North Africa: *asabiyah* ("social cohesion") predates Durkheim by 500+ years; social/historical forces shaping individual lives predates Mills by ~600 years; called sociology *ilm al-umran* ("the science of civilization") — yet Comte gets the credit.
- Also overlooked: **Benoy Sarkar** (India, 1887–1949), **José Rizal** (Philippines, 1861–1896), **Kunio Yanagita** (Japan, 1875–1962).
- **Clarence Batan (2004, Philippines):** Western theories arose from Western-specific problems; calls for non-Western frameworks accounting for **postcolonial realities**.

### C. Weberian Theory
**Max Weber (1864–1920)** — German; studied law/history, worked as lawyer; breakdown after the 1897 fight with his father (who died a month later) → years unable to work; pessimistic about modern social forces.

**Key concepts:**
- **Rationalization** — application of economic logic to all spheres of human activity; efficient, goal-oriented, rule-governed **bureaucracies** (*Economy and Society*, 1921).
- **Iron cage** — *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904–05): people trapped by industriousness in a cage of bureaucratic rules; modern life = **disenchantment** (≈ Durkheim's anomie, Marx's alienation).
- **Verstehen** ("empathic understanding") — social scientists should study human action with scientific empathy for actors' experiences, intentions, meanings; researcher must avoid imposing own opinions.
- Bridge between macro theory and later micro theory: interested in how individual motivation → social action → society.

**Modern application:** **George Ritzer** — "McDonaldization" of society (bureaucracy/rationalization applied to fast food and beyond: education, law enforcement, globalization).

## 6. Microsociological Theory

### Symbolic Interactionism (America's contribution)
**Roots: Mead + the Chicago School (1920s–30s)**
- **University of Chicago** — first major American branch of sociology; dept. head **Albion Small** recruited Park, W. I. Thomas, Cooley, Mead, Blumer.
- Chicago = "frontier city" transformed by industrialization/immigration → laboratory for **field research** (interviews, observation) inspired by Weber's *verstehen*; micro focus (e.g., race relations in neighborhoods) → larger phenomena (racial inequality).
- **Pragmatism (William James, John Dewey):** truth = usefulness in everyday life ("if it works, it's true!"); adaptation is immediate and involves conscious thought (vs. social Darwinists).

**George Herbert Mead (1863–1931):**
- Mind, self, and society are **social processes** requiring interaction; **language** is crucial — no mind without language; language = product of social interaction.
- Meaningful human behavior = linguistic "gestures" (words, facial expressions); we use language to "name ourselves, think about ourselves… feel proud or ashamed of ourselves"; we act toward ourselves as we act toward others.

**Herbert Blumer (1900–1987):** named the theory "symbolic interactionism" after Mead's death (1931); taught at Chicago and Berkeley.

**Three tenets (Blumer 1969, p. 2):**
1. We act toward things on the basis of their **meanings** (a tree = shade or obstacle)
2. Meanings are **not inherent** — negotiated through interaction with others
3. Meanings can **change or be modified** through interaction (contractor persuaded to spare the neighbor's shade tree)

**Offshoots:**
- **Erving Goffman (1922–1982)** — self is "on loan" from society; created through interaction, ever-changing by context (first date vs. job interview vs. poker); **dramaturgy** = social life as theatrical performance, strategic self-presentation.
- **Harold Garfinkel — ethnomethodology** (1967): "folk methods" / everyday analysis of interaction; background knowledge is "seen but unnoticed"; shared understandings are precarious and take work to sustain.
- **Conversation analysis** (UCLA): meaning-making visible in naturally occurring conversation; scrutinize each conversational turn; larger social phenomena are constructed step-by-step through interaction.

**Key scholars of the era (associated with Chicago School values/methods, though not U of C faculty):**
- **W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963)** — first African American PhD from Harvard; professor at Atlanta University; groundbreaking research on slave trade, Reconstruction, ghettos, Black American society; **founding member of the NAACP (1909)**; targeted by Hoover/McCarthy as a communist (didn't join the party until age 93, as protest); emigrated to Ghana, died at 95 — one year before the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
- **Jane Addams (1860–1935)** — taught extension courses at U. of C. but never joined faculty (feared it would curtail activism); early proponent of **applied sociology**; founded **Hull House (1889)** — shelter, medical care, legal advice, training, education for immigrants/single mothers/the poor → often considered **founder of social work**; co-founded **ACLU** and (with Du Bois) **NAACP**; first American woman to win the **Nobel Peace Prize (1931)**.

**Advantages & critiques:**
- ✅ The only perspective assuming an **active, expressive model of the human actor**, treating individual and social at the same level of analysis; strong research-method legacy (ethnography, conversation analysis).
- ❌ Criticized (Fine 1993) as "apolitical… unscientific… limited to social psychology… astructural"; dubbed **"the loyal opposition"** (Mullins 1973). But a micro-macro détente has since emerged — all levels of analysis needed.

### 💼 On the Job: Famous Sociology Majors
- **Ronald Reagan**, **Saul Bellow** (Pulitzer & Nobel), **Martin Luther King Jr.** (civil rights leader)
- **Michelle Obama (b. 1964)** — first African American First Lady; BA in sociology from Princeton (senior thesis: alienation of Black students at an Ivy League school), law degree from Harvard; fought childhood obesity, helped working mothers & military families.
- **Kal Penn / Kalpen Modi (b. 1977)** — actor (Kumar in *Harold & Kumar*); served as associate director, White House Office of Public Engagement (2009); critical of racial stereotypes in media; memoir *You Can't Be Serious* (2021).
- **Amanda Gorman (b. 1998)** — National Youth Poet Laureate; Harvard sociology graduate (2020); wrote/recited "The Hill We Climb" after the Jan 6, 2021 Capitol riot; plans to run for president in 2036.
- >500,000 sociology BAs awarded in the U.S. between 1990 and 2018 (U.S. Dept. of Education 2019).

## 7. New Theoretical Approaches

### A. Postmodernism
- Developed from the French intellectual scene, late 20th century: **Jacques Derrida (1930–2004), Jean Baudrillard (1929–2007), Michel Foucault (1926–1984)** (they dislike the label).
- **Modernism** (reaction against): began with the Enlightenment/Age of Reason; values scientific knowledge, linear history, universal human nature.
- **Postmodernism:** no absolutes — no claims to truth, reason, order, stability; everything relative, fragmented, temporary, contingent; certainty is illusory.
- Critical of **"grand narratives"** (overarching stories/theories justifying dominant beliefs) → prefer **mini-narratives** (small-scale stories) combined into a "collage of meaning"; practice **deconstruction** — taking apart stories/theories (e.g., "factual" history is no more accurate than fiction).
- *Pop culture examples:* hip-hop (hybrid genre, sampling), mash-ups (DJ Danger Mouse's *Grey Album* = Beatles *White Album* + Jay-Z *Black Album*).
- **Critiques:** discards the scientific method; dismissed by conservative critics for rejecting moral standards; not widely practiced — but offers alternatives to consumerism/globalization and resonates with the Information Age.

### B. Midrange Theory (Robert Merton)
- Merton feared uncritical reverence for classical "**total sociological systems**" held sociology back; grand theories were hard to test practically ("many sociological products can… be considered both bad science and bad literature" — Boudon 1991).
- **Midrange ("middle range") theory** = a *style of theorizing*, not a theory of a specific thing — strikes a balance between micro and macro; integrates research questions + empirical data into smaller-scale theories that build cumulatively.
- Practitioners since the 1990s–2000s: **Annette Lareau** (social class & parenting, 2011), **Dalton Conley** (racial identity 2000; digital-age leisure 2009), **Peter Bearman** (public health; "vaccine refusers," 2010).

## Table 1.1: Theory in Everyday Life — College Admissions Case Study

| Perspective | Approach to Society | College admissions lens |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural Functionalism** | Society is a unified whole functioning through contributions of separate structures | Admitted = worthy/well-qualified; rejected = not deserving; there are other places in society for them |
| **Conflict Theory** | Social conflict is the basis of society and change; materialist, critical of status quo | Decisions made on criteria other than grades/scores — donor parents, athletic/musical talent; some denied for race, gender, sexuality |
| **Symbolic Interactionism** | Interaction and meaning are central; meanings created through interaction | All about self-presentation & meaning-making — how applicants present themselves, how the committee constructs the ideal applicant, how applicants interpret acceptances/rejections |
| **Postmodernism** | Social reality is diverse, pluralistic, constantly in flux | An acceptance doesn't mean you're smart, rejection doesn't mean you're stupid — beware of "facts," they're illusory and contingent |
