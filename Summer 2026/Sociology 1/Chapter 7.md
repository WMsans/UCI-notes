# Chapter 7: Social Class: The Structure of Inequality
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Social stratification** | The **division of society into groups arranged in a social hierarchy** — members categorized and ranked, with higher strata enjoying more access to rewards and resources. |
| **Social inequality** | The **unequal distribution of wealth, power, or prestige** among members of a society. |
| **Slavery** | The **most extreme form of social stratification**, based on the **ownership of people** as property; forced labor under threat, no rights. |
| **Caste system** | A form of social stratification in which status is determined by **one's family history and background and cannot be changed** — heredity, endogamy (marry within), no mobility. |
| **Apartheid** | The system of segregation of racial and ethnic groups **legal in South Africa between 1948 and 1991**; literally "apartness." |
| **Social class** | A system of stratification based on access to such resources as **wealth, property, power, and prestige**; practiced primarily in capitalist societies. |
| **Socioeconomic status (SES)** | A measure of an individual's place within a social class system; often used interchangeably with "class" — reminds us of the *social* as well as *economic* basis of ranking. |
| **Intersectionality** | (Crenshaw 1991) A concept that identifies how **different categories of inequality (race, class, gender, etc.) intersect** to shape the lives of individuals and groups — we experience class *and* race *and* gender simultaneously, not one at a time. |
| **Upper class** | An **elite and largely self-sustaining group** who possess most of the country's wealth. |
| **Upper-middle class** | Social class consisting of mostly **highly educated professionals and managers** who have considerable financial stability. |
| **Middle class** | Social class composed primarily of **white collar workers** with a broad range of education and incomes. |
| **White collar** | A description characterizing **lower-level professional and management workers** and some highly skilled laborers in technical jobs. |
| **Working class / lower-middle class** | Social class consisting of mostly **blue collar or service industry workers** who typically do not have a college degree. |
| **Blue collar** | A description characterizing **skilled and semiskilled workers who perform manual labor** or work in service or clerical jobs. |
| **Working poor** | Poorly educated **manual and service workers who may work full-time but remain near or below the poverty line**. |
| **Underclass** | The poorest group, comprising people who are **experiencing homelessness or who are chronically unemployed**. |
| **Status inconsistency** | A situation in which an individual holds **differing and contradictory levels of status** in terms of wealth, power, prestige, or other elements of SES (e.g., Sam Walton, Mother Teresa). |
| **Feudal system** | A system of social stratification based on a **hereditary nobility** who were responsible for and served by a lower stratum of forced laborers called **serfs**. |
| **Wealth** | A measure of **net worth** that includes income, property, and other assets. |
| **Prestige** | The **social honor people are given** because of their membership in well-regarded social groups (occupations are a common source). |
| **Social reproduction** | (Bourdieu) The tendency of social classes to **remain relatively stable as class status is passed down** from one generation to the next. |
| **Cultural capital** | (Bourdieu) The **tastes, habits, expectations, skills, knowledge, and other cultural assets** that help us gain advantages in society — inherited from parents, can be acquired. |
| **Everyday class consciousness** | **Awareness of one's own social status and that of others** — reading class clues in appearance, speech, possessions. |
| **Homogamy** | The tendency to choose romantic partners **similar to us** in terms of class, race, education, religion, and other social group membership ("like marries like"). |
| **Heterogamy** | The tendency to choose romantic partners **dissimilar to us** in terms of class, race, education, religion, and other social group membership. |
| **Hypergamy** | **Marrying "up"** in the social class hierarchy. |
| **Hypogamy** | **Marrying "down"** in the social class hierarchy. |
| **Social mobility** | The **movement of individuals or groups within the hierarchical system of social classes**. |
| **Closed system** | A social system with **very little opportunity to move from one class to another** (India's caste system). |
| **Open system** | A social system with **ample opportunities to move from one class to another** (the U.S. — at least in perception). |
| **Intergenerational mobility** | Movement between social classes that occurs **from one generation to the next** (child vs. parents' class). |
| **Intragenerational mobility** | Movement between social classes that occurs **during the course of an individual's lifetime**. |
| **Horizontal social mobility** | Movement **within a particular social class**, most often as a result of changing occupations (therapist → college professor). |
| **Vertical social mobility** | Movement **between different class statuses**; often called either **upward or downward** mobility. |
| **Structural mobility** | Changes in the social status of **large numbers of people as a result of structural changes in society** (Great Depression; dot-com boom). |
| **Relative deprivation** | A **relative measure of poverty** based on the standard of living in a particular society (a minimum-wage clerk vs. a neurosurgeon). |
| **Absolute deprivation** | An **objective measure of poverty**, defined by the **inability to meet minimal standards for food, shelter, clothing, or health care** (e.g., Eswatini/Swaziland, life expectancy <58 years). |
| **Federal poverty line** | Federal index that defines **"official" poverty in the U.S. based on household income**; updated annually. |
| **Culture of poverty** | (Lewis 1959) The idea that **entrenched attitudes can develop among poor communities** (fatalism, resignation, immediate gratification) and lead the poor to accept their fate rather than attempt to improve their lot — and are passed down to children. |
| **Just-world hypothesis** | (Lerner 1965, 1980) Argument that people have a deep need to see the world as **orderly, predictable, and fair**, which creates a tendency to **view victims of social injustice as deserving of their fates**. |
| **Residential segregation** | The **geographical separation of the poor from the rest of an area's population** — often along racial as well as socioeconomic lines. |
| **Disenfranchisement** | The **removal of the rights of citizenship** through economic, political, or legal means (the poor vote less and lack political clout). |
| **Wealth gap** | The **unequal distribution of assets across a population**. |
| **Digital divide** | The **unequal access to computer and internet technology**, both globally and within the United States — the "homework gap" during Covid remote learning. |
| **Meritocracy** | A system in which **rewards are distributed based on merit** (the American Dream's claim — which sociologists find contradicted by the evidence). |
| **Simplicity movement** | A loosely knit movement that **opposes consumerism** and encourages people to work less, earn less, and spend less, in accordance with nonmaterialistic values ("downshifting"). |
| **False consciousness** | (Marx) The **inability to see the ways in which we may be oppressed** — buying into the American Dream ideology without recognizing its structural flaws. |
| **Redlining** | (now illegal) Practice in which banks/lenders **identify high-risk areas (usually low-income or minority neighborhoods) and refuse mortgages or charge prohibitively high rates** — keeps the poor from building assets. |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: Hungry and Homeless College Students

- **The "broke college student" is not a cliché:** almost **half of students** struggle to find a stable living situation in college, and almost as many experience **food insecurity** (limited or uncertain access to food).
- **Sara Goldrick-Rab** and the **Wisconsin HOPE Center** ran the largest-ever studies of student food and housing insecurity ("Hungry and Homeless" surveys, 2017):
  - **Food insecure:** 36% of university students, **42% of community college students** (in the 30 days before the survey). ~1 in 3 CC students and 1 in 4 university students skipped or reduced meals for lack of money.
  - **Housing insecure:** 36% of university students, **51% of community college students** (trouble paying rent/utilities).
  - **Homeless:** 9% of university students, **12% of community college students** in the last year.
  - Higher for **women than men**, and for **LGBTQ students** vs. heterosexual students.
  - Basic-needs insecurity **hurts academics**: worse grades and lower graduation rates (Broton 2017).
- **Campus responses:** NIU's on-campus food pantry (started at a church, moved into the old field house); DePaul's emergency home-placement; Kennesaw State's year-round housing; Amarillo College's emergency aid center.
- **The point:** inequalities on campus are real and **reflect the inequalities of the larger society** — even in "the world's wealthiest country."
- **"How to Read This Chapter":** stratification exists in all human societies; America is profoundly hierarchical despite claims of equal opportunity. The sociological perspective helps you (1) see inequities you overlooked, (2) see hierarchies across institutions (health care, justice, employment, housing), (3) locate yourself in those arrangements, and (4) potentially help change systems of inequality.

> **Lesson:** Social class shapes life chances — including the chance to get a meal and a roof while pursuing an education.

## 1. Social Stratification and Social Inequality

- **Social stratification** = the division of society into groups arranged in a social hierarchy. Members are grouped by whatever criteria matter to that society (gender, race, class, age); higher strata enjoy **more access to rewards and resources**; the unequal distribution = **social inequality**.
- In the U.S., being **wealthy, white, heterosexual, or male** typically confers higher status than being poor, nonwhite, queer, or female.
- **Four basic principles of social stratification:**
  1. **A characteristic of society, not a reflection of individual differences** — in Japan men rank higher as a *group*, even though a particular woman (e.g., actress/singer Ryoko Hirosue) can outrank a particular man.
  2. **Persists over generations** — in Great Britain a child inherits regional accent along with race; wealthy families stay wealthy.
  3. **All societies stratify, but use different criteria** — industrialized nations: material wealth (social class); hunter-gatherer societies (e.g., the Khoisan Bushmen of southern Africa): gender.
  4. **Maintained through widely shared beliefs** — e.g., the myth that the poor failed to "pull themselves up by their bootstraps."

## 2. Systems of Stratification

### Slavery (most extreme)
- **People as property**, bought and sold, forced to work under mental or physical threat, no rights.
- Ancient and worldwide (biblical enslaved Israelites; ancient Greece/Rome where owners and enslaved shared race/nationality/religion). People became enslaved through: **debt, warfare, crime (punishment), or capture/kidnapping** (transatlantic slave trade).
- Usually for life, children inherited enslaved status; some systems were temporary (buying freedom).
- **Illegal and immoral everywhere today** (Universal Declaration of Human Rights) — yet **modern slavery persists**: child soldiers, serfdom, forced/bonded labor, human trafficking, sex slavery in South Asia, West Africa, and elsewhere.
- **U.S. complicity:** National Human Trafficking Hotline reported **11,500 cases / 22,000+ victims in 2019; >70% sexual slavery**; Americans buy products made with enslaved labor. **ILO/Walk Free Foundation: 40+ million people** in modern slavery.

### Caste
- **Heredity-based:** born into a stratum; no chance to change position regardless of individual achievement; marry within; ranking passes to children.
- **India:** five categories — **Brahman** (scholars/priests), **Ksatriya/Chhetri** (rulers/warriors), **Vaisya** (merchants/traders), **Sudra** (farmers/artisans/laborers), **Dalit** (outcasts/"untouchables"). Justified by **karma** — caste as reward/punishment for past lives; spiritual status with real-world inequality. Constitutionally prohibited since 1949, still prevalent.

#### The case of South Africa — apartheid (1948–1991)
- Legal separation of racial/ethnic groups; literally "**apartness**." Four legal groups: **white, Indian, "colored" (mixed race), Black** (60% of the population).
- Black people were forcibly removed from ~**80% of the country** (reserved for the three minority groups) into "homelands"; needed passes to work as "guest laborers"; African Americans got "**honorary white**" status. Schools, hospitals, buses, parks, beaches, libraries, graveyards — all segregated.
- **Resistance:** strikes, demonstrations, international sanctions; Steve Biko, Nelson Mandela; abolished 1991; democratic elections 1994.
- **Legacy:** still one of the world's most unequal countries — **top 10% own >90% of national wealth** and earn >55% of income; that stratum is almost exclusively white (8% of the population); **white people still own ~72% of individually held land**. New patterns of *class* stratification are replacing rather than erasing *racial* stratification. (Trevor Noah's memoir *Born a Crime* — born to a Black mother and white father under apartheid.)

#### 🇺🇸 The New Jim Crow (Michelle Alexander 2011)
- A **caste-like system in the U.S. runs through the criminal justice system**: discrimination by race is illegal, but discrimination against people with **criminal convictions** is allowed — felons lose voting and other citizenship rights even after release. Since **Black men are disproportionately imprisoned**, they bear the brunt of **felon disenfranchisement** and the stigma of the "ex-con."
- *"An extraordinary percentage of black men in the United States are legally barred from voting today…"* — instead of slavery or Jim Crow segregation laws, **discriminatory criminal laws and an unfair justice system enforce racial inequality** today. (Also covered in [[Chapter 6]].)

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Systems of Stratification around the World
- **Brazil — skin color:** intermarriage eliminated distinct racial groups, but **skin color still defines place in society**; light-skinned Brazilians hold wealth and power. Freyre's "racial democracy" (1970) was challenged (Telles 2004); critics call the inequality **racismo cordial ("polite racism")**.
- **Iran — religion:** before the 1979 Islamic Revolution, class (landowners/industrialists vs. entrepreneurs/civil service); after, **religious observance became the basis of stratification** — non-Muslims and Western-educated civil servants forced into exile or required to study Islamic law.
- **Sweden — government equalization:** deliberately lessens inequality with generous social services (health care, education, child/elderly care, unemployment benefits) funded by high taxes (**60% top rate**) — benefits: higher life expectancy/literacy, lower infant mortality, homelessness, poverty, crime.

### Social Class
- Stratification **based on wealth, property, power, and prestige**; also called **SES**. Less rigid than caste: children tend to "inherit" class but **can move up or down during a lifetime**. Not formally based on race, ethnicity, gender, or age — but there's overlap.

### 🔀 Intersectionality (Crenshaw 1991)
- We don't experience life as just "middle-class" — we are simultaneously classed, raced, gendered, aged, religious. **Life chances are shaped by all these categories together.**
- **Karyn R. Lacy (2007):** ethnography of **Black middle-class suburbanites** in the DC region — their identities were shaped by income, occupation, residential status, *and* race; they struggled to convince store employees, real estate agents, and bankers they belonged to the middle class. Race and class are experienced as **inseparable** in everyday life.

## 3. Social Classes in the United States

Think of classes along a **continuum**, not strict divisions (Gilbert 2014; Wright et al. 1982). Most Americans claim "middle class" regardless of reality.

| Class | % of pop. | Income (approx.) | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Upper class** | **1%** | >$1.5M/yr | Net worth **greater than the bottom 90% combined** (Wolff 2017). "Old money" (Rockefellers, Carnegies) vs. "new money" (Elon Musk, Cardi B). Wealth mostly from **investments**; tax deductions; private schools, prestigious universities; self-sustaining — few new members. |
| **Upper-middle class** | **14%** | ~$150k/yr | College/postgrad degrees; executives, managers, professionals (+ well-paid trades like plumbing/welding); some investment support but mostly **salaried work**; most financially stable; homeowners; value travel and higher education. |
| **Middle class** | **30%** | ~$70k/yr | White collar workers, tradespeople, skilled laborers, small entrepreneurs; high school + 2- or 4-year degree; traditionally homeowners — **the Great Recession and mortgage crisis changed that**; many can no longer afford homes. |
| **Working (lower-middle) class** | **30%** | ~$40k/yr | High school education; **blue collar manual labor + service industry** (retail, restaurant, tourism); routine jobs with little control; unions can lift some above average; low net worth, rental housing. |
| **Working poor** | **13%** | ~$25k/yr | Generally not high-school graduates; low literacy; unskilled/temporary/seasonal jobs (minimum wage, housekeeping, day labor, migrant farmwork); high unemployment/underemployment; some on welfare. |
| **Underclass** | **12%** | ≤$15k/yr | "Truly disadvantaged"; live in poverty; chronic difficulty meeting basic needs; depend on public benefits/charity; inner cities (growing in suburbs too); substandard housing or homelessness; officially impoverished. |

### Problematic categories: status inconsistency
- SES mixes income, wealth, education, power, prestige — so categories blur. **Bill Gates** (Harvard dropout) and **Richard Branson** (high school dropout) are billionaires; a novelist cleaning houses is highly educated but low-income.
- **Sam Walton** (Walmart founder): "Okie" farm boy, state college graduate → billionaire; called "America's shopkeeper"; stayed close to rural roots.
- **Mother Teresa**: occupational prestige as clergy, but vows of poverty — virtually no personal wealth; among the most admired people of the 20th century; Nobel Peace Prize.
- Both are examples of **status inconsistency** — stark contrasts between levels of different statuses.
- Status inconsistency is especially prevalent in the U.S. because of the "open" class system — though **class mobility is actually less attainable here than in many other countries** (Chetty et al. 2014b).

## 4. Theories of Social Class

### Conflict Theory (Marx)
- Nineteenth-century Europe: the **feudal system** (nobles vs. serfs) was breaking down as cities industrialized. Marx saw a new inequality between the **bourgeoisie** (capitalists who own the means of production) and the **proletariat** (workers who own only their labor).
- Economic relationships became the only ones that matter; wealth and privilege concentrate among capitalists; workers stay exploited; inequality grows.
- **Erik Olin Wright (1997):** chessboard metaphor — pawns try to overthrow the aristocracy but the "rules of the game" doom them to relive unequal roles: social structure shapes and sustains inequality.

### Weberian Theory
- Owning the means of production isn't the only path: **wealth = income + property** (Bezos $180B, Musk $166B, 2021). Three interrelated components:
  1. **Wealth** — can be inherited or earned.
  2. **Power** — the ability to impose one's will; comes from occupying roles in organizations; executives influence markets, consumers, employees even without owning the company.
  3. **Prestige** — social honor from membership in well-regarded groups; occupations rank it (Table 7.1: physician 86 at the top, janitor at the bottom; notably, **athletes (65) outrank sociologists (61)**). "Old money" is more prestigious in the U.S. than "new money."
- The three convert into each other: the **Kardashians** turned inherited wealth into celebrity prestige (social reproduction).

**Table 7.1 Prestige scores (NORC 2015):** Physician 86 · Lawyer 75 · Professor 74 · Architect 73 · Dentist 72 · Clergy 69 · Pharmacist 68 · Registered Nurse 66 · Athlete 65 · Electrical Engineer 64 · Veterinarian 62 · Airplane Pilot 61 · Sociologist 61 · Police Officer 60 · Actor 58 · Social Worker 53 · Firefighter 52 · Secretary 51 · Electrician 46 · Farmer 36 · Child-Care Worker 36 · Hairdresser 31 · Auto-Body Repairperson 29 · Cashier 28 · Waiter/Waitress 22 · Janitor (bottom).

### Structural Functionalism (Davis & Moore 1945)
- Stratification is **functional**: society needs incentives to fill the most important, most difficult roles. Some roles require more talent/training (doctors = 86 prestige, long education, internship) and are critical to society — so they get bigger rewards.
- **Critique:** is it really *functional* for rewards to be so unequally divided? Are doctors more important than teachers or carpenters? Pop-culture heroes (actors, athletes) outrank everyday heroes (day-care providers, firefighters). **Whose values structure the system?**

### Postmodernism: Bourdieu (1973, 1984) — Social Reproduction & Cultural Capital
- Studied French schools: social class is **passed down across generations** (**social reproduction**).
- Mechanism: **cultural capital** — children inherit tastes, habits, expectations, skills from parents (educated parents help with homework, expect credentials). Cultural capital also shapes **others' perceptions** (job interviews: socially skilled candidates win).
- People try to acquire it (adult ed classes, concerts, tennis clubs, European travel) — but childhood effects are powerful: **accent gives you away** ("talks like a hillbilly," "sounds too 'street'").

### Symbolic Interactionism
- Class statuses are **negotiated in everyday interaction**, not fixed:
  - **Sudnow (1972):** split-second judgments of who people are from appearance.
  - **Cicourel (1972):** we infer status in situations — the first-class "starving student" upgraded from coach, the "thrifty millionaires" in coach.
  - **Goffman (1956):** clothing, speech, gestures, cars, homes, friends, vacations are all **presentation of self** (see [[Chapter 4]]) — clues others read for SES.
- **Paul Fussell's "living room scale" (1983):** coffee-table *New York Review of Books* +5 pts; *Popular Mechanics* −5; working fireplace +4; wall unit with built-in TV/stereo −4; +3 per silver-framed B&W family photo; −3 for cowboy art. High score = high SES. (Dr. Ferris's living room features a silver-framed B&W photo of her father as a child — dressed as a cowboy on horseback. Real life defies easy categorization.)
- **Sarah Kristian (2018):** rural Newfoundland youth who aspired to leave spoke more standard English; those without aspirations used more rural-identified speech (dropping "g"s) — **aspirations shape interactional style**.
- **Everyday class consciousness** — we act on split-second evaluations (choosing not to sit next to someone "who didn't look quite right"); appearances can be deceiving but are **always consequential**.

> **📋 Data Workshop — Everyday Class Consciousness:** participant observation in a busy public place (airport, coffee shop, post office); sample every 7th or 10th passerby; guess class instantly; take field notes; analyze what clues you used (dress, shoes, logos, jewelry, car, accessories, voice) and what the consequences are. *Prep-Pair-Share* or a 3–4 page DIY essay.

## 5. Socioeconomic Status and Life Chances

Knowing someone's SES lets you predict their life chances in family, health, education, work, and crime.

### 👨‍👩‍👧 Family
- We tend to marry people like ourselves — not by preference, but because we have **more access to people like us** (classmates, co-workers, neighbors).
- **Cross-class marriage (Jessi Streib 2015):** conflicts over career advancement and child-rearing — higher-SES partners more deliberate/organized; lower-SES more "go with the flow"; but differences can also be the attraction, and lasting unions are possible.
- **Education shapes family:** college graduates marry **later** and divorce **less**; women with a master's+ have their first child at ~**30** vs. ~**24** for high-school graduates; less-educated women have more children.

### 🩺 Health
- Lower class → less adequate nutrition, shelter, clothing, health care; can't afford prevention, so they wait for crises and end up in **expensive emergency rooms**.
- **Covid-19:** social distancing/working from home were privileges of affluence — lower-income people live in higher-density settings, work in-person service jobs, lack remote technology (Yancy 2020; Reeves and Rothwell 2020).
- **Life expectancy gap (Chetty et al. 2016b):** for men, the **richest 1% live ~15 years longer** than the poorest 1%; for women, **~10 years**. Exercise is part of it — but exercise is a **luxury**: gym memberships, safe outdoor recreation, and free time (Ray 2017).

### 🎓 Education
- Schools are financed by **local property taxes**; district borders reflect **residential segregation by class and race** → schools unequally funded; low-income schools get fewer resources, fewer qualified teachers, less parental support.
- **Within the same school (Calarco 2018):** middle-class parents coach kids to **request accommodations** from teachers; poorer students are taught **not to bother teachers** and work independently — middle-class kids secure classroom advantages.
- **College gap:** 78% of top income-quartile students enroll in college vs. 48% of bottom-quartile (Cahalan et al. 2021). **Graduation gap is even bigger:** top-income young adults are **~5× more likely** to earn a BA by 24 (62% vs. 13%).
- **Anthony Jack (2019):** elite universities fail to support poor students *after* admission — "office hours" and "fellowship" are assumed knowledge; dorms/dining halls close over breaks; **admission is not the same as inclusion**.
- High school education doesn't mean what it once did — college/advanced degrees increasingly required for middle-class lifestyles, and children of lower SES risk slipping **down** the ladder.

### 💼 Work and Income
- **Top 1% captures ~20% of all income** (finance, management, medicine, law); **top 10% ~50%**; the other half of income goes to the bottom 90% (Saez 2020). The income gap has widened over the past decades.
- Crises hit lower-income workers hardest: Covid-19 → **~15% of workers lost jobs**, half still unemployed at end of 2020; some jobs may never return (unemployment/underemployment).
- **Working poor:** spend ≥ half the year in the labor force but stay below the poverty line (food service, housekeeping, maintenance).
- **"Precarious labor"** (Kalleberg and Vallas 2018): uncertain, unpredictable, unprotected work — contract, temp, part-time, and **gig work** (rideshare). Hit first by downturns; downsizing/outsourcing now threatens blue *and* white collar workers — even executives.

### ⚖️ Criminal Justice
- Lower SES → more contact with the criminal justice system, as perpetrator **or victim**; but the data are tricky: the poor are **more visible, less powerful, more likely to be apprehended and labeled**.
- **White collar criminals** (e.g., **Bernie Madoff's** Ponzi scheme) are less likely to be arrested/prosecuted/convicted, can afford the best lawyers, and get lighter sentences.
- **Death penalty:** **90% of death-row inmates couldn't afford a lawyer**; the *quality of representation* — not the facts — predicts death sentences (Gorn 2018); prosecutors seek the death penalty more in counties with larger Black populations, juries sentence Black defendants more (Beckett and Evans 2014). **Race × class intersect in sentencing** (Phillips 2009).
- **Victimization:** people in poor households are **more than twice as likely** to be victims of violent crime; poverty and violence share causes — unaddressed trauma and historical discrimination (Heartland Alliance).
- **Policing:** lower-income residents feel more threatened by police; on Skid Row, **Forrest Stuart** found "**therapeutic policing**" — officers arrest/ticket the homeless for minor offenses (jaywalking, loitering) as leverage into shelters/rehab, which the homeless experience as harassment.

### 💞 In Relationships: SES and Mate Selection
- **Homogamy** ("like marries like"): we choose partners similar in race, ethnicity, religion, class, education, age — even height and attractiveness (Kalmijn 1998). Class-based homogamy holds even when people are heterogamous on race or religion.
- **Why:** we simply have few opportunities to meet across class lines — school, work, gym, coffee shop are class-homogeneous.
- **Upper classes enforce it most strictly:** prestigious schools and posh summer camps keep privileged kids among themselves — consolidating wealth and power.
- **Famous examples:** Julie Nixon × David Eisenhower; Kerry Kennedy × Andrew Cuomo; Ryan Reynolds × Blake Lively; Beyoncé × Jay-Z; Tom Brady × Gisele Bündchen.
- **Dating apps cut both ways:** they can connect across social circles (inhibiting homogamy) — but niche sites (Jdate, The League, BlackPeopleMeet, TrekDating.com) **strengthen** homogamy.
- The "Cinderella story" (Sabrina, *Pretty Woman*) is fantasy — except for one sociological truth: when boundaries are crossed, **women practice hypergamy (marry up), men hypogamy (marry down)**.

## 6. Social Mobility

- **Social mobility** = movement within the stratification system. **Closed systems** (India's caste) restrict it; the U.S. is perceived as an **open system** — though pre–Civil War slavery and its vestiges contradict this.
- **Three kinds of movement:**
  1. **Intergenerational mobility** — child ends up in a different class than parents. Baby Boomers mostly moved **up**; but mobility has **stalled in the last 50 years**. **A child born in the bottom fifth of income has just a 9% chance of reaching the top fifth** (Chetty et al. 2014b).
     - **Geography matters:** San Jose, CA — **13%** chance; Charlotte, NC — **4%**. Five characteristics of high-mobility areas: **less residential segregation, less income inequality, better schools, greater social capital, greater family stability**.
     - **Mechanisms protecting the top:** tax laws and social policies let the wealthy protect and pass down assets — e.g., the **2019 college admissions scandal** (families bribing officials at Yale, Stanford, Georgetown).
  2. **Intragenerational mobility** — within one lifetime. Two directions: **horizontal** (job change within a class: therapist → college professor; far more common) and **vertical** (up or down: marrying a corporate president = up; unemployment = down).
  3. **Structural mobility** — large numbers move because society changes: **Great Depression** (1929 crash) pushed masses down; **dot-com boom** (late 1990s) made overnight millionaires.
- **Bottom line:** individual effort isn't the whole story — structure moves people too.

## 7. Poverty

### Defining poverty
- **Relative deprivation:** poor compared to others in your society (min-wage clerk vs. neurosurgeon).
- **Absolute deprivation:** cannot meet minimal standards of food, shelter, clothing, health care (Eswatini: >25% of adults living with HIV/AIDS; life expectancy <58 years).
- **Federal poverty line (2021):** **$26,500 for a family of four**; $21,960 (3 people); $17,420 (2); **$12,880 (individual)**. An *absolute* measure, updated annually.
- **Scale:** **10.5% of the population = 34 million people** below the line in 2019 (Semega et al. 2020); never below 10% in 40 years; rose above 15% in 2009 and 2011; as high as **22% in the late 1950s**; surged to ~**12% in fall 2020** (8 million more Americans during Covid).
- **Critiques of the poverty line:** uniform across regions (DC vs. Des Moines need 2–3× as much; only Alaska/Hawaii get exceptions); ignores non-cash assistance (**SNAP**, **EITC**); overemphasizes food costs from 1963 — housing has since soared. Many struggling working families are **invisible to official statistics**.
- **Who is poor (2019):** Black Americans **19%**, Hispanic Americans **16%**, Asian Americans **7%**, non-Hispanic whites **7%**; higher among people with disabilities, foreign-born, women, children, single-parent households; highest in the **South**; in inner cities, rural areas, and suburbs.
- **Myth-busting:** most poor people **work** — full-time at **$7.25/hr** still leaves a family below the line. At **no time in its ~80-year history** has the federal minimum wage lifted a worker with a dependent above poverty. 80%+ of low-income minimum-wage workers can't guarantee a decent standard of living.

### Social Welfare and Welfare Reform
- **The New Deal (FDR, Great Depression):** Social Security, unemployment insurance, TANF — a safety net for adversity, old age, poverty, joblessness.
- **The Great Society (JFK proposed, LBJ instituted 1964):** Medicaid, Head Start — a "war on poverty"; uniform standard benefits, no time limits. Success: Social Security/Medicaid lifted retirees and the medically needy out of poverty; Head Start and Upward Bound helped poor children; SNAP improved nutrition; **poverty fell from 22.2% (1970, from the 1950s high) to 12.6% — the fastest drop ever**.
- **The 1980s backlash:** critics blamed welfare for creating a "permanent underclass" living off checks. **Reality check on the budget:** only **8% of the 2019 federal budget ($361B)** went to safety net programs vs. Social Security ~23% ($1T) and defense ~16% ($697B).
- **Welfare Reform Act (Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act, Clinton, 1996):** ended "entitlements" — recipients must find work within **2 years**; **5-year lifetime limit**; states design their own programs.
- **Evidence:** moving from welfare to work **does not raise income** — assistance is replaced by an equally low-paying job; added costs (child care, health insurance, transportation) keep families below the line.
- **$2.00 a Day (Edin & Shaefer 2016):** welfare rolls down **75%+** since 1996, but **~1.5 million families (3 million children)** now live on almost nothing — no cash safety net when jobs can't be found. Coping: **plasma "donations" hit 32.5 million in 2014** (tripled since 2004); food pantry use nearly doubled (7 million families), then almost doubled again in 2020.

### 💼 On the Job: Minimum Wage or Living Wage?
- **Who earns minimum wage?** The average min-wage worker is **36 years old, female, working full-time** — not a teenager. **89% are at least 20; 37% are over 40**; concentrated in hospitality, retail, fast food.
- **Federal minimum wage: $7.25/hr since 2009** (~$1,256/month full-time); worth **$4 less in real dollars than in the late 1960s**. 2018: **7 million workers** were "working poor" (27+ weeks in the labor force, still below the line).
- **Living wage** = income for a *decent* standard of living (food, clothing, shelter + utilities, transportation, health care, education, retirement savings) — runs **50–150% above minimum wage** (~$11–$18). First ordinance: **Baltimore 1994**; hundreds of cities since (Seattle, St. Louis, Philadelphia, Chicago, Miami).
- **"Fight for $15"** is the largest campaign. Corporate responses: Walmart $10/hr (2016), McDonald's/Target/Gap, Costco & IKEA $12, **Amazon $15** (while phasing out stock options). In 2021, the $15 federal minimum wage was **stricken from Biden's Covid relief bill** to get it passed.

### The "Culture of Poverty" and Its Critics
- **Oscar Lewis (1959):** studying poor Hispanics in Mexico and the U.S., argued the excluded poor develop a distinct way of life — **fatalism, resignation, immediate gratification** — that's passed down and keeps them ill-equipped to change.
- **Critiques:**
  - It blames the **victims** and overlooks social conditions; implies eradicating poverty is pointless.
  - It feeds the **just-world hypothesis** (Lerner 1965, 1980): we need the world to be fair, so we decide victims "asked for it." Lerner's experiments: observers judged randomly chosen cash-prize winners as **more deserving and harder-working**; strong just-world believers feel less need to change society (Rubin and Peplau 1975).
  - It **lacks sociological imagination** (see [[Chapter 1]]): **Dalton Conley (2002)** — to solve poverty, examine **wealth**; laws that protect wealthy inheritances while giving working families few breaks reveal structural causes. Extreme wealth itself should be conceptualized as a social problem.

### The Invisibility of Poverty
34 million Americans below the poverty line — yet we rarely see them. Why?
- **Residential segregation:** geographical isolation of the poor — often racial ("wrong side of the tracks"): **public housing projects** (HUD-funded, high-density, poorly maintained, high-crime); neighbors resist integration; **redlining** — banks refusing mortgages to "high-risk" (read: minority/low-income) neighborhoods or charging prohibitive rates (illegal but still practiced) — blocks the asset-building that could lift people out.
- **Political disenfranchisement:** the poor vote less, feel unserved, lack clout — politicians court older adults, "soccer moms," small-business owners instead. **Counter-example:** Rio's favela residents (25% of the city) organized after the 2016 Olympics demolished homes without replacement — turning invisible communities into political forces.
- **The digital divide:** unequal access to computers/internet. **94% of adults earning $100k+ have home broadband vs. 56% of those under $30k**; >1/3 of lower-income households with school-age children lack high-speed internet — the **"homework gap"** became critical during Covid remote learning; internet proficiency is now a requirement for finding *and keeping* jobs.
- **Homelessness:** at least **2.5–3.5 million people (~1% of the population) experience homelessness each year**, plus 7.4 million doubled up; the Census undercounts (street homeless aren't counted). **Matthew Desmond's Eviction Lab:** ~**300,000 evictions** tracked in 5 states/27 cities from March–December 2020; the **CDC** used emergency powers in Sept 2020 to halt some evictions for public health. People avert their eyes out of discomfort and guilt — **John Coleman** went undercover as a homeless Manhattan man for 10 days: his accountant, landlord, and co-worker **looked right through him**; police shook him awake; a waiter made him pay upfront; other homeless men showed kindness.

### 📡 In the Future: Inequality and Global Warming
- **Andrew Sayer, *Why We Can't Afford the Rich* (2016):** extreme wealth is a social problem — and a planetary threat.
- Climate change's burdens **fall disproportionately on poorer regions**: hotter days hurt the poorer South (crop yields, labor productivity, mortality, crime) far more than wealthier New England/Pacific Northwest (Hsiang et al. 2017, *Science*) — global warming **intensifies regional inequality**.
- **The rich are super-consumers:** multiple homes (Oprah's five houses), fossil-fuel lifestyles (Jay Leno's 150 cars; Patrick Dempsey's 24-hour races; corporate jets). The wealthy can shield themselves — for a while. (Some help: Musk/Tesla.) A sustainable society requires **fairer distribution of economic resources**.

## 8. Inequality and the Ideology of the American Dream

- **The American Dream:** own a home, good marriage, good job, vacations, and the belief that **anyone can succeed if they work hard enough** — a poor kid can become president, astronaut, movie star.
- **It's an ideology**, not a description of reality: a belief system that **explains and justifies** the social class hierarchy (Hochschild 1996). It legitimizes stratification — success/failure becomes the individual's responsibility, inequality becomes "incentives and rewards for achievement."
- **Marx's false consciousness:** we buy into the ideology without seeing how we may be oppressed.
- **Oprah Winfrey** — born poor in Mississippi (1954), now worth **$2.6 billion**, Forbes' most powerful celebrity (2010), OWN network, philanthropist — is cited as proof of the Dream. But she's **the exception, not the rule**.
- **The evidence against meritocracy:** most people make little movement no matter how hard they work; mobility depends on **ethnicity, class, gender** rather than merit — whites experience more upward mobility than people of color (Chetty et al. 2018); married women more than unmarried (Thompson 2013); new immigrants move **down** in the first generation but **up** in the second (Papademetriou et al. 2009).
- **Consumerism and discontent:** chasing the Dream leaves us less secure and satisfied than previous generations — more debt, less free time (De Graaf, Waan, Naylor 2002); ~half of Americans say they don't have enough time (Gallup).
- **The simplicity movement:** backlash — "downshift": work less, earn less, spend less; grow vegetables, bike to work, secondhand clothes, more time with friends/family.
- **Freegans** (free + vegan; Barnard 2016): avoid consumerism entirely — **"urban foraging"/dumpster diving**, shared housing and transportation, minimal economic participation.

## 9. Closing Comments

- **Social stratification is all about power.** Stratification systems allocate wealth, political influence, and occupational prestige in **fundamentally unequal ways** — built into both the social structure and everyday interactions.
- Race/ethnicity and gender/sexuality stratification come in the next chapter — but we don't experience them separately: we are women or men, working class or upper class, Black or white, gay or straight **simultaneously**. Keep **intersectionality** in mind.

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Stratification = hierarchy + unequal access to rewards.** Four principles: societal (not individual), persistent across generations, criteria vary by society (wealth vs. gender), maintained by shared beliefs (bootstraps myth).
- **Three systems of stratification:** slavery (most extreme — people as property; still 40+ million today), caste (heredity, no mobility; India's five ranks; apartheid South Africa; *The New Jim Crow*), social class (wealth/property/power/prestige — the least rigid).
- **Global contrasts:** Brazil — *racismo cordial* (skin color); Iran — religion post-1979; Sweden — taxes/equalization (60% top rate).
- **U.S. class ladder:** Upper 1% (>$1.5M; net worth > bottom 90% combined) → Upper-middle 14% (~$150k) → Middle 30% (~$70k) → Working 30% (~$40k) → Working poor 13% (~$25k) → Underclass 12% (≤$15k). **Everyone says they're middle class.**
- **Status inconsistency:** Sam Walton, Mother Teresa, Gates/Branson — contradictory status levels; common because we think the system is "open."
- **Marx:** bourgeoisie own the means of production, proletariat own only their labor → conflict, exploitation, false consciousness.
- **Weber:** wealth + power + prestige — convertible (Kardashians: money → celebrity); prestige table: physician 86 at top, janitor at bottom, *athletes (65) outrank sociologists (61)*.
- **Davis & Moore:** inequality is functional — incentives fill important roles. *But whose values decide what's important?*
- **Bourdieu:** social reproduction via **cultural capital** — parents pass down tastes/habits/expectations; accent gives you away.
- **Symbolic interactionism:** class is *negotiated* — split-second reads (Sudnow, Cicourel), Goffman's presentation of self, Fussell's living room scale (NYRB +5, Popular Mechanics −5).
- **Life chances by SES:** family (homogamy; degrees → later marriage, less divorce), health (richest men live ~15 yrs longer), education (78% vs. 48% college enrollment; 62% vs. 13% BA completion), work (top 1% takes ~20% of income; precarious labor), justice (90% of death row couldn't afford a lawyer; poor 2× more likely crime victims).
- **Mate selection:** like marries like; women hypergamy, men hypogamy; Cinderella is fantasy.
- **Mobility:** intergenerational (9% bottom-fifth → top-fifth; San Jose 13% vs. Charlotte 4%), intragenerational (horizontal vs. vertical), structural (Depression vs. dot-com). U.S. is less mobile than we believe.
- **Poverty:** relative vs. absolute; 2021 line $26,500/family of 4; 10.5% = 34M Americans (2019), never below 10% in 40 years; min wage never lifted a worker with a dependent above the line.
- **Welfare history:** New Deal → Great Society (poverty 22.2% → 12.6%, fastest drop ever) → 1980s backlash → **Welfare Reform Act 1996** (2 years to work, 5-year cap) → *$2.00 a Day*: rolls −75% but 1.5M families on almost nothing.
- **Minimum wage vs. living wage:** $7.25/hr (since 2009; worth $4 less than the late 1960s); average worker is 36, female, full-time; Fight for $15; Amazon $15; Walmart $10.
- **Culture of poverty (Lewis):** fatalism + immediate gratification — *criticized*: blames victims, just-world hypothesis (Lerner), needs the sociological imagination (Conley: study wealth too).
- **Why poverty is invisible:** residential segregation + redlining, political disenfranchisement (favela activism), digital divide (94% vs. 56% broadband; homework gap), homelessness (2.5–3.5M/yr; Eviction Lab; John Coleman ignored on the street).
- **American Dream = ideology, not reality:** legitimizes inequality; false consciousness; Oprah is the exception; evidence: mobility depends on ethnicity/gender/class. Backlash: simplicity movement, freegans (dumpster diving).
- **Global warming is class inequality too:** the rich are super-consumers; climate burdens fall on the poor (Hsiang et al. 2017).
- **Bottom line:** stratification allocates power unequally — in the structure and in everyday life. Class, race, gender, sexuality are experienced **simultaneously** (intersectionality).
