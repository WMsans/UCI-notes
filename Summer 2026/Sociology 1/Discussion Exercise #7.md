# Gender Expression, Essentialism, and the Social Construction of Gender

Gender expression refers to the external, behavioral manifestations of an individual's gender, including the clothing, grooming, behavior, body language, and gestures through which gender is presented and read by others. Its purpose is to make gender identity visible in everyday interaction. It's interesting that people can barely interact with one another without first determining the other's gender. In other words, gender expression is the primary information we both interpret and signal during an interaction. Due to its behavioral nature of gender expression, it can also be changed by people. For instance, a transgender man may cut his hair short, adopt masculine clothing, and change his name so that his outward expression aligns with his gender identity. In this way, gender expression functions as one's presented self's coordination with one's authentic self.

Essentialist and constructivist arguments offer opposing accounts of where gender comes from. Essentialists maintain that gender is immutable and biological in origin. The biological factors including chromosomes, hormones, and genitalia determine identity produce an unambiguous two-category system known as the gender binary while the culture plays little to no role. This perspective is common in medicine, theology, and biology. On the other hand, constructivists argue that gender is a social construction shaped by culture and historical period. The biological factors are not the only way to classify gender. Instead, the meaning of masculinity and femininity differs across cultures. For instance, nearly 150 Native American cultures acknowledged "two-spirit" people as a third gender, the hijras of South Asia have been recognized for centuries, and families in Afghanistan have raised daughters as "bacha posh" boys out of demographic necessity. Since gender demonstrably varies across cultures and over time, it cannot be reduced to fixed biology. In other words, it is socially produced and maintained, which is precisely what the sociological lens examines.
