# Chapter 3: Culture
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term                       | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Culture**                | The **entire way of life of a group of people** (including both material and nonmaterial elements) that acts as a lens through which one views the world and that is passed from one generation to the next. |
| **Material culture**       | The objects associated with a cultural group — tools, machines, utensils, buildings, artwork; **any physical object to which we give social meaning**.                                                       |
| **Nonmaterial culture**    | The ideas associated with a cultural group — ways of thinking (beliefs, values, assumptions) and ways of behaving (norms, interactions, communication).                                                      |
| **Ethnocentrism**          | The principle of using **one's own culture as the standard** by which to evaluate another group or individual; leads to the view that other cultures are abnormal or inferior.                               |
| **Cultural relativism**    | The principle of understanding other cultures **on their own terms**, rather than judging or evaluating them according to one's own culture.                                                                 |
| **Culture shock**          | A sense of disorientation that occurs when entering a radically new social or cultural environment. (See [[Chapter 1]] — we can generate it without traveling.)                                              |
| **Signs**                  | Symbols that stand for or convey an idea (traffic signals, price tags, product logos, emojis).                                                                                                               |
| **Gestures**               | The ways in which people use their bodies to communicate without words; actions that have symbolic meaning ("body language," nonverbal communication).                                                       |
| **Language**               | A system of communication using vocal sounds, gestures, or written symbols; the **basis of nonmaterial culture** and the primary means through which we communicate and perpetuate culture.                  |
| **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** | The idea that **language structures thought** — ways of looking at the world are embedded in language (a.k.a. the principle of linguistic relativity).                                                       |
| **Values**                 | Ideas about what is right or wrong, good or bad, desirable or worthy in a particular group; express what the group **cherishes and honors**.                                                                 |
| **Norms**                  | Rules or guidelines regarding what kinds of behavior are acceptable and appropriate within a particular culture; typically emanate from the group's values.                                                  |
| **Laws**                   | Norms that are **formally codified** to provide an explicit statement about what is permissible or forbidden, legal or illegal in a given society.                                                           |
| **Folkways**               | **Loosely enforced** norms involving common customs, practices, or procedures that ensure smooth social interaction and acceptance.                                                                          |
| **Mores**                  | Norms that carry **great moral significance**, are closely related to the core values of a cultural group, and often involve severe repercussions for violators.                                             |
| **Taboo**                  | A norm ingrained so deeply that even **thinking about violating it** evokes strong feelings of disgust, horror, or revulsion (cannibalism, incest).                                                          |
| **Moral holiday**          | A specified time period during which some norm violations are allowed (Mardi Gras, spring break, Las Vegas).                                                                                                 |
| **Sanctions**              | Positive or negative reactions to the ways that people follow or disobey norms — **rewards for conformity, punishments for violations**.                                                                     |
| **Social control**         | The formal and informal mechanisms used to elicit conformity to values and norms and thus promote **social cohesion**.                                                                                       |
| **Multiculturalism**       | A policy that values diverse racial, ethnic, national, and linguistic backgrounds and so **encourages the retention of cultural differences** within the larger society.                                     |
| **Dominant culture**       | The values, norms, and practices of the group within society that is **most powerful** (in terms of wealth, prestige, status, influence, etc.).                                                              |
| **Hegemony**               | Antonio Gramsci's term for the **cultural aspects of social control**, whereby the ideas of the dominant group are accepted by all.                                                                          |
| **Subculture**             | A group within society differentiated by its **distinctive values, norms, and lifestyle** that exists *harmoniously within* the larger mainstream culture.                                                   |
| **Counterculture**         | A group within society that **openly rejects or actively opposes** society's values and norms.                                                                                                               |
| **Culture wars**           | Clashes **within mainstream society** over the values and norms that should be upheld.                                                                                                                       |
| **Ideal culture**          | The norms, values, and patterns of behavior that members of a society **believe should be observed in principle**.                                                                                           |
| **Real culture**           | The norms, values, and patterns of behavior that **actually exist** within a society (may or may not correspond to its ideals).                                                                              |
| **Technology**             | Material artifacts **and the knowledge and techniques required to use them** (hammer → space shuttle, graffiti → search engine algorithm, HTML).                                                             |
| **Cultural diffusion**     | The dissemination of material and nonmaterial culture (tools and technology, beliefs and behavior) **from one group to another**.                                                                            |
| **Cultural imperialism**   | The imposition of one culture's beliefs and practices on another culture **through media and consumer products rather than by military force**.                                                              |
| **Cultural leveling**      | The process by which cultures that were once unique and distinct become **increasingly similar** to one another.                                                                                             |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: Bathroom Bills & the Culture War

- The norm of **sex-segregated bathrooms** seems "natural" — but is learned culture (women's restrooms have baby-changing stations → cultural values about *who* is responsible for child care).
- We tolerate mothers taking young sons into women's rooms but not fathers taking daughters into men's rooms → reveals gendered beliefs/assumptions.
- **"Bathroom bills"** (e.g., North Carolina): legislation requiring people to use the restroom matching their **sex assigned at birth** or face legal sanctions. Proponents: "commonsense" protection of privacy/safety. Opponents: anti-LGBTQ discrimination condoned by law.
- Example of a **culture war** (Bloom 1987; Garber 1998) — clashes over conflicting viewpoints. Trans activists protested with hashtags *Occupotty* and *WeJustNeedToPee*; the White House added its **first all-gender restroom**.
- Restriction laws rest on an **essentialist premise**: gender emerges naturally from sex assigned at birth (binary view), plus the assumption that men are a threat to women/children.
- **Evidence check:** no link between trans bathroom access and bathroom crime (Hasenbush, Flores & Herman 2018); trans/nonbinary teens were at **greater risk of sexual assault** when schools denied bathroom access matching their gender identity (Murchison et al. 2019). Yet many still support the laws → shows how **deeply held cultural ideas about sex and gender** override evidence.

> **Lesson:** Cultural norms (bathrooms) both *reflect* and *reproduce* society's values. Ask what a mundane object/rule communicates about values and norms.

## 1. What Is Culture?

### The concept
- **Broadest definition:** culture = the **entire way of life of a group of people** — language, gestures, dress, standards of beauty, customs, rituals, tools, artifacts, music, child-rearing, even how customers line up in a grocery store.
- Culture forms **basic beliefs and assumptions** about the world and defines the **moral parameters** of right/wrong, good/bad.
- All societies develop culture; it is the **human equivalent of instinct in animals** — we have some instincts, but culture accounts for our success as a species. We are totally dependent on it.
- Culture is **shared, handed down across generations, and passed from group to group**.

### Learned, not innate
- Culture is **learned rather than innate** — but we learn it so slowly and incrementally that we're unaware of the process (who remembers learning slang, personal-space distance, patriotism, or opinions about social class?).
- It becomes **internalized** — ingrained in our thinking and acting; guides how we make sense of the world and make decisions.
- We can speak of the culture of a country, state, community, ethnic/religious group, profession — even sports enthusiasts, schoolmates, or a clique of friends.

### How has culture been studied?
| Who studies it | Approach |
|---|---|
| Theologians & philosophers | Debate the morals/values of an **ideal** culture |
| Art/literature/film scholars | Novels, films, paintings, plays as **expressive, symbolic** activities |
| Cultural anthropologists | Fieldwork in societies **outside the U.S.** |
| Archaeologists | Cultures of the **past**, digging for artifacts |
| **Sociologists** | Culture **closer to home**, often their own society |

- ⚠️ Sociologists may engage in **"othering"** — studying the unusual/extraordinary/deviant and missing the culture right in front of them → the **sociology of everyday life** studies the mundane *and* the exceptional to see both differences ("us"/"them") and similarities.
- **Connects to Chapter 1:** the [[beginner's mind]] and culture shock are tools for seeing our own taken-for-granted culture.

## 2. Ethnocentrism and Cultural Relativism

- Culture acts as a **lens** through which we view the world — it can **elucidate or obscure**. We often can't see our own culture because we're so familiar with it; another culture's differences seem "exotic."

### Classic study: Horace Miner — "Body Ritual among the Nacirema" (1956)
- Written as if by an anthropologist studying an "exotic primitive tribe," describing body rituals: a **household shrine** (bathroom) with a **charm-box** (medicine cabinet), **holy-water font** (sink), daily **rite of ablution** (washing), visits to **medicine men** (doctors) and **holy-mouth men** (dentists), and a bizarre **mouth-rite** (brushing teeth with "hog hairs" + "magical powders").
- **"Nacirema" = "American" spelled backward.** Miner doesn't invent anything — he just approaches the familiar as if he knows nothing about it.
- **Why it's famous:** demonstrates how easy it is to fail to see our own culture because we take it for granted; shows how useful culture shock is for seeing the familiar as bizarre. Student social analysts must observe **both "them" and "us."**

### Ethnocentrism
- Believing our own culture is the "normal" one; using it as a **measuring stick** to judge other individuals/societies — anyone outside the group seems "off-center" or abnormal.
- Can give pride in our group, **but** prevents seeing/understanding others; can become a source of **prejudice and hostility**.
- Relates to Chapter 1's warning to suspend preconceptions.

### Cultural relativism
- Seeing each culture as simply **different** — not better or worse, right or wrong; evaluating it **on its own terms**, placing values/beliefs/norms/practices within their own cultural context.
- Being **culturally sensitive** lets us see people clearly, without judgment, and appreciate their way of life; discover viewpoints and interpretations of reality different from our own.
- **Increases in importance** as our society becomes more diverse.
- ⚠️ Caveat (from closing comments): *cultural relativism itself is a value* — subject to intellectual examination, not an absolute.

## 3. Components of Culture

### A. Material culture
- **Any physical object to which we give social meaning**: art, artifacts, tools, utensils, machines, weapons, clothing, furniture, buildings, toys.
- Studying it = an **archaeological dig of the present**: a designer purse → fashion-conscious; skateboarding logo on a tee → interests; carpeting/lighting/furniture/artwork → about the people who live/work there; sports arenas, transportation, monuments, city dumps → community character. Drive-through fast-food proliferation → Americans' taste for road time, fewer home-cooked meals, predictability.
- **Case study — Santa Barbara, CA:** zoning regulations preserve the Mediterranean "red tile roof" look; new construction must fit the style (size, design, signs, lighting, paint, landscaping restricted) → a new grocery store is hard to distinguish from the century-old post office. Material culture shows how the city preserves history/heritage and resists urban development.

### B. Nonmaterial culture
- The **ideas and beliefs** of a group: from specific rules/customs (drive on the right vs. left) to broad systems (democracy) to large-scale patterns (marriage).

## 4. Communication: Signs, Gestures, and Language

### Signs (symbols)
- Designed to **meaningfully represent something else**: traffic signals, price tags, sheet music, product logos, numbers, letters, bathroom icons.
- Some symbols are nearly universal; others must be learned; context matters for interpretation.

### Gestures (body language / nonverbal communication)
- Signs made with the body: clapping, nodding, smiling, facial expressions, a knowing glance, a raised fist.
- Few besides basic emotions are innate — most are **learned**. The U.S. "thumbs-up" (praise) is obscene/insulting in parts of Asia and South America. Check before traveling whether handshakes/waving are appropriate.

### Language — the most significant component
- A **human universal** (present in all societies) though it varies; one of the most complex, fluid, creative symbol systems (letters/pictograms → words → sentences in near-infinite combinations).
- **Basis of culture** and the primary means of communication: conveys abstract concepts, passes culture across generations, lets us conceive the past and plan the future, categorize people/places/things, and share perspectives on reality.

### The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (Edward Sapir & Benjamin Lee Whorf, 1930s)
- Research with the **Hopi tribe** (American Southwest): claimed the Hopi had **no words distinguishing past, present, future** → therefore did not "see" or experience time the same way.
- **Core claim:** language actually **structures thought** (linguistic relativity) — words help *create* perceptions, not just express them.
- **Contested:** critics challenged the Inuit "many words for snow" claim and the Hopi-no-tenses claim (Martin 1986; Pullum 1991); disagreement remains about *how strongly* language shapes thought (Edgerton 1992). But the hypothesis still influences social thinkers; language plays a real role in constructing reality and categorizing the world.
- **Contemporary relevance:** >44 million foreign-born people in the U.S. speak well over 100 languages → differences in perceptual realities (Budiman 2020).
- **Everyday example:** *Mean Girls* — loosely based on Rosalind Wiseman's *Queen Bees and Wannabes* (2002); the cafeteria classification system ("jocks," "cheerleaders," "goths," "preppies," "skaters," "nerds," the "plastics," "wannabes") — would we perceive classmates the same way if no such labels existed? Shows how **language shapes the meanings** we give to the world.

## 5. Values, Norms, and Sanctions

### Values
- Shared beliefs about what is worthwhile/desirable — good/bad, right/wrong, beautiful/ugly; articulate what a group **cherishes**.
- **Structural functionalists (Durkheim)** stress shared values' strength in regulating behavior. But there is **not always agreement**; values change over time (company loyalty was once central — now workers expect downsizing/career changes and feel less obligation).
- American core values (Bellah et al. 1985; Williams 1965): freedom, democracy — but **inconsistencies** between individualism (best for self) vs. humanitarianism (best for others), equality vs. group superiority. Newer values: environmentalism, mindfulness.

### Norms — rules of acceptable behavior, derived from values
- **Formal norms:** officially codified and explicitly stated — laws (speeding in a school zone, drinking age 21), basketball rules, honor-society requirements, Constitutional amendments, the Ten Commandments. Not always followed despite authority.
- **Informal norms:** implicit, unspoken — standing in line without cutting; embedded in consciousness, cover almost every aspect of social life; usually only noticed when **violated**.

**Table 3.1 — Theory in Everyday Life: Religion** (see also Chapter 1's Table 1.1)

| Perspective | Approach to Culture | Religion lens |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural Functionalism** | Values/norms widely shared and agreed upon; contribute to **social stability** by reinforcing common bonds and constraining individual behavior | Religion = social institution functioning as the basis of morals/ethics followers embrace → promotes **social order** |
| **Conflict Theory** | Values/norms are part of the **dominant culture** and tend to represent/protect the interests of the **most powerful** groups | Religion **controls the masses** by creating rules; sanctions on violators may be unequally/unfairly applied; **culture wars** reflect tension over which values/norms dominate |
| **Symbolic Interactionism** | Values/norms are **social constructions** that vary over time and context; meaning is created, maintained, and changed through interaction | Religion = beliefs and rituals part of interaction among followers (reciting the Lord's Prayer, bowing toward Mecca, keeping kosher = meaningful displays); leaders may create social change |

### The three types of norms (from weak to strong)
- **Folkways** — ordinary conventions of everyday life, not strictly enforced; ensure smooth/orderly interaction (standards of dress, etiquette; flip-flops with a business suit; eating with fingers at the buffet). Violators = **peculiar or eccentric**, not dangerous.
- **Mores** — greater moral significance, closely tied to core values; practically everyone must conform; breaches treated seriously (theft, rape, murder — also formalized into laws).
- **Taboos** (a type of mores) — the most powerful norms; so deeply ingrained that the **mere thought** of committing the act (cannibalism, incest) evokes disgust/horror.

### Norms are specific to situation, culture, and time period
- Public nudity: acceptable in many cultures; illegal (mostly) in the U.S. — yet permitted at strip clubs and nudist resorts → a **moral holiday** from imposed norms. Mardi Gras and spring break tolerate mild violations; Las Vegas ("What happens in Vegas, stays in Vegas") suspends norms by place.
- Murder on city streets = crime; on the battlefield = valor. Dress folkways change across generations (jeans at school 50 years ago → pajama bottoms and slippers today).

### Sanctions
- Means of **enforcing norms**: **positive sanctions** (approval: handshake, smile, praise, award) and **negative sanctions** (disapproval: frown, harsh words, fine, incarceration).
- From a **functionalist perspective**: sanctions help establish **social control** — people behave acceptably → social cohesion. Authority figures (government, police, school administrators, supervisors, parents) exercise power to get others to follow rules; violations trigger prescribed sanctions as a deterrent.

### 💡 In Relationships: Individual Values vs. University Culture
- **"Let's talk about sex on campus."** Public, state-funded universities typically provide on-campus sexual health services (contraceptives, STI testing/treatment, pregnancy testing).
- **Private/religious schools vary widely:** Gonzaga (WA) — no contraceptive benefits but covers STIs; Georgetown — contraceptives only if medically indicated, not for contraception; only a small % of Catholic universities offer contraception (pushback at Fordham and Boston College since the Affordable Care Act mandated coverage). BYU and TCU do offer contraception; Liberty and Eastern Nazarene don't. Unaffiliated schools usually offer services if they have a student health center.
- **Point:** universities have their own cultures (traditions, customs, beliefs, values). Institutional values may **conflict with student culture** — e.g., Boston College barred Students for Sexual Health from campus despite a 2018 referendum in which **94% of students** voted to allow contraceptive distribution (Hunt 2018).
- Schools can control dorm assignments, academic/athletic rules, on-campus health care — **even your sex life**. Individual values ≠ university culture.

### 🦠 Covid and Culture
- Pandemic (March 2020) produced new **material culture**: face masks (from uncommon to ubiquitous), gloves, hand sanitizer, disinfecting wipes — items that existed but were repurposed as critical pandemic artifacts.
- And new **nonmaterial culture**: unfamiliar norms — stay-at-home orders, lockdowns, work-from-home, six-feet **social/physical distancing**, new rules for parks, playgrounds, grocery stores, hand-washing, mask-wearing, standing in line.
- **Norms derive from values — and pandemic norms exposed clashing American values:** respect for authority + belief in science + community solidarity vs. **individualism and personal freedom**; some saw governmental overreach. Conservative vs. liberal divide: **29% of Republicans vs. 63% of Democrats** said people should always wear masks in public (Pew 2020).
- **Social control via sanctions:** police/public-health education, fines in some cities, "No mask, no service" business policies → deep divides within the larger culture.

## 6. Variations in Culture

- Within-culture variation exists; "cultural diversity" and "multiculturalism" became buzzwords as Americans became aware of increasing variety of cultural groups.
- **Multiculturalism** = a *policy* honoring diverse racial, ethnic, national, and linguistic backgrounds, encouraging retention of cultural differences.

### Dominant culture & hegemony
- Subgroups within a larger culture are **not all equal**: some, by size, wealth, or historical happenstance, claim greater power and influence.
- **Dominant culture** = values, norms, practices of the most powerful group; others = "alternative"/minority views, relegated to second-class status → produces **cultural hegemony** (Gramsci 1985, 1988) — the *cultural* aspects of social control, where the dominant group's ideas are accepted by all.
- *Example — popular music:* commercial radio's limited playlists are set by station/record-company business interests, not artistic preference; streaming (Pandora, Spotify) must deliver audiences to advertisers → musicians outside the mainstream can't match John Legend or Taylor Swift's reach.

### Subcultures vs. countercultures
- **Subculture** = a group with a distinctive way of life (own values, norms, practices, beliefs) that exists **harmoniously within** the larger mainstream culture. Based on ethnicity, age, interests, etc. Examples: Korean Americans, senior citizens, snowboarders, White Sox fans, greyhound owners, firefighters, Trekkers.
- **Counterculture** = a subgroup whose norms/values are **incompatible with or in direct opposition to** the mainstream (Zellner 1995). 1960s hippies, antiwar protesters, feminists = "the counterculture." Radicals come in many stripes: eco-terrorists, computer hackers, modern-day polygamists.
  - **Far-right countercultures:** after the 1993 Waco siege (Branch Davidians, 82 deaths), Timothy McVeigh bombed the Oklahoma City federal building in 1995 (168 deaths). The "militia movement" / "sovereign citizens" trace heritage to the Minutemen and see the federal government as the enemy of Constitutional liberties (gun control, environmental law violate individual/states' rights).
  - **Anonymous:** international "hacktivists" opposing censorship and governmental/institutional control of the internet; targets include Nissan, Visa, PayPal, Bank of America, NYSE; also undermined ISIS operations and lent technical support to Black Lives Matter (exposing police misconduct; Beran 2020).

### 📡 In the Future: Online Radicalization — Spreading Extremism
- Recruitment online: colleges, employers, armed services, nonprofits, charities — **and extremist groups**.
- Domestic threat picture changed: experts (FBI, DHS) identify **white nationalist groups** as the most significant threat — responsible for **67% of terrorist plots and attacks in 2020** (Jones et al. 2020).
- Similarities between Islamic terrorists and white nationalists: both use digital tools and social media to spread ideology and recruit; radicalization happens **faster than ever, entirely online**.
- White nationalist/neo-Nazi groups hold an apocalyptic vision of a global race war; memes and propaganda are easy to produce; platforms used: Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, Instagram, **Parler, Gab**, plus encrypted apps (**Telegram, WhatsApp**); they also intimidate opponents. Content slips past platform policies (Squire 2019).
- **Cases:** Charlottesville "Unite the Right" (Aug 2017) organized via a Facebook event page — James Alex Fields Jr. drove through counterprotesters (1 killed, 19 injured); inspired by the neo-Nazi **Daily Stormer** (same site Dylann Roof visited before the 2015 Charleston church massacre). When the site was shut out by domain registrars, it moved to the **dark web**. **Kevin Mathewson's "Kenosha Guard"** (Aug 2020): Facebook call to "take up arms" → 4,000+ responses → armed vigilantes; a gunman shot three protesters, killing two. **January 6, 2021** insurrection at the U.S. Capitol (5 dead, dozens injured): Proud Boys and Oath Keepers organized via social media and private messaging apps (Heilweil & Ghaffary 2021).
- **Question:** How responsible should tech companies be for curbing hate/violence on their platforms? Can they legitimately curtail free expression?

### Culture wars
- Conflict need not come from the margins — it can emerge **within the mainstream**: not everyone agrees which values/norms should be upheld → **culture wars** (Hunter 1991, 2006), waged mainly over values, morality, and solutions to social problems, with liberals and conservatives fighting to define culture. Often played out in media, online, and on the political stage.
- **QAnon** (far right): conspiracy theories — America run by Satan-worshipping pedophiles/sex traffickers stopped only by Trump; false claims about Covid, BLM, the 2020 election; platforms pressured to flag/remove content.
- **Antifa** (far left, "antifascist"): opposes authoritarianism, white nationalism, racism, sexism, homophobia; extreme adherents embrace anarchism and challenge capitalism. Antagonism boiled over at Charlottesville (2017, 1 dead).
- **Popular culture battlegrounds:** graphic content (violence, drug use, sexuality); celebrities as role models — **Harry Styles** on the cover of *Vogue* in a lacy Gucci ball gown (first man to appear solo): "feminization of our men" vs. "referendum on masculinity" (Bradley 2020) — musicians have long challenged traditional gender roles.
- Other battleground issues: **abortion** (Texas "heartbeat law" banning abortion after six weeks), gun violence, family values, LGBTQ rights, immigration, biomedical ethics, school prayer. Culture wars are bound to continue in a multicultural, democratic society.

### Ideal vs. real culture
- **Ideal culture** = norms/values members believe *should* be observed in principle; **real culture** = patterns of behavior that *actually exist*. Organizations fall short of mission statements; a self-described vegetarian enjoys a Big Mac.
- **Enduring U.S. example:** "all men are created equal" enshrined in founding documents, yet equality remains unrealized — from slavery and Western expansion to the oppression of women, discrimination against ethnic minorities, and the battle for LGBTQ rights: a nation that *believes* in equality but doesn't always deliver it.

## 7. Cultural Change

- Cultures usually change **slowly and incrementally**, but change can be rapid and dramatic (1960s social movements; early 2000s). Change is usually viewed as "progress." Variations in culture (multiculturalism, countercultures, culture wars) all contribute to growth and change.

### Technological change
- Most changes in material culture are **technological**. Technology = material artifacts *and* the knowledge/techniques to use them (hammer → space shuttle; graffiti → search-engine algorithm → HTML).
- New technology provides the basis/structure through which culture is disseminated.
- **Digital/Information Age** — spurred by the computer microchip; radical changes comparable to the steam engine in the Industrial Revolution. Timeline: TV in daily U.S. life (1950s), internet common (1990s), smartphones (2000s), tablets and cloud computing/streaming (2010s). The Digital Revolution shapes culture at an increasingly rapid pace.

### Cultural diffusion
- Change when groups **share material and nonmaterial culture**; exposure to another culture → aspects get appropriated. McDonald's-style restaurants export more than hamburgers — the whole fast-food culture travels.
- **Eric Schlosser (2002):** Japanese fast-food consumption doubled in the 1980s — obesity rates rose too, plus heart disease and stroke risk. Japan passed the 2008 **"Metabo Law"**: those 45–74 must have waistlines measured annually; out-of-range → seek medical attention. A single cultural product can't be exported without a raft of cultural consequences; more recently obesity rose in sub-Saharan Africa (Ghana) as KFC/Pizza Hut expanded.
- **Direction:** usually from more developed to less developed nations; "Western" culture spreads rapidly via capitalism, globalization, transportation, communication. Watch MTV in India, *Game of Thrones* in Uzbekistan, Rihanna in Morocco. Media reflect the culture that produces them → implicitly promotes Western ideas, which can conflict with other countries' traditional norms/values.

### Cultural imperialism
- The proliferation of Western media amounts to what critics call **cultural imperialism** (Schiller 1995): media as an invading force — film, TV, music, soft drinks, running shoes instead of guns.
- Historically, imperialism = conquering nations by military might (British Empire once occupied a third of the world's land). Now you can **occupy a territory culturally without setting foot on foreign soil** — Western media companies command enough economic resources to dominate wherever their products go.
- National responses: forbid/restrict information, censor (Iran officially censors non-Islamic content, though many Iranians use hidden satellite dishes), or promote domestic cultural production. Long run: cultural imperialism is hard to prevent.

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Otaku Culture and the Globalization of Niche Interests
- **Otaku** = Japanese word for devoted fans, usually of manga, anime, or video games. Extremely knowledgeable; outsiders may look down on the obsession, but otaku wear it as a **badge of honor** — dedicated, not obsessed; connoisseurs, not fanatics. Japan even has a rigorous **national certification exam** for otaku (McNicol 2006).
- **"Otaku spaces"** (Galbraith 2012): museum-like rooms for collections — illuminate the connection among **fantasy worlds, material commodities, and virtual communities**.
- **Global reach:** fans worldwide flock to Tokyo's **Akihabara** neighborhood (center of anime/videogame commerce). Distinctive: **diffusion in an uncommon direction — East influencing West**. Otaku culture = the globalization/transnationalization of what was Japan-specific. "Citizenship matters less than shared interests, nationality less than knowledge, location less than expertise. Your identity is defined by what you're into."
- The internet made otaku culture accessible globally; fans validate their commitments by connecting with others who share their interests.

### Cultural leveling
- When once-distinct cultures become **increasingly similar**: Walmarts on interstates drive mom-and-pop stores off Main Streets; people bemoan the loss of uniqueness/diversity. Blending can create interesting hybrids (East × West) — or a blander, more diluted culture of sameness.
- Not one-way: Japan's influence on the U.S. (anime was a fringe/geek interest; now Disney partners with Hayao Miyazaki — *Spirited Away*, *Howl's Moving Castle*, *Ponyo*). Still, the U.S. remains the **primary exporter** of global media content.

## 8. American Culture in Perspective

- American culture is **highly visible worldwide** → its moral/political values are equally visible. Reruns of *Friends* or *Grey's Anatomy* in Egypt, Malaysia, or Lebanon transmit American values on sex, gender, work, family; military ventures (Operation Enduring Freedom in Afghanistan, Operation Iraqi Freedom) export democratic, capitalist, and even Christian values.
- **Why this is a problem elsewhere:** in many traditional cultures, young people live with parents until marriage (sometimes to family-chosen partners); shows portraying independent young adults casually dating and sleeping with unmarried partners present distasteful values. The value placed on **individualism, sexual freedom, and material satisfaction** can antagonize cultures valuing **familial involvement and moral/social restraint** → anti-American sentiment.
- **Politics:** the U.S. tries to stem nuclear weapons development in North Korea, Iran, Pakistan while keeping its own arsenal → resentment over America's perceived failure to live up to (or fairly apply) its own political values/ideals.
- **Putting it in perspective:** anti-Americanism is complex — not merely failure to understand "good" TV or accept "superior" political systems; there are meaningful cultural differences, including within the U.S. itself.

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Culture = the "human equivalent of instinct"** — learned, shared, passed across generations; not innate.
- **Material = things** (objects with social meaning); **Nonmaterial = ideas & behaviors** (values, norms, language).
- **Ethnocentrism = our culture as measuring stick**; **Cultural relativism = on their own terms** — "different, not better/worse."
- **Nacirema = American** (spelled backward) — the familiar made strange.
- **Norms ladder (weak → strong):** Folkways (etiquette) → Mores (morality; theft, murder) → Taboos (unthinkable; incest, cannibalism). **Laws** = formalized norms.
- **Sapir-Whorf = language shapes thought** (not just expresses it) — "the words we have create the world we see."
- **Sanctions enforce norms:** positive (reward) & negative (punishment) → **social control**.
- **Subculture = lives *within* the mainstream** (harmoniously); **Counterculture = *opposes* the mainstream** (directly).
- **Hegemony (Gramsci) = dominance through accepted ideas**, not force.
- **Ideal culture = what we preach; Real culture = what we practice** ("all men are created equal" vs. actual inequality).
- **Cultural change engines:** Technology, **Diffusion** (sharing), **Imperialism** (imposed via media/products), **Leveling** (cultures becoming similar).
- **Culture war:** clashes *within* mainstream society over which values/norms should win (bathroom bills, abortion, QAnon vs. Antifa).
