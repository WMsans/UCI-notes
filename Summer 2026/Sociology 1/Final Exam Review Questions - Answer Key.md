# Final Exam Review Questions — Answer Key

## Groups

### 1. What are the features of groups?
- **Group** = a collection of two or more people who share some attribute, identify with one another, and have ongoing social relations (e.g., family, soccer team).
- Per the lecture notes, group members: (1) are **recognized as part** of the group (common identification), (2) have **interaction** among members, (3) share **goals** that are interdependent, and (4) share **norms** — a common set of expectations.
- **What groups are NOT:** **crowds/aggregates** = temporary gatherings or collections that share a physical location but lack common identity and lasting social relations (e.g., people watching a fire, an audience). **Categories** = people sharing one attribute but no sense of common identity or belonging (e.g., all 18-year-olds, all Chevy truck owners). Groups differ by creating **lasting social relations**.
- **Primary groups** (Cooley 1909) = groups composed of the people most important to our sense of self; marked by **face-to-face interaction, high cooperation, and intense feelings of belonging** — families and close friends. They are our most important "looking glasses" and "significant others."
- **Secondary groups** = larger and less intimate groups, usually organized around a **specific goal** and often temporary — co-workers, college classes, labor unions.
- *Example:* a family (primary) vs. a political party (secondary). Note the line blurs: secondary groups often generate primary ties (friends you meet at work).

### 2. What are social networks and how can we use them to study groups?
- **Social network** = the web of direct and indirect ties connecting an individual to other people who may also affect the individual; **social ties** = the connections between individuals (direct: you ↔ your friend; indirect: you ↔ your friend's cousin you've never met).
- Networks vary in structure: **dense networks** = the people you know also know each other; **loose networks** = the people you know are involved in a large variety of fields. "Six degrees of separation" suggests nearly everyone is connected to everyone else within about six steps.
- **Use #1 — explain behavior:** Duncan Watts (*Six Degrees*, 2003) showed **network structure shapes action** — we may change our vote if enough friends vote the other way; network analysis explains fads, fashions, job information, and disease spread.
- **Use #2 — measure influence:** Joseph Conti's WTO study found the U.S. loses most trade disputes yet remains influential — what matters is **centrality** (having the most ties), which itself confers influence.
- **Use #3 — trace opportunity/inequality:** Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) — **weak ties** (acquaintances in different networks) are crucial for job hunting; homogeneous networks limit new information. Hoffman and Torres (2002) found women with more men in their networks heard of better job leads; Pedulla and Pager (2019) found Black and white applicants both benefit from networks but **white applicants benefit more** — networks can reproduce racial inequality.
- **Use #4 — track contagion:** Christakis and Fowler — networks have **connection**, and **contagion** flows through ties (obesity, smoking, STDs); STD risk stays concentrated because people with many partners connect with others like them.
- **Use #5 — public health:** Covid-19 **contact tracing** works through network ties; a Cornell study (Weeden and Cornwell 2020) found almost all undergraduates were separated by just **two other students** — campuses are "small worlds" — which helped keep colleges online; monitoring **central individuals** can give advance warning of outbreaks.
- *Example:* mapping who gives job leads to whom (weak vs. strong ties) reveals why some groups get better jobs.

### 3. What leads to feelings of alienation? Describe Durkheim and Putnam's arguments.
- **Alienation** is tied to **anomie** = "normlessness"; the alienation and loss of purpose resulting from **weaker social bonds and an increased pace of change** — the lecture notes gloss it as feelings of alienation arising in rapid social change.
- **Durkheim's argument:** group norms place **limits on individual desire**; without those limits we endlessly chase unattainable things and become deeply unhappy. Group membership anchors us to the social world "and keeps us alive" — without it we feel disconnected and may even be at risk of **suicide**. He worried that modern society's fragmentation — rapid technological/cultural change — would weaken social bonds, increase anomie, and disconnect people (he had the same fear about the Industrial Revolution that we now have about the Digital Age).
- **Putnam's argument:** Robert Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000) — Americans are less likely to bowl in leagues, join the PTA, Kiwanis, the League of Women Voters, or play bridge: a **decline in civic engagement** and in the community bonds that **keep anomie at bay**. Fewer voluntary group ties → more isolation/alienation.
- **Causes of alienation, summary:** rapid social change (Durkheim) that outruns group norms + the decline of civic/community ties (Putnam).
- **Caveats from the notes:** critics say Putnam romanticizes the "good ol' days," and new technologies/online groups offer alternative ways to connect; Turkle (2011, 2015) worries tech replaces deep bonds with "broader but shallower" ones, but Pew research, Klinenberg, and Fischer find Americans are no more lonely than before.

### 4. Describe a highly cohesive group.
- **Group cohesion** = the sense of solidarity or loyalty individuals feel toward a group; the force that binds members together; per the lecture notes, the **desire to remain in the group**. A minimum level of cohesion is necessary for group survival.
- **Two kinds of cohesion:** **social cohesion** = members stay because they **like one another** and want to interact (tends to occur when members are similar); **task cohesion** = members stay because they are **involved in the group's tasks** (greater when the goal is valuable, interesting, challenging, and clearly defined).
- **Sources of cohesion:** positive emotion and **interdependence** (lecture); the chapter adds shared values, shared demographics (race, age, gender, class), attraction to the group, and successful cooperation toward goals.
- **Consequences of cohesion:** better communication between members; higher-quality interactions; members are more likely to influence one another; **higher conformity**; and task cohesion can increase productivity and performance.
- *Example:* a fraternity or clique of friends who share values and demographics, enjoy interacting, and cooperate successfully — or a sports team whose clearly defined goal (winning the championship) produces task cohesion.
- **Caveat:** high cohesion has a downside — in very cohesive groups the demand for unanimity can suppress critical reasoning (see groupthink, Q8).

### 5. How does the illusion of out-group homogeneity increase in-group cohesion?
- **In-groups vs. out-groups:** an **in-group** = the social group we belong to = "we"; an **out-group** = the social group we do not belong to = "they." One is either an insider or an outsider — feelings toward insiders are **solidarity**; toward outsiders, **avoidance and rejection**. Friendliness and cooperation operate within the group; unfriendliness and conflict apply without.
- **Ethnocentrism and stereotyping:** insiders feel a sense of **ethnocentrism** (superiority) about their own group, while outsiders are viewed through **negative stereotypes** — the illusion of out-group homogeneity lumps "them" together as a uniform mass, heightening the contrast with "us."
- **Merton's double standard** (Robert Merton 1968): the *same* behavior is judged positively in "us" and negatively in "them" — *we* are laid-back, *they* are lazy; *we* are classy, *they* are snobbish; *we* are devout, *they* are zealots. Granting insiders favorable judgments while homogenizing outsiders under negative ones sharpens the boundary between the groups.
- **The cohesion effect:** "us vs. them" **intensifies loyalty and cohesion** within the group — a homogenized, negatively viewed out-group makes members feel more distinct, superior, and united. The notes confirm the parallel point that **out-group pressure increases in-group bonds**.
- **The costs:** the same dynamics fuel prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination (by class, race, gender, sexuality, religion, politics) — at worst enabling slavery and genocide.
- **The remedy:** **intergroup contact** can break down stereotypes, especially when it is **institutionally supported** by social norms promoting equality. Rites of passage (e.g., hazing, basic training) and **degradation ceremonies** (Garfinkel 1956) bind members by marking the transformation from outsider to insider.
- *Example:* rival sports fan bases or rival fraternities — each side stereotypes the other as all alike ("they're all the same") while seeing itself as diverse and superior, which tightens bonds among insiders.

### 6. What impact does intergroup conflict have on processes internal to the group (do groups benefit in certain ways from intergroup conflict)?
- **Intergroup conflict** = organized conflict between organized groups; *Example:* nations at war.
- **Yes — groups benefit internally in several ways.** The notes' "within-group process" findings: conflict changes the structure of participating groups, making them more **cohesive**, making **leadership more militant**, and making **winning the conflict the priority**; **norms change and conformity increases**.
- **Out-group pressure increases in-group bonds** — external threat pulls members together.
- **Minimal group paradigm research:** even **arbitrary group distinctions** (no real history or stakes) trigger in-group/out-group processes such as **ethnocentrism** and **biased evaluations of group performance** — in-group members rate their own group's performance more favorably.
- **So the "benefits" are internal solidarity effects** — stronger cohesion, unified norms, clearer priorities — which come at the cost of hostility, prejudice, and biased judgment toward the out-group. Cohesion's usual consequences follow: better communication, higher-quality interactions, greater mutual influence, and higher conformity within the group.
- *Example:* nations at war rally around leaders and flags; a company facing a rival may become more unified internally even as it stereotypes the competitor.

### 7. Describe reference groups/social comparison theory.
- **Reference group** = a group that provides a **standard of comparison** against which we evaluate ourselves — "the membership to which we refer to know ourselves." It may include groups we **aspire to join** but are not yet members of (e.g., future nurses or lawyers comparing themselves to the profession).
- We use reference groups to ask: **Am I good enough? Smart enough? Fast enough?** Possible reference groups include peers, professional groups, celebrities, and even fictional characters.
- **Negative self-image risk:** if we do not see ourselves as having the same desirable qualities as the reference group, we may **adopt a negative self-image**.
- **Social comparison theory** = people prefer to compare themselves with others who are **similar in salient (relevant) respects**.
- **Practical tip from the notes:** choose your comparison groups carefully, or you will lose self-esteem quickly.
- *Example:* a college student comparing grades to classmates in the same major (similar/salient comparison) vs. constantly measuring against a celebrity or top-of-field professional (an unrealistic standard that breeds negative self-image).

### 8. What are the symptoms and causes of groupthink? Be able to provide an example.
- **Groupthink** (Irving Janis 1971, 1982) = the failure to realistically evaluate alternative courses of action because of **pressures for unanimity** within the group; in very cohesive groups, the tendency to **enforce a high degree of conformity**, creating a demand for unanimous agreement and **suppressing critical reasoning**.
- **Causes:** (1) **homogeneity** of members; (2) **insulation** of the group from its environment; (3) **lack of clear-cut rules** to guide decision-making within the group; (4) **high levels of group stress**.
- **Symptoms:** (1) **illusions of invulnerability**; (2) **illusions of the group's morality**; (3) **collective rationalization**; (4) **stereotyping of the adversary**; (5) **self-censorship** (members suppress doubts); (6) **pressure on dissenters**; (7) **mindguarding**; (8) **apparent unanimity**.
- **Example:** the **NASA Challenger explosion (1986)** — scientists failed to take warnings seriously. Other noted examples: the **Iraq War (2003)** — the Senate Intelligence Committee blamed groupthink for exaggerated WMD claims; the **2016 election** — media/polling groupthink predicted a Clinton win.
- **Antidotes/avoidance:** the **leader should encourage dissent** and be impartial — not announce a preference; the group should **divide into independent subgroups**; hold a **"second chance" meeting** after tentative consensus; and appoint a **"devil's advocate"** to challenge members' positions. Lincoln's "team of rivals" — advisers diverse enough to challenge groupthink — is the model antidote.

### 9. Explain the focus of each perspective with regard to groups
- **Structural functionalism:** group life helps **regulate and give meaning to individual experience**, contributing to **social cohesion and stability**.
- **Conflict theory:** group membership is the basis for the **distribution of rewards, privileges, and opportunities**; individuals may be treated **preferentially or prejudicially** based on group membership.
- **Symbolic interactionism:** group **norms, values, and dynamics are generated situationally, in interaction** with other members.

| Perspective | Focus with regard to groups | *Example:* Fraternities (Table 5.1 case study) |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural Functionalism** | Groups regulate and give meaning to individual experience, contributing to social cohesion and stability. | Fraternities create social cohesion within a larger, possibly alienating, university by bringing young men with shared values together. |
| **Conflict Theory** | Group membership drives the distribution of rewards, privileges, and opportunities; people are treated preferentially or prejudicially based on membership. | In-group/out-group dynamics create stereotyping and conflict — "us vs. them" toward other fraternities and non-Greeks. |
| **Symbolic Interactionism** | Group norms, values, and dynamics are generated situationally, in interaction with members. | Pressure to conform (peer pressure, groupthink) can produce negative outcomes (hazing, binge drinking) or positive ones (volunteering, charity fundraisers). |

- **Bottom line:** each perspective answers a different question about groups — functionalism asks how groups create stability and meaning, conflict theory asks who benefits and who is disadvantaged by group membership, and symbolic interactionism asks how group life is built up through everyday interaction.

## Deviance

### 1. What is deviance?
- **Deviance** = a behavior, feeling/value, thought/belief, trait, or other characteristic that **violates a norm** and causes a negative reaction (Goode 1997: a behavior, trait, or belief that **departs from a norm** and **generates a negative reaction in a particular group**).
- **Both elements are necessary** — the norm departure *and* the group's negative reaction — nothing is deviant in itself.
- **Deviance is socially constructed and relative:** the same act can flip from conformity to deviance depending on culture, history, and situation. *Example:* in some societies eating with your hands is normal, but eating a whole steak dinner with your hands would disgust your American friends.
- **Gradations of deviance:** some violations are mildly deviant (chewing with your mouth open), others taboo almost beyond thought (cannibalism); deviance must be serious or unusual enough to trigger a **negative sanction** (punishment).
- **We are all deviants of one sort or another:** speeding, drinking before 21, nose-picking in public, taking office pens.
- **Fluidity over time:** *Example:* marijuana — the Founding Fathers grew hemp (Jefferson drafted the Declaration of Independence on hemp paper), Hearst-led propaganda criminalized it by 1937, and today a majority of states have legalized it. Nothing is inherently deviant — the cultural, historical, and situational context makes it so.

### 2. Does deviance have a function (Durkheim)?
- **Yes — deviance is functional:** from the functionalist perspective, deviance serves society, and people **reinforce norms** by defining deviance.
- **Function 1: clarifying moral boundaries** — deviance reminds everyone of shared notions of right when wrongs are addressed. *Example:* Cecil the lion (2015) — dentist Walter Palmer killed a protected lion legally, but the public deemed it immoral.
- **Function 2: promoting social cohesion** — shared outrage over deviance unites a community. *Example:* the Cecil case went viral worldwide, sparking protests outside Palmer's office and condemnation from Jane Goodall and PETA.
- **Function 3: promoting social change** — **"deviant heroes"** (Wolf and Zuckerman 2012) violate norms and risk the repercussions intending to create positive change. *Example:* Colin Kaepernick knelt during the national anthem (2016) to protest racial injustice; it cost him his football career but raised awareness and contributed to police reforms.
- **Deviance is inevitable:** even in Durkheim's hypothetical "society of saints" (1895), deviance would still occur — so it cannot be eliminated, only understood.

### 3. Describe Structural Strain Theory. Be able to provide examples.
- **Structural strain theory** (Merton 1938) = deviance results from **strain** between culturally approved **goals** (e.g., financial success) and the **legitimate means** available to achieve them.
- Bridges functionalism and conflict theory: like Durkheim, deviance is inevitable; like conflict theory, **one's position in the social structure affects one's experience of deviance**.
- **Merton's typology** — five adaptations defined by combinations of accepting/rejecting goals vs. means:
  - **Conformist** = accepts society's approved goals *and* the approved means. *Example:* T'Challa in *Black Panther*, who upholds Wakanda's policies through approved means.
  - **Innovator** = accepts the goals but **rejects the approved means**. *Example:* drug dealing and embezzlement; Nakia in *Black Panther*, who shares Wakandan values but favors unconventional methods.
  - **Ritualist** = has given up hope of achieving the goals but still operates by the approved means — going through the motions. *Example:* Okoye in *Black Panther*, who loyally follows Wakanda's rules even under Killmonger until she loses faith in his new goals.
  - **Retreatist** = **renounces both goals and means** and lives outside conventional norms. *Example:* dropouts and hermits; M'Baku and the Jabari, the mountain tribe rejecting Wakanda's norms.
  - **Rebel** = rejects society's goals and means **and creates new, sometimes revolutionary goals and means instead**. *Example:* Killmonger in *Black Panther*, who wants to destroy the world with Wakanda's weapons.

### 4. Describe Differential Association Theory and Labeling Theory
- **Differential association theory** (Sutherland 1939) = **we learn to be deviant through our interactions with others who break the rules** — the logic of "don't hang out with the bad kids." *Example:* an athlete on steroids influencing teammates to "dope."
- **Context matters in what is learned:** we may not think a behavior is deviance in our own environment, even though it is deviance in other environments.
- **Critiques of differential association:** not everyone who associates with deviants becomes deviant, many deviants never consorted with rule breakers, and it can't explain deviance that isn't a willful act (e.g., mental illness).
- **Labeling theory** (Becker 1963) = deviance is a **consequence of external judgments, or labels**, which modify the individual's self-concept and change how others respond to them. Deviance is not inherent in any act — it is determined by **social context**. *Example:* a man who kills an intruder attacking his child may be labeled a hero, while a man who kills a cashier during a robbery is a villain — the act (homicide) is the same.
- **Labels stick:** Rosenhan's "On Being Sane in Insane Places" (1973) — pseudo-patients admitted to psychiatric hospitals were treated as mentally ill throughout and discharged with schizophrenia "in remission"; once labeled schizophrenic, the pseudo-patient was stuck with that label.
- **Primary deviance** (Lemert 1951) = the **initial act or attitude that causes one to be labeled deviant**. *Example:* having excess body weight, which U.S. culture treats as deviant.
- **Secondary deviance** (Lemert 1951) = the **subsequent deviant identity or career that develops as a result of being labeled**. *Example:* *The Biggest Loser* contestants coming to see themselves as "fatties" who need slimming; the lecture's example — "because you are female, you are bad at math."
- **Tertiary deviance** (Kitsuse 1980) = **redefining the stigma of a deviant label as a positive phenomenon**. *Example:* "Sure, I'm fat, but there shouldn't be anything wrong with that" — fat acceptance.

### 5. What is the relationship between the self-fulfilling prophecy and stereotype threat/promise?
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy** (Merton, derived from the **Thomas theorem**, W. I. Thomas 1928) = a prediction that **causes itself to come true merely by being stated**. Thomas theorem: "If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences." *Example:* a 1930s bank collapsing on a rumor of its own insolvency.
- **Stereotype threat** = a **type of self-fulfilling prophecy** (Claude Steele 1997/2010) in which the **fear of performing poorly — and thereby confirming negative stereotypes about one's group — causes people to perform poorly**. *Example:* priming high-achieving African American students or highly ranked female math students to think about their group membership before an exam made them perform worse (Spencer, Steele, and Quinn 1999); the lecture's example — "because you are female, you are bad at math."
- **Stereotype promise** = the flip side (Lee and Zhou 2014): a **self-fulfilling prophecy in which positive stereotypes lead to positive performance outcomes**. *Example:* the "model minority" label — Asian American students receive more AP placements, higher grades, and better treatment from teachers.
- **The relationship:** threat and promise are two directions of the same mechanism — stereotypes **become real as people act on them**; a negative stereotype about your group produces a self-defeating prophecy (threat), while a positive stereotype produces a self-enhancing prophecy (promise).
- **Real-world illustration of the cycle:** Elijah Anderson's *Streetwise* (1990) — police and community perceive Black male inner-city teenagers as a criminal element, so they are more likely to be arrested and reported and must constantly defend their innocence, making incarceration more likely — a negative self-fulfilling cycle that is hard to break.

### 6. What are outsiders? How do they view deviance?
- **Outsiders** (Becker 1963) = people who are **labeled deviant and subsequently segregated from "normal" society**; in the lecture, people living outside of mainstream society. They may still pass among "normals," or deviance may become their **master status** — *Example:* punk subculture members are assumed to be loud troublemakers regardless of their actual personalities.
- **Outsiders do not necessarily view their own behavior as deviant** — deviance is defined by context: a behavior may not be seen as deviance within one's own environment even though mainstream society defines it as deviant.
- **Deviance avowal** (Turner 1972) = rather than having the label imposed, some outsiders **initiate the labeling process against themselves** — deviance as a role rather than an isolated act. *Example:* AA's first step, admitting "I am an alcoholic."
- **Voluntary outsiders** = people who find it **preferable to be deviant** in spite of mainstream norms, using the deviant identity to justify avoiding conventional expectations ("neutralization of commitment"). *Example:* a recovering alcoholic who refuses a 9-to-5 job or family gatherings because stress or attendance "always made them drink."
- **In-group orientation** = rather than accepting mainstream judgment, stigmatized outsiders may **reject prevailing judgments and develop new standards that value their group identity**. *Example:* groups like PFLAG, the National Association of the Deaf (NAD), and the National Association to Advance Fat Acceptance (NAAFA).

### 7. Discuss the connections between crime and variables such as age, gender, class, and race.
- **Crime** = a violation of a norm that has been **codified into law**, for which you could be arrested and imprisoned; the lecture adds that crime requires **violating the law *and* being caught**. General patterns: murder peaks in **July–August (summer)** and is higher in the **Southern United States**; property crimes outnumber violent crimes and larceny is the most common.
- **Class:** crime rates look higher in poor urban areas — but **not necessarily because the poor commit more crime**: police **concentrate their efforts there**, so they are simply **caught more often**. Class relates more to **how people are officially treated** by police, courts, and prisons than to who actually commits crime. Sampson and Wilson (2005): the same factors that disadvantage an area (lack of jobs, child care, good schools) also encourage crime. Meanwhile, **white-collar crime** (Sutherland) — crime by the respectable and high-status (fraud, embezzlement, insider trading) — is **policed and punished less strenuously than street crime**. *Example:* Elizabeth Holmes and Theranos — charged with "massive fraud" in 2018.
- **Age:** younger people commit more crime — peak arrest age is **19**, and 18–24-year-olds are 9% of the population but 20% of arrests; there is "aging out of crime" (65+ are 16% of the population but only 1.6% of arrests). Caution: our stereotype of the youthful criminal makes police and the public more likely to **arrest young people (they are caught more often)**; youth commit more *visible* crimes, and older people's crimes (embezzlement, fraud) are harder to detect.
- **Gender:** males are far more likely to commit crimes — **88% of murder arrests and 73% of all arrests** (2019). The lecture cautions this is largely a matter of males being **caught more often** — if women gained more power, they too would be caught more. Messerschmidt (1993): as women gain power in the labor market (education, income), their crime rates should rise toward men's — largely supported (2010–2019 male arrests fell 23%, female arrests only 15%). Early "biology" explanations have been replaced by social-structural ones emphasizing women's social and economic roles.
- **Race:** African Americans are 13% of the population but **27% of arrests and 33% of prisoners**. Sociologists caution against linking race and criminality, pointing instead to **institutional racism** (disproportionate targeting), **class variables** (Black Americans are more likely to live in lower-income, heavily policed communities with fewer opportunities), legal-system bias, and **self-fulfilling prophecy**. *Example:* Michelle Alexander's *The New Jim Crow* (2011) — criminal-justice contact permanently stigmatizes African American men, denying them employment, housing, and public benefits. *Example:* Devah Pager (2003) — in a matched-pair job audit, a **white man with a criminal record got more callbacks than a Black man with no record**.
- **Intersectionality:** these variables don't act in isolation. *Example:* Nikki Jones (2012) found inner-city Black girls in Philadelphia caught in a **respectability vs. practicality bind** — expected to be "good girls" while neighborhoods require them to look tough and be ready to fight — because their race, class, gender, and age interact.

## Inequality

### 1. Define inequality? What are its basic principles?
- **Social inequality** = the unequal distribution of wealth (*especially* wealth), power, or prestige among members of a society.
- **Social stratification** (the structure inequality takes) = the division of society into groups arranged in a social hierarchy; members are categorized and ranked, and higher strata enjoy more access to rewards and resources (by criteria such as age, gender, race, class).
- **Principle 1 — Inequality is a characteristic of society, not a reflection of individual differences.** Stratification ranks *groups*, not persons. *Example:* in Japan men rank higher as a group, even though a particular woman can outrank a particular man.
- **Principle 2 — Social stratification persists over generations.** Position tends to be inherited rather than earned anew. *Example:* in Great Britain a child inherits a regional accent along with race, and wealthy families stay wealthy.
- **Principle 3 — All societies stratify, but the criteria vary.** Different societies rank people on different bases. *Example:* industrialized nations rank mainly by material wealth (social class), whereas hunter-gatherer societies (e.g., the Khoisan Bushmen of southern Africa) rank by gender.
- **Principle 4 — Stratification is maintained by widely shared beliefs.** People accept the system partly through ideas that justify it. *Example:* the myth that the poor simply failed to "pull themselves up by their bootstraps."

### 2. Describe the three systems of stratification. Be able to provide an example of each.
- **Slavery** = the most extreme form of social stratification, based on the **legal ownership of people**. Enslaved people are bought and sold as property, forced to work under threat, and denied rights. *Example:* the transatlantic slave trade, in which people were captured/kidnapped and enslaved for life (children inherited enslaved status). Though slavery is illegal and considered immoral everywhere today, modern slavery persists in the form of child soldiers, serfdom, forced/bonded labor, and human trafficking.
- **Caste** = a form of stratification based on **heredity**: status is determined by family history and background and cannot be changed, regardless of individual achievement. Castes are endogamous (members marry within), and ranking passes to children. *Example:* India's caste system — Brahman (scholars/priests), Ksatriya/Chhetri (rulers/warriors), Vaisya (merchants/traders), Sudra (farmers/artisans/laborers), and Dalit (outcasts/"untouchables") — justified by karma and constitutionally prohibited since 1949 yet still prevalent. *Related example:* apartheid in South Africa (1948–1991), legal segregation of racial/ethnic groups.
- **Social class** = a system of stratification based on access to **wealth, property, power, and prestige** (also called socioeconomic status, SES); practiced primarily in capitalist/industrial societies and common since industrialization. It is less rigid than slavery or caste — children tend to "inherit" class but can move up or down during a lifetime — and it is not formally based on race, ethnicity, gender, or age, though there is huge overlap among these variables (studied as **intersectionality**, Crenshaw 1991). *Example:* the U.S. class ladder — upper class (1%, net worth greater than the bottom 90% combined), upper-middle class (14%), middle class (30%), working/lower-middle class (30%), working poor (13%), and underclass (12%).

### 3. How do functionalist theorists and conflicts theorists explain inequality? Compare and contrast.
- **Functionalist explanation** (structural functionalism, Davis & Moore 1945): stratification is not egalitarian, but it serves a purpose — society needs a variety of roles to be performed, and it needs incentives to fill the most important, most difficult roles. Roles requiring more talent and training (e.g., doctors) and that are critical to society therefore receive bigger rewards. Americans largely agree with functionalist arguments.
- **Conflict explanation (Marx):** conflict between social groups is central to the workings of society and serves as the agent of social change. Capitalism creates two classes — the **bourgeoisie** (owners of the means of production) and the **proletariat** (workers who own only their labor). Wealth and privilege concentrate among the capitalists, workers remain exploited, and inequality grows; Marx also identified **false consciousness** — the inability to see how we may be oppressed.
- **Conflict critique of functionalism:** it is often unclear which roles are more critical or desirable, and incentives do not always correspond with importance or difficulty — e.g., pop-culture heroes (actors, athletes) outrank everyday heroes (day-care providers, firefighters), so whose values structure the system? Erik Olin Wright (1997) adds that social structure itself shapes and sustains inequality.
- **What both share:** both are macro-level theories that treat inequality as a feature of the social system rather than of individuals — they mainly differ on whether that feature is useful (functionalist) or harmful and conflict-generating (conflict).

| Dimension | Functionalist (Davis & Moore 1945) | Conflict (Marx) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of inequality | Necessary and functional for society | A product of group conflict and exploitation |
| Why it exists | Incentives are needed to fill the most important, most difficult roles | Capitalists (bourgeoisie) concentrate wealth by exploiting workers (proletariat) |
| Who benefits | Everyone — society runs more smoothly | The owners of the means of production |
| Social change | Not the focus — stability via shared roles | Conflict is the agent of social change |
| Critique of the other | Conflict view ignores that some reward differences are needed | Unclear which roles are truly most critical; incentives don't match importance/difficulty |

- **Within conflict theory — Marx vs. Weber:**

| Dimension | Marx | Weber |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of class | Ownership of the means of production (bourgeoisie vs. proletariat) | Owning the means of production is *not* the only path — three components |
| Components of stratification | One economic divide: owners vs. workers | **Wealth** (income + property), **power** (ability to impose one's will), and **prestige** (social honor from membership in well-regarded groups) |
| Can dimensions convert? | Not emphasized | Yes — the three convert into each other (e.g., the Kardashians turned inherited wealth into celebrity prestige) |
| Example | Nineteenth-century Europe's industrializing feudalism | Prestige table ranking occupations, e.g., physician (86) at the top, janitor at the bottom — and athletes (65) outrank sociologists (61) |

### 4. What is social reproduction? How does cultural capital contribute to this process?
- **Social reproduction** (Bourdieu 1973, 1984) = the tendency of social classes to remain relatively stable as class status is passed down from one generation to the next. Social class is inherited, not earned afresh — *Example:* the Kardashians converted inherited wealth into celebrity prestige, reproducing their family's high status.
- **Cultural capital** (Bourdieu) = the tastes, habits, expectations, skills, knowledge, and other cultural assets that help us gain advantages in society. It is inherited from parents, though it can also be acquired.
- **Mechanism 1 — Inherited advantages in everyday life:** children inherit their parents' cultural assets; educated parents help with homework and expect credentials, so their children navigate school and careers more successfully.
- **Mechanism 2 — Shaping others' perceptions:** cultural capital also shapes how others judge us — in job interviews, for example, socially skilled candidates win. Class can be read instantly from speech and manner: *Example:* accent "gives you away" — "talks like a hillbilly," "sounds too 'street.'"
- **Mechanism 3 — Acquisition is possible but unequal:** adults can try to acquire cultural capital (adult-ed classes, concerts, tennis clubs, European travel), but childhood effects are powerful — the tastes and habits instilled early are hard to match later.
- **Why this matters:** because cultural capital is passed down with class position, it is the key mechanism by which social reproduction happens — it converts parents' class advantage into children's success, keeping social classes relatively stable across generations.

### 5. What are the effects of social class on life chances? Be specific.
- **Overall:** socioeconomic status (SES) predicts life chances across family, health, education, work, and crime — with government policy and friendships also shaping outcomes.
- **Health:** higher-SES individuals have more access to healthcare resources, typically feel healthier, and live longer; lower-class people have less adequate nutrition, shelter, and care, cannot afford prevention, and so wait for crises and end up in expensive emergency rooms. *Specific data:* for men, the richest 1% live about **15 years longer** than the poorest 1%; for women, about **10 years** (Chetty et al. 2016b) — even exercise is a luxury (gym memberships, safe recreation, free time). During Covid-19, social distancing and working from home were privileges of affluence.
- **Family:** people are more likely to have relationships with and marry people similar to them — **homogamy** ("like marries like"). Higher-SES women marry and have children later and tend to have fewer children. *Specific data:* college graduates marry later and divorce less; women with a master's degree or more have their first child at about 30 versus about 24 for high-school graduates.
- **Education:** performance in school affects where you go to college, what jobs you can get, and how much you are paid. Schools are financed by local property taxes, so class-segregated neighborhoods produce unequally funded schools. *Specific data:* 78% of top income-quartile students enroll in college versus 48% of bottom-quartile students; top-income young adults are about **5× more likely to earn a BA by age 24** (62% vs. 13%) (Cahalan et al. 2021).
- **Income and employment:** lower-SES individuals are more vulnerable to unemployment and underemployment, and crises hit them hardest. *Specific data:* the top 1% captures about **20% of all income** (top 10% about 50%); the working poor may work full-time yet stay near or below the poverty line; "precarious labor" (contract, temp, part-time, gig work) is uncertain, unpredictable, and unprotected (Kalleberg and Vallas 2018).
- **Criminal justice:** lower-SES individuals are more likely to encounter the criminal justice system as both victims and perpetrators — in part because the poor are more visible, less powerful, and more likely to be apprehended and labeled. *Specific data:* about **90% of death-row inmates could not afford a lawyer**, and the quality of representation — not the facts — predicts death sentences (Gorn 2018); people in poor households are more than twice as likely to be victims of violent crime; white-collar criminals like Bernie Madoff are less likely to be arrested, prosecuted, or convicted and get lighter sentences.

### 6. Compare and contrast closed and open stratification systems. Which is the U.S.?
- **Closed system** = a stratification system with *very little opportunity to move from one class to another*; rank is fixed, usually at birth. **Open system** = a stratification system with *ample opportunities to move from one class to another*; position is at least partly achieved rather than purely assigned.
- **Closed systems take the form of slavery or caste.** **Slavery** = the most extreme form of stratification — people as property, forced to work under threat, no rights; status usually lifelong and inherited by children. **Caste system** = status determined by *one's family history and background and cannot be changed*; based on heredity with endogamy (marriage within one's group) and no mobility. *Example:* India's caste system — ranked categories from Brahman (scholars/priests) down to Dalit ("untouchables"), justified by karma and passed to children; constitutionally prohibited since 1949 but still socially prevalent. *Example:* apartheid South Africa (1948–1991) — legally ranked racial groups.
- **Open systems take the form of social class.** **Social class** = stratification based on access to resources such as *wealth, property, power, and prestige*; less rigid than caste — children tend to "inherit" class but *can move up or down during a lifetime*; not formally based on race, ethnicity, gender, or age, though there is heavy overlap between them (**intersectionality**, Crenshaw 1991).
- **Key contrasts:** the *basis of rank* (birth/heredity/legal rules vs. wealth, prestige, achievement); the *degree of permitted mobility* (very little vs. ample); and how rank persists (caste is reinforced by endogamy and heredity, while class boundaries are blurrier and generate **status inconsistency** — differing/contradictory levels of wealth, power, prestige).
- **The U.S. is an open system — at least in perception.** Class-based systems allow movement, and the American Dream/meritocracy ideology holds that anyone can succeed through hard work. Status inconsistency is common *because* the system is believed to be open.
- **But the evidence shows limited actual mobility:** mobility has *stalled in the last ~50 years*; a child born in the bottom fifth of income has just a **9% chance of reaching the top fifth** (Chetty et al. 2014b), and class mobility is actually *less attainable in the U.S. than in many other countries*; chances vary by geography (*Example:* San Jose, CA ≈13% vs. Charlotte, NC ≈4%). History also contradicts openness: pre–Civil War slavery, and *The New Jim Crow* (Alexander 2011) — a **caste-like system** running through the criminal justice system (felon disenfranchisement falling disproportionately on Black men).

### 7. Distinguish between intergenerational mobility, intragenerational mobility, and structural mobility
- **Intergenerational mobility** = movement between social classes that occurs *from one generation to the next* — comparing a child's class standing with their parents'. *Example:* Baby Boomers mostly moved *up*, but mobility has *stalled in the last 50 years*; a child born in the bottom fifth of income has just a **9% chance of reaching the top fifth** (Chetty et al. 2014b). Geography matters too: ≈13% in San Jose vs. ≈4% in Charlotte.
- **Intragenerational mobility** = movement between social classes that occurs *during the course of an individual's own lifetime*. It comes in two directions:
  - **Horizontal social mobility** = movement *within a particular social class*, most often from changing occupations. *Example:* therapist → college professor (by far the more common type).
  - **Vertical social mobility** = movement *between different class statuses* — upward or downward. *Example:* marrying a corporate president = up; losing a job/unemployment = down.
- **Structural mobility** = changes in the social status of *large numbers of people at once*, caused by *structural changes in society* rather than individual effort or failure. *Example:* the Great Depression (1929 crash) pushed masses down; the dot-com boom (late 1990s) made overnight millionaires.
- **Bottom line:** individual effort is not the whole story — *structure moves people too*.

### 8. Distinguish between absolute and relative poverty. What characteristics do people in poverty share?
- **Relative deprivation** (relative poverty) = a person's standard of living is *below that of other members of their own society* — poverty judged relative to the prevailing standard of living. *Example:* a minimum-wage clerk compared with a neurosurgeon.
- **Absolute deprivation** (absolute poverty) = an *objective* measure — people *cannot meet minimal standards of food, shelter, clothing, and healthcare*. *Example:* Eswatini (Swaziland), where >25% of adults live with HIV/AIDS and life expectancy is under 58 years.
- **Federal poverty line** = the U.S.'s official, *absolute* measure of poverty, defined by household income and updated annually. *Example:* $26,500 for a family of four (2021). About **10.5% of the population (≈34 million people)** fell below it in 2019 — never below 10% in 40 years.
- **Characteristics people in poverty share:**
  - **Most poor people are employed** — often full-time; the stereotype of the lazy poor is a myth. Yet full-time work at the federal minimum wage ($7.25/hr since 2009) still leaves a family below the line: at no point in its ~80-year history has the minimum wage lifted a worker with a dependent above poverty. The **working poor** = poorly educated manual and service workers who may work full-time yet remain near or below the poverty line.
  - **Race/ethnicity gaps:** higher poverty rates among Black Americans (19%) and Hispanic Americans (16%) than Asian Americans (7%) and non-Hispanic whites (7%) (2019).
  - **Higher rates among:** people with disabilities, the foreign-born, women, children, and single-parent households.
  - **Geography:** poverty is concentrated in the South and found in inner cities, rural areas, and suburbs alike.
  - **The underclass** = the poorest group at the extreme end — the chronically unemployed or people experiencing homelessness, dependent on public benefits/charity, in substandard housing or homeless.
  - **Employment instability:** lower-SES individuals are more *vulnerable to unemployment and underemployment*; even those who spend most of the year in the labor force (the working poor) stay below the line.
  - **Invisibility:** the poor are residentially segregated and politically disenfranchised — most people don't see poverty unless they are part of it.

### 9. Explain the Culture of Poverty argument. What are some criticisms of this argument?
- **Culture of poverty** (Oscar **Lewis 1959**, studying poor Hispanics in Mexico and the U.S.) = the argument that the poor, *because they were excluded from mainstream society*, developed a **way of life qualitatively different from that of the middle class**.
  - Its features: **resignation, fatalism, and acceptance of fate** rather than trying to improve their lives, plus a preference for **immediate gratification**.
  - These entrenched attitudes are **passed down to children**, so the culture reproduces itself — the poor accept their lot rather than attempting to change it and remain ill-equipped to do so.
- **Criticisms:**
  - **Victim blaming** — it blames the poor themselves and overlooks the social conditions that produce poverty; it implies eradicating poverty is pointless.
  - **Misdiagnoses the cause** — it suggests poverty is a *cultural* problem and not an *economic* one.
  - **Ignores structure** — it ignores the social-structural factors that shape culture in the first place.
  - **Feeds the just-world hypothesis** (Lerner 1965, 1980) = the tendency, from a deep need to see the world as orderly, predictable, and fair, to *view victims of social injustice as deserving of their fates*. In Lerner's experiments, observers judged randomly chosen cash-prize winners as more deserving and harder-working; strong just-world believers feel less need to change society (Rubin and Peplau 1975).
  - **Lacks sociological imagination** (Conley 2002) — to solve poverty one must also examine *wealth*: laws that protect wealthy inheritances while giving working families few breaks reveal structural causes; extreme wealth itself should be conceptualized as a social problem.

### 10. What affects someone's ability to exit homelessness in Los Angeles?
- **Context:** the notes' homelessness material is centered on Los Angeles (the course's home city) — specifically **Skid Row, Los Angeles**, the setting of the notes' key study on policing the homeless.
- **The poverty baseline:** most poor people *are* employed, yet full-time work at the federal minimum wage has never, in its ~80-year history, lifted a worker with a dependent above the poverty line. The **working poor** (full-time but below the line) and the **underclass** (chronically unemployed or homeless, dependent on public benefits/charity, in substandard housing or homeless) show that having a job is not automatically an exit — the income available to the poor is often insufficient for stable housing.
- **Eviction and housing instability:** **Matthew Desmond's Eviction Lab** tracked ~300,000 evictions across 5 states/27 cities (March–December 2020), and the **CDC** used emergency powers in September 2020 to halt some evictions for public health — ongoing housing instability works against people securing and keeping a place to live.
- **Housing-market barriers to building assets:** **redlining** (lenders refusing mortgages or charging prohibitively high rates in "high-risk" — usually low-income and minority — neighborhoods) and **residential segregation** block the asset-building and neighborhood access that could lift people out of poverty and homelessness, often confining the poor to high-density, poorly maintained housing.
- **Criminal-justice entanglement — Skid Row:** **Forrest Stuart's** ethnography documented "**therapeutic policing**" — police arrest and ticket homeless people for minor offenses (jaywalking, loitering) as leverage into shelters/rehab, which the homeless experience as *harassment*. More broadly, lower-SES people are *more visible, less powerful, and more likely to be apprehended and labeled*, and more likely to encounter the criminal justice system as both victims and perpetrators — entanglements that stack further barriers to stability.
- **Invisibility and lack of political clout:** at least **2.5–3.5 million Americans (~1% of the population) experience homelessness each year**, plus 7.4 million doubled up, and the Census *undercounts* the street homeless — so the scale of need for housing resources is understated; the poor/homeless are politically **disenfranchised** (vote less, lack clout), so their needs draw little political response. *Example:* John Coleman, undercover as a homeless man in Manhattan for 10 days, had his own accountant, landlord, and co-worker look right through him — people avert their eyes out of discomfort and guilt.

## Race and Ethnicity

### 1. Define race and ethnicity. Why do sociologists view them as social constructs?
- **Race** = a socially defined category based on real or perceived **biological** differences between groups of people. It is not a biological reality — there are no "pure" races and no race chromosome.
- **Ethnicity** = a socially defined category based on a common **language, religion, nationality, history, or some other cultural factor**. *Example:* the Scotch-Irish are a distinct American ethnic group (Vance, *Hillbilly Elegy*, 2016); Jewish people are an ethnic group, not a race, since a blond, blue-eyed Scandinavian Jew and a Black Ethiopian Jew share the culture.
- **Social construction** = something is real, meaningful, or valuable when society tells us it is:
  - What we take for granted as real, natural, or self-evident is assigned **different meanings and values in different cultural contexts** (*Example:* children are protected today; they were not a few hundred years ago).
  - Human beings act toward ideas, concepts, and values on the basis of the meaning those things have for them.
  - These **meanings are a product of social interaction** in human society, modified through an interpretative process each individual uses in dealing with outward signs.
- **Science shows race has no biological basis:** 19th-century biologists grouped humans into three "races" (Negroid, Mongoloid, Caucasoid) believed to have separate biological makeups, but modern science rejects this — all humans are **99.9% genetically identical**, and there is **greater genetic diversity within racial populations than between them**. Physical differences are geographic adaptations: melanin near the equator (sun protection), less melanin near the poles (to absorb enough sunlight to produce vitamin D).
- **The "white" boundary has shifted over time** — evidence race is social, not fixed: early 1900s native-born white Protestants did not consider Irish, Italian, or Jewish immigrants white (Brodkin 1999; Ignatiev 1996, 2008); after WWII skin color became the main white/nonwhite line; today Arabs and Muslims are being symbolically labeled "nonwhite" in the post-9/11 climate.
- *Example:* Stuart Hall's two-and-a-half-year-old son, told "You're Black," replied "No. I'm brown" — if race were biological, the boy would be right; race is instead a social category framed in terms of biological features.
- **Bottom line:** race and ethnicity are not biological facts — they are categories whose meanings are created, maintained, and modified through social processes in which we all take part.

### 2. Distinguish between symbolic and situational identities. Provide examples.
- **Symbolic identity (symbolic ethnicity)** = an ethnic identity that is an **expression of group membership** but is **relevant only on specific occasions** and does not significantly affect everyday life.
  - *Examples:* St. Patrick's Day (parades, green beer, Chicago's green river, corned beef and cabbage), Passover, Cinco de Mayo, Nowruz.
- **Situational identity (situational ethnicity)** = an ethnic identity that people **deliberately assert in some situations while downplaying it in others** — a **cost-benefit analysis** in which the identity is displayed or concealed depending on its usefulness in a given situation.
  - *Example:* Dr. Ferris's Lebanese ancestry — downplayed in Southern California because of a political climate of suspicion toward Arabs, but treated as a valuable asset when she moved to Peoria, Illinois, where a large Lebanese-descent population included the mayor, a councilman, state senator, congressman, and prominent families.
- **The catch (Mary Waters 1990):** "The social and political consequences of being Asian or Hispanic or black are not, for the most part, symbolic, nor are they voluntary. They are real, unavoidable, and sometimes hurtful."
- **Key implication:** **white people have "ethnic options"; nonwhite people do not** — for minorities, visible group membership is not a choice.

### 3. Define majority and minority groups. How are they different from numerical majorities/minorities?
- **Minority group** = a social group that is **systematically denied the same access to power and resources** available to society's dominant group(s).
- **Majority (dominant) group** = the group that holds that access to power and resources — the standard to which minority groups are denied equal access.
- **Minority status is NOT about numbers:** the terms refer to **power and access, not population size** — members of a minority group are **not necessarily fewer in number** than the dominant group.
  - *Example:* South Africa under apartheid — Black people outnumbered whites **7:1**, yet a small white minority controlled the country until Mandela's 1994 election.
  - *Example:* California — a "majority-minority" state (2019: 37% white; nonwhite groups together >60%), yet whites remain the dominant group in power, resources, and representation; Hispanics/Latinos are underrepresented in the UC system and state government but overrepresented in prisons, poverty counts, and violent-crime victimization.
- **Demographic trend reinforces the point:** whites were **85% of the U.S. population in 1965 → 60% today → a projected 49.7% by 2045**, making the U.S. a majority-minority country (Frey 2018b) — a group can lose its numerical majority and still dominate.

### 4. Distinguish between racism, prejudice, and discrimination.
- **Racism** = an **ideology or set of beliefs** about the claimed superiority of one racial or ethnic group over another; used to justify inequality, often rooted in the assumption that differences are innate/genetic (Kendi 2016). It provides the ideological support for unequal social arrangements.
- **Prejudice** = a "prejudgment" — an **inflexible attitude**: an idea about the characteristics of a group that is applied to all members of that group and is **unlikely to change regardless of the evidence** against it. Usually negative, but can be positive; it can also flow from minorities toward the dominant group or toward one's own group (internalized racism, Pyke 2010), and may be absorbed unknowingly through socialization. *Example:* "All Asians are good at math."
- **Discrimination** = **behavior** — an action that results in **unequal treatment of individuals** based on their membership in a social group, usually motivated by prejudice.
- **Key distinctions:** racism is an **ideology** (a system of beliefs about group superiority), prejudice is an **attitude** (an inflexible generalization), and discrimination is an **action** (unequal treatment).
- **They do not always go together:**
  - **Prejudice without discrimination:** Merton's "timid bigots" (1949) — prejudiced people who do not act on it, e.g., a teacher who believes a stereotype but does not grade accordingly.
  - **Discrimination without prejudice:** a non-prejudiced person carries out institutional policies with discriminatory outcomes, e.g., promoting by seniority privileges dominant-group members who have longer tenure.
- **Related concept:** **implicit bias** = unrecognized or unconscious prejudices and stereotypes that shape interactions. *Example:* pediatricians give white patients more pain management than Black patients (Sabin and Greenwald 2012).

### 5. Distinguish between individual and institutional discrimination. Provide examples.
- **Individual discrimination** = discrimination carried out by **one person against another** — when one person treats others unfairly because of their race or ethnicity.
  - *Example:* a racist teacher grading a Hispanic student down.
- **Institutional discrimination** = **systematic and widespread** discrimination that occurs when **institutions** (government, schools, banks) practice **discriminatory policies that affect whole groups** of individuals — it reinforces itself.
  - *Example:* Ferguson, Missouri (2014) — after police killed unarmed Michael Brown and a grand jury declined to indict, a DOJ investigation found the police department routinely violated Black residents' constitutional rights; in a city that was 67% Black, Black residents were 85% of vehicle stops, 88% of use-of-force cases, and 93% of arrests.
  - *Example:* FHA redlining (see Q6).
- **Key difference:** individual discrimination is one person's act of unequal treatment, which can be stopped by changing that person's behavior; institutional discrimination is systematic — carried out by institutions through policies that affect whole groups of individuals, independent of any single person's prejudice.

### 6. What is redlining. Is it an example of individual or institutional discrimination? Explain.
- **Redlining** = the practice in which **Black neighborhoods were designated on federal maps**, and the **Federal Housing Authority (FHA) in the 1930s explicitly refused to insure mortgages for Black borrowers** — in both Black and white neighborhoods (Rothstein, *The Color of Law*, 2017).
- **Effects:** this policy **severely restricted Black homeownership**; redlining is now illegal, **but the segregation it produced continues** today.
- **Classification:** redlining is an example of **institutional discrimination**.
- **Why:**
  - It was **carried out systematically by an institution** — the federal government's Federal Housing Authority — not by one person acting unfairly against another.
  - It operated through an official **policy that affected whole groups** of individuals (Black borrowers generally), matching the definition of institutional discrimination: discrimination carried out systematically by institutions such as government, schools, and banks through policies that affect whole groups (the chapter lists "FHA redlining" as its example of institutional discrimination, alongside the Ferguson Police Department).
  - It is **not individual discrimination**, which is one person treating another unfairly because of race or ethnicity (e.g., a racist teacher grading a Hispanic student down) — redlining did not depend on individual prejudice but on government policy.

### 7. What is colorblind racism? Contrast it with race consciousness.
- **Color-blind racism** (Eduardo Bonilla-Silva 2003/2017, 2020) = an ideology that removes race as an explanation for any form of unequal treatment — it rests on the belief that we live in a society where prejudice and discrimination no longer exist.
- **How it works** = the claim "I don't see race." Although it sounds good, Bonilla-Silva argues it is a **new, more "civilized" form of racism** — it denies that race matters and attributes any remaining differences to **nonracial dynamics**.
- **Race consciousness** = an ideology that acknowledges race as a powerful social construct that shapes individual and social experiences; the chapter presents it as **the alternative to color blindness**.
- **Contrast — what each ideology does with race:**
  - Color blindness **removes/denies race** as an explanation for unequal treatment — it treats the problem of discrimination as already solved.
  - Race consciousness **acknowledges race as a powerful factor in everyday life and institutions** and asks us to recognize **one's own privilege as part of the structure of inequality**.
- *Example:* the argument "We elected a Black president" as proof of a postracial society — Bonilla-Silva's point: electing a Black president ≠ a postracial world.
- **Context:** the concept grows out of the study of privilege (roots in W.E.B. Du Bois 1903; reintroduced by Peggy McIntosh 1988 as the "invisible knapsack") — whites enjoy unearned advantages they are largely unaware of, and color blindness lets that structure go unnamed.

### 8. What is cultural appropriation? What are its effects? Provide examples
- **Cultural appropriation** = the adoption of cultural elements belonging to an **oppressed group** by members of the **dominant group**, **without permission** and often **for the dominant group's gain**. Lecture shorthand: in other words, **"cultural steal."**
- **How it happens** = the dominant group **adopts, co-opts, or takes cultural elements** — art, music, dance, dress, language, rituals — from a marginalized group **for its own advantage**.
- **Effects:**
  - **Profit and advantage accrue to the dominant group**, which turns an oppressed group's symbols into **commodities for profit** — described in the chapter as "postmodern cultural imperialism."
  - **Sacred items are used disrespectfully:** *Example:* Native American headdresses and sweat lodges treated as costumes or novelties.
  - **The exchange is one-sided and nonconsensual** — elements are taken *without permission*, so the group that created the culture neither agrees nor benefits while the dominant group does.
- **Examples:**
  - *Example:* Halloween costumes and college theme parties that adopt other cultures as dress-up.
  - *Example:* fashion runways borrowing Native American style — suede, fringe, moccasins, turquoise.
  - *Example:* **Native mascots** — Cleveland's MLB team did not drop the "Indians" name and mascot until **2021**; an oppressed group's symbols were sold as commodities.
  - *Example:* **Hollywood "whitewashing"** — Matt Damon cast as a Chinese warlord (*The Great Wall*), Scarlett Johansson as a Japanese anime character (*Ghost in the Shell*), and Emma Stone as a multiracial Asian character (*Aloha*) — all widely criticized.

### 9. How do functionalist, conflict, and symbolic interaction theorists explain race and racism? Compare and contrast.
- **Functionalism** (macro; social order and cohesion):
  - Functionalist theories on groups **focus on social solidarity and group cohesion**. Prejudice is rooted in **ethnocentrism** — each group treats its own culture as "right and normal"; positive feelings bind a group together, but that cohesion breeds **unfavorable views of outsiders**.
  - **Cultural differences and the lack of integration** of minorities into the larger society **tend to feed fear and hostility**.
  - Table 8.1: racial inequality "has functions that maintain social order" — e.g., it creates cohesion within both the dominant group and minority groups.
  - Strength/limitation: functionalism best explains how **European immigrant groups (Irish, Italians) assimilated**, but it is **less successful at explaining why Black and Hispanic Americans maintain distinct identities** — because it treats any persistent phenomenon, including racial inequality, as contributing to social stability.
- **Conflict theory** (macro; power and economic competition):
  - Focuses on the **struggle for power and control**; racism is partly **driven by economic competition and struggle over scarce resources**.
  - **Edna Bonacich (1980):** the **"split labor market"** — one group of workers routinely paid less — keeps minority wages low, compounding racism with poverty.
  - **William Julius Wilson (1980):** early racist government policies and individual racism/discrimination created a **Black underclass**, but **economic factors, not racial ones**, now perpetuate it.
  - **Tomás Almaguer,** *Racial Fault Lines* (2008): racist beliefs became **institutionalized and part of political and economic life** — 19th-century California's racial hierarchy put whites on top, then Mexicans, Black Americans, Asians, and American Indians at the bottom; "manifest destiny" justified taking land, and labeling groups "uncivilized heathens" justified killing.
  - **Omi and Winant (1994, 2015):** race is not secondary to class — it **permeates lived experience, the economy, and government**. Extensions: **intersectionality** (Patricia Hill Collins 2006, bell hooks 1990, Gloria Anzaldúa 1987 — race experienced as it intersects with class and gender) and **critical race theory** (Bell 1980; Delgado and Stefancic 2012; Williams 1991), developed in the 1980s by legal scholars, holding that racism permeates institutions, especially the judicial system.
  - Table 8.1: differences create intergroup conflict — *Example:* white people (and men) object to affirmative action programs.
- **Symbolic interactionism** (micro; meaning and identity in everyday life):
  - Race and ethnicity are **part of our identity and are displayed as part of our self-presentation**; race is **created and interpreted in everyday life**, and its meanings change over time.
  - **Omi and Winant:** which features are chosen to make racial distinctions "is always and necessarily a **social and historical process**," and the definitions are "at best imprecise and at worst completely arbitrary" — yet race is a "fundamental dimension of social organization and cultural meaning in the U.S."
  - *Example:* **Stuart Hall's son** — told "You're Black," the two-and-a-half-year-old replied "No. I'm brown": if race were biological the boy would be right, but race is a *social* category framed in terms of biological features.
  - Key concepts: **passing** = living as if one is a member of a different racial category; **embodied identities** = elements of identity generated through others' perceptions of our physical traits; **code-switching** = adjusting behavior (especially language) to the dominant group's norms.
  - Table 8.1: some people (white ethnics, light-skinned nonwhites) can conceal their race/ethnicity, escaping inequality **as individuals** without erasing it from society.
- **Compare and contrast:**
  - **Level of analysis:** functionalism and conflict theory are macro theories about society-wide cohesion vs. power struggles; symbolic interactionism is micro, examining face-to-face interaction, meaning, and identity.
  - **Why racism exists:** functionalism — ethnocentrism and lack of integration feed hostility, and persistent inequality is read as serving stability; conflict — economic competition over scarce resources and struggles for power; symbolic interactionism — racial categories themselves are (re)created through everyday social and historical processes.
  - **Why inequality persists:** functionalism can rationalize inequality as functional for order (its weakness); conflict points to economic structure (Wilson) and institutionalized racist beliefs (Almaguer); symbolic interactionism shows individuals can slip past inequality (passing, concealment) while the structure itself goes unchanged.
  - **Overlap:** conflict-tradition intersectionality scholars (Collins, hooks, Anzaldúa) hold that race is **created symbolically in everyday interactions — sharing common ground with interactionism**; and both functionalism (cohesion) and interactionism (identity) recognize race/ethnicity as a source of group belonging and solidarity.

### 10. What are the effects of race and ethnicity on life chances? Be specific.
- **Family:**
  - **Marriage:** 52% of white households and 47% of Hispanic households include a married couple, but only **28% of Black households** do (Census 2020a).
  - **Family structure:** in 2020, **79% of white children, 68% of Hispanic children, and 41% of Black children** lived with two parents — nearly half of Black children live with their mother only.
  - **Teen birth rates (2019, per 1,000):** white **11**, Black **26**, Hispanic **25**. Angela Y. Davis (2001): Black teenage girls see fewer educational/work opportunities and choose motherhood — only attacking racism in education and the workforce will change that.
  - Kathryn Edin and Maria Kefalas (2005): low-income women of all ethnicities see marriage as offering few benefits given the men they are likely to encounter.
- **Health:**
  - **Life expectancy (2018):** white males 76 and white females 81 vs. Black males 71 and Black females 78 — while **Hispanics live longest** (79 for men, 84 for women).
  - **Maternal and infant mortality:** Black mothers die from pregnancy complications at a rate **more than 3×** that of other mothers; **infant mortality for Black babies is more than 2×** that of white babies.
  - **"Weathering"** (Geronimus et al. 2006): exposure to racism erodes health — racially differentiated stressors produce more chronic disease and lower life expectancy; minorities also face more workplace dangers, environmental toxins, and violence.
  - **Covid-19 was not the "great equalizer":** Black Americans were **2×** more likely to die than whites, Hispanics **2.3×**, American Indians **2.4×** — due to essential/frontline work, crowded housing, unequal access to care, and discrimination in the health-care system.
  - **Uninsured (2018, nonelderly):** **19% of Hispanics and 12% of Black people** vs. 8% of whites and 7% of Asians.
- **Education:**
  - **High school graduation (2019):** Asian **93%**, white **89%**, Hispanic **82%**, Black **80%**; the Hispanic dropout rate (8%) is nearly 2× that of white students.
  - **Bachelor's degrees (2020, ages 25+):** Asian **61%**, white **41%**, Black **28%**, Hispanic **21%**.
  - **School-to-prison pipeline** (Victor Rios 2017): Latino youth misread by teachers and school authorities get labeled "bad," "deviant," "at risk," face greater scrutiny and punishment, and are criminalized.
  - **Stereotype threat** (Claude Steele 2010): in demanding, high-stakes situations the fear of confirming negative stereotypes hampers Black students' performance, though they perform as well as peers when unstressed; **stereotype promise** (Lee and Zhou 2015): the positive "model minority" stereotype boosts Asian American students in the same situations.
- **Income and work:**
  - **Median household income (2019):** Asian **$98,174**, white **$76,057**, Hispanic **$56,113**, Black **$45,438** — Black households earn **60%** and Hispanic households **74%** of white households' median income.
  - **Poverty (2019):** **9% of whites, 19% of African Americans, 16% of Hispanics.**
  - **Employment discrimination** (Bertrand and Mullainathan 2004 résumé experiment): identical résumés with white-sounding names (Emily, Greg) got **50% more callbacks** than Black-sounding names (Lakisha, Jamal); added qualifications helped white applicants **30% more**.
  - **Occupational segregation (2020):** 37% of home health aides and 38% of postal clerks were Black; Hispanics were 46% of construction workers and 46% of maids/housecleaners — concentration in semiskilled/unskilled jobs.
  - **Housing and credit:** *Example:* Patricia Williams (1997) — a Black law professor with an excellent credit history was approved for a mortgage over the phone, but when she corrected her race from "white" to "African American" the bank demanded a bigger down payment and higher interest rate. "Membership in a racial category carries a high price."
- **Criminal justice:**
  - **Imprisonment (2018):** the prison population was **33% Black, 30% white, 23% Hispanic** even though whites are ~60% of the U.S. population — **Black men are imprisoned at a rate nearly 6× that of white men**.
  - **Stops and frisks (ACLU 2008):** Black and Hispanic Americans are **127% more likely to be stopped** and **43% more likely to be frisked** — yet **42% (Black) and 32% (Hispanic) less likely** to be carrying a weapon or drugs.
  - **Racial profiling on the roads:** *Example:* in Missouri (2017 report), Black drivers were **85% more likely to be pulled over** and, when stopped, **51% more likely to be searched**; in Oakland, Black people were 28% of the population but **60% of police stops**; Black men are **2.5× more likely to be killed by police** than white men (Edwards, Lee, and Esposito 2019).
  - **Institutional discrimination:** *Example:* Ferguson, Missouri (2014) — a city that was 67% Black, where Black residents were **85% of vehicle stops, 88% of use-of-force cases, and 93% of arrests**.
  - **Sentencing — crack vs. powder cocaine:** until 2010, 5 grams of crack (disproportionately used by Black people) carried the same 10-year mandatory sentence as 500 grams of powder (100:1 disparity); in 2010, **79% of federally sentenced crack offenders were Black** though two-thirds of crack users were white or Hispanic. The Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 reduced the disparity to **18:1** — still 18× harsher.
  - **Victimization and hate crimes:** in 2018, **54% of murder victims were Black** though Black people are 13% of the population; over half of 2019 hate crimes were race/ethnicity-based, with **48% targeting Black Americans** (16% white, 14% Hispanic, 4% Asian).

### 11. Distinguish between assimilation and pluralism. What are some criticisms of assimilation?
- **Assimilation** = a pattern of intergroup relations in which the **minority group is absorbed into the dominant group**, making society more homogeneous — the "**melting pot**." The logic: if everyone belongs to the same group, conflict decreases.
  - **Racial assimilation** = absorption into the dominant group **through intermarriage**, until races are mixed.
  - **Cultural assimilation** = absorption **by adopting the dominant group's culture**. Often both happen at once.
  - *Example:* twentieth-century immigrants — Irish, Italians, and Eastern Europeans were once "ethnics" but assimilated so fully that they are now practically unrecognizable as distinct groups, absorbed into the category of "white Americans."
- **Pluralism** = a cultural pattern of intergroup relations that **encourages racial and ethnic variation and acceptance** within a society — the "**salad bowl**," in which ingredients keep their distinct qualities; also called **multiculturalism**, with **tolerance** as its core value.
  - *Example:* Canada as the model — two official linguistic groups (English and French) plus European, Chinese, and Indian immigrants and First Nations peoples, with government funding for multicultural programs.
- **Key distinction:** assimilation pushes toward **homogeneity** — the minority drops its distinct culture and is absorbed into the dominant group; pluralism preserves **heterogeneity with acceptance** — groups keep their distinct qualities and are valued for them. The chapter ranks five patterns of intergroup relations from most violent to most tolerant: genocide → population transfer → settler colonialism/segregation → **assimilation** → **pluralism (most tolerant)**.
- **Criticisms of assimilation:**
  - **It is not always voluntary — often forced:** groups may be *forced* to give up their religion and language; *Example:* many American Indians lost tribal languages and cultural practices within a few generations.
  - **It is one-way absorption, not mutual blending:** the melting pot really means minorities are absorbed *into the dominant group* — society becomes homogeneous on the dominant group's terms.
  - **Limited applicability:** it describes European immigrant groups well, but it is **less successful at explaining why Black and Hispanic Americans maintain distinct identities** — the functionalist framework built around assimilation fails on this point.
  - **It is not equally available to everyone:** assimilation assumes a group can shed its distinctness and blend in, but for nonwhite people membership is not a choice — per Mary Waters (1990), the consequences of being Asian, Hispanic, or Black are "**real, unavoidable, and sometimes hurtful**," while white people enjoy "ethnic options."
  - **It ranks below pluralism in tolerance:** assimilation eliminates racial and ethnic variation rather than accepting it, which is why the chapter places pluralism — not assimilation — at the most tolerant end of the spectrum.

## Gender and Sexuality

### 1. Distinguish between sex and gender.
- **Core distinction:** **sex is biological; gender is social/cultural**. Gender identity and gender expression may differ from the sex assigned at birth — which raises the question of which set of attributes is most consequential to everyday life.
- **Sex** = an individual's membership in one of two biological categories — male or female — based on **chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive organs** (primary sex characteristics), plus secondary sex characteristics such as facial/body hair, musculature, and bone structure (Table 9.1: females — XX, estrogen; males — XY, testosterone).
- **Intersex** = a person whose chromosomes or sex characteristics are neither exclusively male nor exclusively female — about **17 babies per 1,000** are born intersex (Fausto-Sterling 2000). Georgiann Davis (2015) argues it should be treated as a **biological variation**, not a medical disorder.
- **Gender** = the physical, behavioral, and personality traits that a group considers normal, natural, right, and good for its male and female members — what a society deems appropriately "masculine" or "feminine." No society leaves it completely up to nature.
- **Gender identity** = our deeply held sense of ourselves as male, female, or some other gender; it may or **may not correspond** to the sex we were assigned at birth.
- **Cisgender** = the term used when gender identity and/or expression aligns with the sex assigned at birth (*cis* = "same"); **transgender** = when identity and/or expression differs from the sex assigned at birth (trans identity is not dependent on medical procedures).
- **Gender nonconforming** = gender identity or expression that differs from societal expectations about gender roles; nonbinary people do not identify as exclusively men or women.
- **Why it matters:** because the biological and social categories can diverge, sociologists now think of sex, gender, and sexuality not as binaries but as **existing on a spectrum**.
- *Example:* a child assigned male at birth (sex) may hold a female gender identity and express femininity through clothing and mannerisms — the biological category alone does not determine the social ones.

### 2. What does gender expression refer to? Provide examples.
- **Gender expression** = an individual's external, behavioral manifestations of gender — clothing, grooming, behavior, body language, gestures, and even names — the outward presentation of gender to others.
- **Function:** expression signals gender so that "people know how to interact with the expresser."
- **Distinct from identity:** gender expression is not the same as gender identity or the sex assigned at birth, and it may or may not align with either.
- **Can change:** gender expression can be altered — e.g., in a **transition**, a transgender person may change their clothing, grooming, and name to align their expression with their gender identity rather than their assigned sex.
- **Can be nonconforming:** when expression differs from societal expectations about gender roles, it is **gender nonconforming**; nonbinary individuals (genderqueer, gender fluid, bigender) don't fit neatly into binary classifications. Use the term an individual prefers.
- *Example:* wearing dresses, makeup, and long hair versus suits and short hair; mannerisms and body language; a chosen name — e.g., a child assigned female at birth who insists on "boy" clothes and a short haircut is expressing gender in a nonconforming way, while a transgender woman changing her clothing, hair, and name during transition uses expression to present her gender identity.

### 3. Distinguish between essentialist and constructivist arguments? How do each view gender? Why do sociologists rely on the latter?
- **Essentialists** = those who believe gender roles have a genetic or biological origin and therefore **cannot be changed**. They see gender as an immutable, biological, unambiguous **two-category system** (the **gender binary**): chromosomes, hormones, and genitalia determine identity, and culture plays little to no role. This view is common in medicine, theology, and biology.
- **Constructivists (constructionists)** = those who believe notions of gender are **socially determined** — that the binary system is just **one possibility among many**. Gender is a social construction: it is **symbolic**, shaped by culture and historical period, and the meaning of masculinity/femininity differs across societies.
- **How each views gender:** essentialists treat gender as fixed by biology into two distinct, opposite, separate categories that cannot change; constructivists treat gender as learned, performed, and variable — society produces the categories themselves.
- **Evidence supporting the constructivist view** = gender categories demonstrably vary across cultures (the chapter's Global Perspective):
  - **Berdaches / "Two-Spirit" people:** individuals neither male nor female but somehow both, documented in nearly 150 Native cultures — biological males doing "feminine" work or females who hunted and waged war.
  - **Hijras (South Asia):** a modern third-gender group; in 2012 Pakistan added a **third-gender option to national identity cards**.
  - **Bacha Posh (Afghanistan):** girls dressed and raised as boys in families with no sons.
- These groups are **not the same as Western "transgender"** — they show that **gender is malleable** and that characteristics we think of as definitive may be viewed differently in other cultures and time periods, "which means it should be possible to view them differently here and now."
- **Why sociologists rely on the constructivist approach:** a sociological perspective reveals the **cultural and environmental influences on supposedly biological identities**; because gender's meaning shifts across societies and history, biology alone cannot explain it. Moreover, if gender categories are socially constructed, they can be socially deconstructed and reconstructed — which is exactly what makes them open to sociological study and critique.

### 4. What is gender role socialization? Provide an example from your own life.
- **Gender role socialization** = the lifelong process of learning what constitutes masculinity and femininity — "learning to be masculine or feminine" — carried out primarily through the four agents of socialization: **families, schools, peers, and the media** (religion may also play a part).
- **Effects:** it perpetuates both **binary ideas of gender and gender conformity** and **heteronormativity** (the belief that heterosexuality is and should be the norm).
- **Mechanism:** **social learning**, i.e., learning behaviors and meanings through social interaction — norms are picked up through modelling, rewards, and teaching that can be **intentional** (e.g., "big boys don't cry") or **unintentional** (observation, imitation, play). By age two children are aware of their own and others' gender; by age three they identify specific traits associated with each gender.
- **Scope:** it is subtle and pervasive, begins early (families even before birth — see Q7), and continues throughout life as the agents keep reinforcing gendered expectations.
- *Example (generic — replace with your own):* a brother is given toy trucks and action figures and told "big boys don't cry" when upset, while a sister is given dolls and praised for being "ladylike" and helping in the kitchen. No one gives a formal lesson, but through everyday interactions, rewards, and role models each child learns which behaviors, clothes, and interests count as feminine versus masculine.

### 5. Define sexuality/sexual identity
- **Sexuality** = the character or quality of being sexual — a person's sexual behavior, desires, and fantasies.
- **Sexual identity / sexual orientation** = the inclination to feel sexual desire toward people of a particular gender (or both genders); also defined as the inclination to desire and engage sexually with persons of a particular sex or gender.
- **Heterosexuality** = sexual attraction toward members of the other gender — the **normative, dominant** category ("straight"), with privileges attached.
- **Homosexuality** = sexual attraction toward members of one's own gender — a minority category, with about **4% of adults** identifying as gay or lesbian (Reece et al. 2010).
- **Bisexuality** = sexual attraction toward members of both genders — about **3% of men and 4% of women** (Reece et al. 2010).
- **Asexuality** = the **lack of sexual attraction of any kind**, no interest in or desire for sex — about **1% of adults** (Bogaert 2004).
- **Percentages are contested:** a more recent CDC (2015) study reported 97.6% heterosexual, 1.6% gay/lesbian, and 0.8% bisexual.
- **Not fixed:** sexuality is not necessarily a permanent either/or — Alfred Kinsey (1940s) showed people fall along a wide spectrum rather than in exclusive categories (see Q6).
- **LGBTQIA** = lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, asexual — the "A" stands for **asexual, not "allies."**

### 6. What is the Kinsey Scale? How does it view sexuality? Why is this approach useful?
- **Alfred Kinsey (1940s)** found through his research that human sexuality is **far more diverse than previously assumed** — people are **not exclusively heterosexual or homosexual** but fall along a wide spectrum.
- **The Kinsey Scale** = the resulting framework showing that sexual orientation is best understood as a **fluid continuum**, not as binary, fixed categories.
- **How it views sexuality:** as a spectrum running between exclusive heterosexuality and exclusive homosexuality, on which individuals can fall anywhere — and as something that **can change over a lifetime** rather than being set permanently.
- **Why the approach is useful:** it captures the real diversity of human sexuality that simple "gay/straight" labels miss — consistent with documented variation (~4% gay/lesbian, ~3–4% bisexual, ~1% asexual) — and it fits sociologists' broader move to think of sex, gender, and sexuality as **existing on a spectrum** rather than as binaries.
- **Queer theory (extension):** goes further than Kinsey: emerging in the 1990s, it **rejects ideas of innate identities or restrictive categories** altogether and emphasizes the **importance of difference** (Butler 1993; Sedgwick 1993). Being queer is about "possibilities, gaps, overlaps, dissonances, and resonances" — any kind of categorization is seen as outmoded and limiting. "Queer" now describes anything that **challenges prevailing binary notions of sex, gender, and sexuality** — hence the "Q" in LGBTQ.

### 7. What are the four agents of socialization? How do they contribute to gender role socialization? Be specific.
- **The four agents** = **families, schools, peers, and the media** (religion may also play a part). Together they carry out gender role socialization — the lifelong process of learning masculinity and femininity — and in doing so they perpetuate binary gender ideas, gender conformity, and **heteronormativity**.
- **Family:** gender role socialization begins **even before birth**: knowing a fetus's sex changes how the mother talks to it (word choice, tone) (Kara Smith 2005). After birth, children receive gendered clothes, rooms, toys, and stories. Norms are learned through interaction, modelling, and rewards, and teaching is both intentional ("big boys don't cry") and unintentional (observation, imitation, play); gender pervades chores, discipline, and where children may go. Family members serve as role models into adulthood — influencing career, mate choice, and how we raise our own children.
- **Schools:** girls and boys are placed in **same-sex groups** and given gender-stereotyped tasks early on (Love and Kelly 2011; Thorne 1993); by fifth grade gender norms are firmly established, even in co-ed schools. Teachers **statistically tend to favor boys** — boys receive more attention and instructional time, are called on more, get more challenging questions and more praise for work quality (though they are punished more for misbehaving) (Smith 1999). Textbooks can be sexist or stereotypical (Robson 2001), and women are concentrated in **lower-level positions** (teachers/aides) while men hold administration — schooling reinforces stereotypes structurally. **Heteronormativity pervades school life:** textbook images of traditional families, prom kings and queens, and binary restrooms that are problematic for trans students.
- **Peers:** children engage in **gender-segregated play**: by age three they prefer same-sex playmates — preschoolers are **3× more likely** and kindergartners **11× more likely** to play with same-sex peers (Maccoby and Jacklin 1987) — and same-sex peer groups appear to *create* gendered behavior rather than reflect it (Fabes, Martin, and Hanish 2003). **Peer groups police gender norms:** children, especially boys, are mocked by peers for crossing gendered borders (Thorne 1993); C. J. Pascoe's *Dude, You're a Fag* (2007) shows boys policing each other's masculinity by lobbing homophobic epithets, and girls bully through homophobic labeling, sexual rumors, and "slut shaming" (Sarah Miller 2016). Children gain prestige along gendered lines — boys through athletic ability, humor, risk-taking, and defying norms (Kimmel 2009); girls through social position and physical attractiveness — and dating pressures enforce heteronormativity into early adulthood.
- **Media:** sex-role behavior in media is **very stereotypical**: girls should be beautiful, caring, sensitive, and reserved; boys assertive, strong, and analytic (Barner 1999). From movies, comic books, and popular music, children learn "how to behave, how to be accepted, what to value, and what is normal." **Sexual diversity is not common and is often portrayed negatively:** it was absent from TV before the 1960s, there was **no gay male kiss on network TV until 2000**, and trans characters are still often cast as villains, victims, or sex workers. Turning points include *Ellen* (1997) and *Will & Grace* (1999), with later visibility from shows like *Orange Is the New Black* (Laverne Cox) and *Billions* (Asia Kate Dillon). Because media may be people's only exposure to LGBTQ people, realistic portrayals matter — the **contact hypothesis** (Pettigrew and Tropp 2011) holds that more contact between groups reduces prejudice. Media also **socialize families, peers, and educators**, giving it an even more overarching power.

### 8. Define sexism. Define patriarchy.
- **Sexism** = the belief that one sex (usually male) is superior to the other. It functions as an **ideology** — a belief system used to justify gender inequality and reinforce the status quo — and it persists in all contemporary cultures.
- **Misogyny** = an ingrained prejudice against women — dislike, contempt, or hatred of women — the extreme form of sexism. (Related: **misogynoir** (Bailey 2010, 2021) = the unique anti-Black sexism Black women experience, sexism intersecting with racism.)
- **Patriarchy** = literally "rule of the father" — a **male-dominated society**; male domination. Gender inequality in all past and present societies invariably takes this form, and there is **little evidence a matriarchal (female-dominated) society ever existed** (the Vanatinai of New Guinea come closest, sharing access to prestige and power).
- Patriarchy is maintained in part through **male privilege** = unearned advantages accorded to men as members of the dominant group. *Example:* men may take up more physical space, aren't expected to smile at all times, and are less likely to be interrupted while speaking.
- *Origin vs. persistence:* early societies channeled hunting/warfare (scarce, prized resources) to men, who gained power by controlling distribution — but this account **does not explain persistence**, since physical strength is no longer required in most jobs and women are not restricted by childbearing demands. Sexism is what keeps justifying the arrangement today.

### 9. What are examples of institutional and individual discrimination faced by women and LGBTQ individuals? (hint: can be different for each group)
- **Discrimination** = unequal treatment of individuals based on their membership in a social group. Individual acts that are cumulative or reproduced across workplaces and industries become **institutional**.

**For women:**
- **Institutional (built into economic, legal, and organizational structures):**
  - **The gender pay gap:** women earn about **82 cents for every dollar a man earns**; compounded by race — Black women earn 68 cents and Hispanic women 62 cents per dollar a white man earns. Causes: occupational segregation, the motherhood/housework burden, and ongoing discrimination.
  - **Occupational segregation and the undervaluing of "women's work":** female-dominated fields (education, health services) are underpaid — women are 98.8% of preschool/kindergarten teachers but only 5.6% of aircraft pilots; male-dominated occupations carry more prestige and pay.
  - **The promotion ceiling:** women occupy a **minority position in power** even though they are a numerical majority — earning less for the same job and **passed over for promotion** (the "glass-ceiling" pattern). In academia and schools, women are concentrated at lower levels (teachers/aides) while men hold administration.
  - **Feminization of poverty:** women are more likely than men to live in poverty — 11.5% of women vs. 9.4% of men (2019) — driven by the wage gap, more single mothers, and child-care costs.
  - **The pandemic "she-cession":** for the first time ever, women lost more jobs than men in a recession — 5.4 million vs. 4.4 million (March–December 2020) — because Covid hit the female-dominated service sector.
  - **Sexist corporate culture:** the **Google memo (2017)** blaming biology for tech's gender gap; the Pinterest case (a female COO fired, $22.5 million settlement — "female executives... are marginalized, excluded, and silenced").
- **Individual (person-to-person):**
  - **Sexual harassment and assault:** the **MeToo** reckoning (the hashtag coined by Tarana Burke 2006) showed how deeply ingrained harassment is; at least **1 in 5 women in college** are victims of sexual assault.
  - **Everyday discrimination in transactions:** being **charged more for a car or home loan**; a **recruiter consistently favoring men**.
  - **Microaggressions and everyday sexism:** being told to **smile**, being interrupted, labeled **shrill or bossy**, catcalls.

**For LGBTQ individuals (distinct pattern — status- and violence-based):**
- **Institutional:**
  - **Employment discrimination:** where not prohibited by law, employers can still discriminate in hiring and pay — **11–28% of LGB workers** report losing a promotion because of sexual orientation; **27% of transgender workers** report being fired, not hired, or denied a promotion. Per worker, the rate of LGBTQ discrimination is **several times higher than for women**. The **Employment Non-Discrimination Act (ENDA)** has consistently failed to pass Congress.
  - **Historical marriage restrictions:** laws historically ratified **only heterosexual marriage**; until **Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)** same-sex marriage was banned in 13 states.
  - **Ongoing legal gaps:** discrimination in **housing, education, employment, the military, and health care** remains a post-Obergefell issue.
  - **Institutional criminal-justice vulnerability:** LGBTQ people are overrepresented in jails/prisons; **transgender inmates are >10× more likely to be sexually assaulted** than other prisoners, LGB inmates ~3×.
- **Individual (and peer-level):**
  - **Homophobia** = prejudice and discrimination toward gay, lesbian, and bisexual people; **transphobia** = prejudice and discrimination toward transgender or gender-nonconforming people. These are learned cultural prejudices, not clinical "phobias."
  - **Bullying and harassment at school:** more than **8 in 10 LGBTQ students** experienced harassment or assault (81% verbally harassed, 34% physically harassed, 15% physically assaulted); the lecture notes say **9 out of 10 LGBTQ children report bullying**.
  - **Hate crimes and bias-motivated violence:** bias-motivated attacks based on sexual orientation are the **third-highest hate-crime category** (after race and religion); in 2019 there were 1,195 anti–sexual orientation and 198 anti-transgender hate crimes; proportionally, **LGBTQ people are the most likely targets of hate crimes in America**.
  - **Coming out can feel unsafe** in an anti-gay, anti-trans society — fear of social/emotional repercussions and hate crimes; **parental/family rejection** after coming out.
  - **Microaggressions:** using "gay"/"queer" as insults, misgendering trans persons, and small acts that make LGBTQ people feel invisible.

### 10. What are microagressions? Provide an example. Why are they dangerous?
- **Microaggressions** = everyday uses of subtle verbal and nonverbal communication that convey **denigrating or dismissive messages** to members of social groups (Sue 2010). Crucially, they are often **unintended** — the speaker need not intend harm for the message to demean.
- *Example:* being told to **"smile."** Tennis star Serena Williams, asked about this after a hard-fought evening match: "It's 11:30. To be perfectly honest with you, I don't want to be here."
- *More examples from the notes:* being **interrupted** while speaking; being labeled **shrill or bossy**; **catcalls**; **misgendering** trans persons (accidentally or deliberately — refusing to use a chosen name or pronoun); using **"gay" or "queer" as insults**; a new acquaintance asking a woman **"what does your husband do?"**
- **Why they are dangerous:**
  - **They demean group members even when well-meaning** — their subtlety means the denigration is deniable, making them hard to call out or challenge.
  - **They reinforce sexist and heterosexist ideology in ordinary life:** male-as-default language (generic "he," "mankind") makes women feel **excluded and demeaned**, and small heterosexist assumptions (the "what does your husband do?" question) make LGBTQ people **feel invisible**.
  - **They are pervasive and constant:** they occur in everyday interaction, so members of marginalized groups face a steady drip of dismissal rather than isolated incidents.
  - **They reproduce inequality structurally:** individual acts of discrimination that are **cumulative or reproduced across workplaces and industries become institutional** — everyday slights accumulate into systematic disadvantage.
  - *The remedy the notes endorse:* allow people to self-identify and affirm it — use chosen names and preferred pronouns ("he"/"she"/neutral "they").

### 11. How do functionalist, conflict, and symbolic interaction theorists explain gender and sexuality? Compare and contrast.
**Structural Functionalism**
- **Core argument:** some social roles are better suited to one gender than the other, and societies are more stable when norms are fulfilled by the "appropriate" sex.
- **Parsons (Parsons and Bales 1955):** complementary roles within the family — the **instrumental role** = the member who provides material support (task-oriented "breadwinner," authority figure); the **expressive role** = the member who provides emotional support and nurturing (stabilizing the instrumental partner's personality against the competitive world). Women are "better suited" to the expressive role, men to the instrumental — so gender segregation upholds the traditional family.
- *Example (Table 9.2):* women are "naturally" nurturing → nurses and teachers of young children; men "naturally" logical → lawyers and computer programmers.
- **On sexuality:** the model family is the heterosexual one — laws historically ratified only heterosexual marriage, teaching that heterosexuality and biological parenting are "normal."
- **Critique:** the rewards are far from equal — feminine work is devalued as unskilled and instinctive; families are also sources of instability, violence, and abuse. The perspective has fallen out of favor in sociology.

**Conflict Theory**
- **Core argument:** men have historically had access to most of society's material resources and privileges, and consequently seek to **maintain their dominant status** — gender inequality is exploitation, like race and class inequality.
- **Engels (1884):** gender inequality is a **derivative of class inequality**, originating with **private property**. Capitalists benefit from patriarchal families two ways: women **reproduce the labor force without compensation** and serve as an inexpensive **"reserve army" of labor**. Abolish private property → classes disappear → no powerful interests forcing women into domestic roles. (家庭并非从来就有的，而是历史的产物，其本质由当时的经济制度决定。现代一夫一妻制建立在私有制和财产继承的需要之上。)
- **Eisenstein (1979):** all men benefit from gender inequality in the short term — without gender segregation they'd do more unpaid work or pay for it, face a larger, more competitive job market, and lose power and prestige as "breadwinners."
- *Example:* male-dominated occupations carry more prestige and pay, and men maintain that advantage (Table 9.2).
- **On sexuality:** prejudice against nonheterosexuals is backed by **heterosexism/cisgenderism** — ideological systems analogous to sexism/racism that stigmatize nonheterosexual and noncisgender identities, benefiting the dominant (heterosexual/cisgender) group.

**Symbolic Interactionism**
- **Core argument:** gender is **socially constructed and maintained in everyday life** — we can barely interact with anyone without first determining their gender identity; we need to categorize and be categorizable.
- **Garfinkel (1967/1984) and "Agnes":** a trans woman who had to learn to **"do being female"** — dress, makeup, body language, speech, deference to her male boss, plus extra precautions — showed that gender is something we **do**; we all enact gender the same way Agnes did.
- *Example:* the **"chilly climate"** in classrooms (Lee and McCabe 2021) — from 95 hours of observation, men students spoke **1.6× as often as women**, interrupted professors and classmates, and used more assertive language — gender status hierarchies reproduced in everyday talk.
- **On sexuality:** heterosexist assumptions saturate everyday encounters — the "what does your husband do?" question, "gay" used as an insult, misgendering — small acts that make LGBTQ people feel invisible; trans/nonbinary people who don't fit existing categories experience difficulty in interaction.
- **Feminist theory (an additional approach):** applies assumptions about gender inequality to social institutions (family, education, economy, media) to understand them anew and **"write women back in"** to scholarship. Related to conflict theory (focus on inequality) and interactionism (focus on lived experience); **Butler (1999), hooks (2003), MacKinnon (2005)** link gender inequality to race/ethnicity, class, and sexual orientation — **gender and power are inextricably intertwined**.

**Compare and contrast:**
- **Level of analysis:** functionalism and conflict theory are **macro** (social structures: the family, the economy); symbolic interactionism is **micro** (everyday face-to-face interaction); feminist theory bridges both and adds activism.
- **Gender difference as natural vs. constructed:** functionalism sees gendered role division as a **natural complementarity that stabilizes society** (a consensus view); conflict theory sees the same division as **exploitation that maintains male dominance** (an inequality view); symbolic interactionism treats gender not as a fixed role or structure but as an **ongoing accomplishment performed in interaction**; feminist theory sees it as a **power hierarchy intertwined with race, class, and sexuality**.
- **Why the division persists:** functionalism → because it keeps society stable; conflict theory → because men actively maintain their advantage; interactionism → because we constantly re-create gender (and its categories) in everyday life.
- **Overlaps:** conflict theory and feminist theory both center inequality and male domination; symbolic interactionism and feminist theory both take lived experience seriously; all except functionalism treat gender as socially produced rather than biologically fixed.
- *Scope note:* the notes' theory section covers the three theories of **gender** inequality; sexuality is addressed chiefly through socialization and heteronormativity, prejudice (homophobia/transphobia, heterosexism/cisgenderism), and life chances rather than through a separate theory of sexuality.

### 12. What are the effects of gender on life chances in terms of family and health? What are the effects of sexuality on life chances in terms of family and health? Be specific.
**Gender → Family**
- **Marriage patterns:** men are more likely than women to report **never having been married** (36% vs. 30%) — reflecting stronger societal pressure on women to marry; women are more likely to be **widowed** (8% vs. <3%), partly because they live longer.
- **Divorce is harder for women, especially women with children:** about **4 of every 5 custodial parents are mothers**; less than half (46%) receive the full child support due to them and nearly a third receive none (Grall 2020).
- **Single parenthood and poverty:** **31% of single mothers** with children under 18 lived in poverty vs. 15% of single fathers and just 5% of married-couple families (2019); women are more likely to be single parents.
- **The second shift** (Hochschild and Machung 1989): after their paid workday, women still do a disproportionate share of unpaid housework and child care. During Covid-19, working mothers cut back on paid work **far more than working fathers** when schools and day cares closed (Landivar et al. 2020).

**Gender → Health**
- **Life expectancy:** women live longer — females born in 2018 are expected to live **81.2 years vs. 76.2 for males** (CDC 2021b). The gap is the **smallest in fifty years**, as women's stress-related behaviors (working outside the home, smoking, drinking) narrowed it (smoking is called "the great equalizer").
- **Men's risk of early death:** young men are especially at risk — men ages 20–24 are **~3× more likely to die from accidents, >4× more likely to commit suicide, and ~7× more likely to be murdered** than women of that age (Heron 2018).
- **Diagnosis differences:** women are **almost twice as likely to suffer depression**; historically women were far more often diagnosed with depression and "hysteria" and treated as a "mentally unfit" category — though women may also be more likely to report symptoms while men feel stigma.
- **Masculinity norms harm men's health:** suppressed emotions, competition, breadwinner pressure — harms men's mental and physical health. *Example:* researchers link it to men being less likely to wear masks during the pandemic.

**Sexuality → Family**
- **Marriage restrictions (historically):** laws ratified only heterosexual marriage, so LGBTQ couples faced legal restrictions against marrying; marriage was legalized nationwide only in **Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)**. Consequences matter because legal marriage conveys benefits domestic partnerships don't — **Social Security, veterans' and disability benefits, inheritance**. After the ruling, a majority of same-sex couples (**61%**, up from 38%) are married, and nearly 10% of all LGBTQ adults are married to a same-sex spouse.
- **Parental/family rejection:** coming out to family can be hard — **parental rejection** is associated with higher depression, attempted suicide, and risk-taking (unsafe sex, illegal drugs) (Ryan et al. 2009); largely because of family rejection, **LGBTQ youth are more than 2× more likely to experience homelessness**; by contrast, supportive families and "families of choice" produce better self-esteem and health (Ryan et al. 2010).

**Sexuality → Health**
- **Suicide and homicide risk:** LGBTQ individuals are more at risk of suicide and homicide. Living in areas with high anti-gay prejudice was associated with a **life-expectancy difference of roughly 12 years** (Hatzenbuehler et al. 2014) — driven by suicide/homicide plus psychosocial stressors linked to cardiovascular risk.
- **Mental health and substance use:** lesbian, gay, and bisexual high school students are more likely to use illicit drugs (heroin, cocaine, prescription opioids) and are at **higher risk of mental illness and suicide** (CDC 2021e).
- **Discrimination in health care and beyond:** even after marriage equality, discrimination in **housing, education, employment, the military, and health care** remains, and 11–28% of LGB workers report losing a promotion — economic strain that compounds health risks.
- **Violence and victimization:** bias-motivated attacks based on sexual orientation are the third-highest hate-crime category; in 2019 there were 1,195 anti–sexual orientation and 198 anti-transgender hate crimes, and LGBTQ people are proportionally the **most likely targets of hate crimes**. Inside the criminal-justice system, **transgender inmates are >10× more likely to be sexually assaulted** than other prisoners and LGB inmates ~3× more likely.
- *Remember:* gender and sexuality are intertwined with race and class (**intersectionality**) — categories work together to shape a person's life chances.

## Social Institutions

### 1. What are social institutions? How do functionalist, conflict, and symbolic interaction theorists explain the role of institutions? Compare and contrast.
- **Social institutions** = systems and structures within society that shape the activities of groups and individuals — "general patterns of social organization or social systems that focus on accomplishing goals related to basic human and/or social needs." The chapter's three are politics, education, and religion.
- **Macro–micro paradox:** institutions function at the macro level to shape our everyday interactions, while at the micro level those same everyday interactions construct the institutions — the chapter's organizing theme.
- **Functionalist explanation:** social institutions provide **critical functions for the needs of society** and help **maintain order and unity** — they serve society as a whole.
- **Conflict theory explanation:** social institutions **represent the interests of those in power** and thus **create and maintain inequalities** in society.
- **Symbolic interactionist explanation:** social institutions are **created through individual participation**; they give meaning to, and are a part of, the everyday experience of members.
- **Applications from the chapter:** the **pluralist model** of political power = a structural-functionalist theory — power is distributed among many groups with equal access, kept in check by laws and courts (Dahl 1961); the **power elite** = a conflict-theory account — a small, insular elite at the top of the economic, political, and military institutions makes the big decisions (Mills 1956/1970); "doing religion" = a symbolic-interactionist account — Orthodox Jewish women construct personal agency within their faith in everyday interaction (Avishai 2008).
- **Compare:** all three perspectives treat institutions as organized patterns of social life that shape individuals — and all accept the micro-macro link that individuals also build institutions through participation (voting, attending school, worship).
- **Contrast — level of analysis:** functionalism and conflict theory work at the macro level, analyzing institutions as structures that do things to society; symbolic interactionism works at the micro level, treating institutions as ongoing accomplishments of participation and meaning-making rather than fixed structures.
- **Contrast — verdict on institutions:** functionalists see institutions meeting society's shared needs and maintaining order and unity (harmony/consensus); conflict theorists see institutions serving the powerful and maintaining inequality (domination); symbolic interactionists focus less on what institutions do to society and more on how members construct and experience them day to day. Each asks a different question: *What functions do institutions perform?* vs. *Whose interests do institutions serve?* vs. *How do people create institutions in everyday life?*

### 2. Compare and contrast power and authority.
- **Power** = the ability to impose one's will on others (lecture notes); Weber defines it as the ability to control the actions of others — to get others to do one's bidding.
- **Authority** = the legitimate (normative) right to wield power — the legitimate, noncoercive exercise of power.
- **Government** = the formal, organized agency that exercises power and control in modern society, especially through the creation and enforcement of laws — it draws on both concepts.
- **Similarities:** both concern getting others to comply — relations of control between people and groups; both are central to politics and government.
- **Difference — basis of compliance:** power works through the ability to impose or control, whether or not those subject to it accept it (it can rest on coercion or force); authority works because it is regarded as **legitimate and right**, making it the *noncoercive* exercise of power — compliance is willing.
- **Difference — legitimacy:** power is about capacity or capability; authority adds the normative dimension — people obey because they believe the wielder has the **right** to command. In short, **authority = power + legitimacy**; one can hold power without authority (rule by force alone).
- **Weber's three types of authority** (covered in the group/organization material — Chapter 5 of the notes — and referenced by the Social Institutions material): 1) **Traditional authority** — based in custom, birthright, or divine right (monarchies, dynasties); 2) **Legal-rational authority** — based in laws, rules, and procedures, not heredity or personality (modern presidencies, parliaments); 3) **Charismatic authority** — based in the perception of remarkable personal qualities in a leader; can be revolutionary. Types can coexist (Barack Obama = legal-rational + charisma).
- **Sociological questions to ask of any government:** who has power and who does not, what kind of power is exerted, and how far does it extend?

### 3. Describe the types of government. Give examples.
- **Government** = the formal, organized agency that exercises power and control in modern society, especially through the creation and enforcement of laws.
- **Authoritarianism** = a system of government by and for a small number of elites that does not include representation of ordinary citizens; citizens have no say in who rules, what laws are made, or how they are enforced. *Example:* Paul Kagame's Rwanda ("elected" with 99% of the vote in 2017 amid irregularities); Bashar al-Assad's Syria (a fourth term in 2021 with a reported 95%, which the U.S. called illegitimate).
- **Dictatorship** = one form of authoritarianism; dictators gain power by election, family succession, or military coup — often combined. Dictatorships can also be party/group-run rather than personal. *Example:* the Taliban.
- **Totalitarianism** = the most extreme and modern version of authoritarianism; the government seeks to control **every aspect — public and private — of citizens' lives**, using surveillance, mass communication, modern weapons, and propaganda (Arendt 1958). *Example:* North Korea under Kim Jong Un — among the world's worst human rights records, with roughly 1,000 people per year risking their lives to defect.
- **Monarchy** = a government ruled by a king or queen, with succession kept within the family; monarchs are not popularly elected, and some claimed to rule by "divine right." Historically: the Tudors, the Ming Dynasty, the Romanovs — most were weakened or overthrown in the revolutions of the 18th–20th centuries (French Revolution 1789; Russian Revolution 1917).
  - **Absolute monarchs** = monarchs with complete authority, resembling dictators. *Example:* Saudi Arabia, Brunei.
  - **Constitutional monarchs** = monarchs whose powers are defined and limited by a political charter or parliament — largely figureheads. *Example:* Japan, Thailand, Great Britain, Sweden.
- **Democracy** = a political system in which all citizens have the right to participate; educated citizens elect officials to represent their interests. It is also a philosophy emphasizing majority rule, basic human rights, civil liberties, freedom, and equality. Originated in ancient Greece.
- **Democracies still exclude:** U.S. women could not vote until 1920; the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) gave Black men the vote "on paper," but poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses blocked African Americans for nearly a century until the 1965 Voting Rights Act; American Indians were excluded from federal elections until 1924; D.C. residents could not vote for president until 1961; and felons remain disenfranchised in some states even after serving their sentences.

### 4. What can affect participation in a political system?
- **Demographic characteristics:** age, race, gender, sexual orientation, religion, geography, class, and education all affect who votes. *Example:* in 2020, turnout was **74% among those over 65 vs. 57% among 18–34-year-olds**; democracy also requires an educated polity, linking education to participation.
- **Felon disenfranchisement:** being stripped of voting rights, temporarily or permanently — all states except Maine and Vermont bar incarcerated felons, 30 states bar parolees, 27 bar probationers, and 11 permanently bar some who have completed their sentences. More than **5 million Americans** — including 1 in 16 African Americans of voting age — are barred (Uggen, Larson, and Shannon 2020).
- **Registration rules:** you cannot vote without registering; only 21 states + D.C. offer same-day registration, and elsewhere registration can close up to 30 days before the election.
- **Election scheduling:** Election Day is a Tuesday — not a weekend or holiday as in other democracies.
- **Voter ID laws:** justified as combating fraud (which is extremely rare; Brennan Center 2021), they disproportionately affect minorities, the elderly, and low-income Americans; critics charge they are intended to suppress turnout among groups that vote Democratic.
- **Gerrymandering:** redrawing state voting-district boundaries to advantage one party, so election outcomes no longer reflect the actual party makeup of residents.
- **Mobilization and organizing:** registration and get-out-the-vote drives can raise participation. *Example:* Stacey Abrams' New Georgia Project and Fair Fight registered and mobilized voters for a decade, helping flip Georgia's Senate seats in 2020.
- **Access rules / convenience of voting:** expanding early and mail voting raises turnout. *Example:* in 2020 states expanded mail voting during Covid-19 (five states — Colorado, Hawaii, Oregon, Utah, Washington — already vote entirely by mail), and turnout surged to **67%**, the highest since 1900.
- **Attitudes and motivation:** people also sit out when they are not interested, dislike the candidates or issues, or feel too busy (see Q5).
- **Suppression as backlash:** claims of fraud can generate laws restricting participation. *Example:* after 2020, Georgia limited ballot drop boxes and even banned handing out food and water to people waiting in line.

### 5. What reasons do people give for not voting in the U.S.? How are these explanations related to larger structural forces?
- **Stated reasons (2020 survey):** "not interested" (18%), "did not like the candidates or campaign issues" (15%), "too busy" (13%) — only 4% cited the pandemic.
- **Individual explanations are shaped by structure:** even the "choice" not to vote tracks social position — in 2020 turnout was 74% among those over 65 vs. 57% among 18–34-year-olds, and age, race, class, education, and geography determine who faces barriers and who feels the system is theirs.
- **"Too busy" reflects structural obstacles:** Election Day is a Tuesday workday rather than a weekend or holiday; registration can close 30 days before an election (only 21 states + D.C. allow same-day registration); voter ID laws disproportionately burden minorities, the elderly, and low-income people — those least able to take time off, wait in line, or produce documents. *Example:* Georgia's post-2020 law banned even giving food and water to people waiting in line.
- **Disenfranchisement removes people from the electorate entirely:** more than 5 million Americans — 1 in 16 African Americans of voting age — are barred from voting (Uggen, Larson, and Shannon 2020), so aggregate "disinterest" statistics undercount structural exclusion.
- **"Not interested" and "didn't like the candidates" reflect concentrated power:** gerrymandering makes outcomes not reflect the district's actual party makeup; money wins elections (incumbency is the biggest advantage — **96% of representatives and 84% of senators won reelection in 2020**); and the power elite view holds that a small, self-perpetuating group of white upper-class men makes the important decisions — the average person will never wield real power regardless of work ethic (Mills 1956/1970; Dye 2002). When elections feel predetermined and unresponsive, staying home is a rational response to the structure.
- **Suppression is itself engineered structure:** voter ID laws are justified as fighting fraud that is vanishingly rare (Brennan Center 2021) but disproportionately hit Democratic-leaning groups; after 2020 the "Big Lie" about a stolen election — contradicted by the Justice Department's finding of no widespread fraud — fueled state-level voter suppression bills. Participation is thus deliberately managed from the top.
- **Takeaway:** the reasons people give (disinterest, busyness, dislike of candidates) are not purely individual — they connect to macro forces: legal barriers, concentrated elite power, and mobilization gaps that fall hardest along lines of age, race, and class.

### 6. How does disenfranchisement, registration policies, and voter identification requirements affect voting? Be specific.
- **Disenfranchisement** = being stripped of voting rights, either temporarily or permanently — *e.g.*, felons in prison, on parole, or (in some states) for life. Even democracies exclude: U.S. women could not vote until **1920**; the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) gave Black men the vote on paper, but **poll taxes, literacy tests, and "grandfather clauses"** blocked African Americans for nearly 100 years until the **1965 Voting Rights Act**; American Indians were excluded from federal elections until **1924**; D.C. residents could not vote for president until **1961**.
- **Felon disenfranchisement is widespread and uneven:** all states except **Maine and Vermont** disenfranchise incarcerated felons; **30 states** disenfranchise parolees; **27** disenfranchise probationers; and **11 states** permanently disenfranchise at least some people who have completed their sentences.
- **Scale of impact:** more than **5 million Americans — including 1 in 16 African Americans of voting age — are barred from voting** (Uggen, Larson, and Shannon 2020), giving felon disenfranchisement a disproportionate racial impact.
- **Registration policies:** you cannot vote without registering, and only **21 states + D.C. offer same-day registration**; elsewhere registration can close **30 days before the election** — a deadline that shuts out late-deciding or less-organized voters. Related structural hurdle: **Election Day is a Tuesday**, not a weekend or holiday as in other democracies.
- *Example of registration's power:* Georgia's Senate wins by **Jon Ossoff and Rev. Raphael Warnock** came after a decade of voter registration and mobilization organizing by **Stacey Abrams** and volunteers with her **New Georgia Project** and **Fair Fight** — flipping a once reliably red state.
- **Voter ID requirements:** justified as combating fraud, but **fraud is extremely rare** (Brennan Center 2021); **minorities, the elderly, and low-income Americans are disproportionately affected**, and critics charge the laws are intended to suppress turnout among groups that vote Democratic.
- **Suppression bills are a live policy force:** the "Big Lie" (the claim that Trump actually won 2020 despite fraud being "vanishingly rare" and the Justice Department finding no evidence of widespread fraud) fueled state-level voter suppression bills — *Example:* Georgia's new law limited ballot drop boxes and even **banned handing out food and water to people waiting in line**.
- **The reverse also holds — expanding access raises turnout:** *Example:* in 2020 states expanded early and mail voting (five states — Colorado, Hawaii, Oregon, Utah, Washington — already vote entirely by mail) and **turnout surged to 67%**, the largest jump between elections in U.S. history and the highest since 1900.
- **Net effect:** disenfranchisement, restrictive registration, and voter ID laws are "obstacles to voting" that lower turnout disproportionately among felons, racial minorities, the elderly, and low-income citizens — the opposite of the mobilization effect seen in 2020 and in Georgia.

### 7. Describe the pluralist model and power elite models of political power. Which theoretical perspective would we associate each model with? Explain.
- **Pluralist model** = a system of political power in which **a wide variety of individuals and groups have equal access to resources and the mechanisms of power**, kept in check by laws, policies, and the courts (**Robert Dahl 1961**).
- **How power works in pluralism:** power is distributed among many organizations — **corporations, parties, professional, ethnic, and religious groups** — each assumed to have equal access, and **checks and balances prevent any one group from dominating**.
- **Power elite model** = (**C. Wright Mills, 1956/1970**) a **relatively small group of people in the top ranks of the economic, political, and military institutions** who make many of the important decisions in American society — an **insular, self-perpetuating** elite, **overwhelmingly white and male**, whose interests **may not coincide with those of the people**.
- **Elite cohesion:** **G. William Domhoff** (1983–2013) showed the elite overlap into a **network of influence** — they attend the same prep schools and Ivy League colleges, marry within the group, share the same neighborhoods, country clubs, churches, and charities, and serve on one another's boards.
- **Elite size:** **Thomas Dye (2002)** counted only **5,778 individuals** holding these elite positions — **less than 0.0026% of the U.S. population**.
- **Comparison — who governs?** Pluralism: power is **dispersed among many competing groups**, each with access, so no single interest can dominate. Power elite: power is **concentrated in a tiny insular elite** drawn from the economy, politics, and the military, and ordinary citizens are shut out no matter how hard they work.
- **Theoretical association — pluralist model ↔ structural functionalism:** functionalism views social institutions as providing critical functions that maintain **order and unity** in society; the pluralist picture of many groups sharing power, restrained by laws, policies, and the courts, fits this orderly, self-balancing view of politics.
- **Theoretical association — power elite ↔ conflict theory:** conflict theory views social institutions as **representing the interests of those in power and thus creating and maintaining inequalities**; the power elite model is exactly that — a small ruling class using the top institutions to serve its own interests at the expense of everyone else.
- **Implications of the power elite view:** it **debunks the rags-to-riches myth** — the average person will never wield real power regardless of work ethic; the U.S. remains controlled by **white upper-class men**; and those with the power to create change **may act only when it serves their own advantage**.

### 8. What are special interest groups? How do they affect elections?
- **Special interest groups (advocacy groups)** = organizations formed expressly to **raise and spend money to influence elected officials and/or public opinion** — including corporate organizations, lobbies, **PACs**, and **527 groups** (which raise unlimited "soft money").
- **PACs (political action committees)** = tax-exempt organizations that **raise money to support the interests of a select group or organization** or to **campaign for or against candidates and legislation**. *Examples from the notes:* BAMPAC (Black America's PAC), AAPAC (Arab American PAC), NOW PAC (feminist), union and trades PACs, even the NFL's Gridiron PAC.
- **Super PACs** = created by **Citizens United v. FEC (2010)**, which called unlimited corporate/union campaign spending a **First Amendment right**; Super PACs can accept **unlimited contributions** (for issue ads) as long as they **don't coordinate with the candidate** — and they are funded primarily by a small group of **ultrawealthy contributors**.
- **Lobbying scale:** special interests pour money directly into lobbying — *Example (2020 top lobbying spenders):* National Association of Realtors **$84.1M**, U.S. Chamber of Commerce **$81.9M**, PhRMA **$25.9M**, Facebook **$19.7M**, Amazon **$18.7M**.
- **Money wins elections:** campaign spending **correlates positively with success**, and **incumbency is the biggest advantage** — in 2020, **96% of incumbent representatives and 84% of incumbent senators won reelection**. Billionaire backing helps — *Example:* Charles Koch and George Soros.
- **Super PAC spending in elections:** in the 2020 cycle Super PACs spent **more than $2 billion**, with conservative groups outspending liberal ones **about 3 to 2**.
- **Net effect on elections:** because money correlates with winning and special interests supply much of it (through lobbies, PACs, 527s, and Super PACs), elections tilt toward the candidates and causes backed by well-funded organizations — and incumbents, already advantaged, are further entrenched by the spending of groups aligned with them.

### 9. What role does the media play in government?
- **Watchdog — the "Fourth Estate":** the media act as **a check on power** — the "fourth branch" of government; the **First Amendment guarantees a free press** because the framers saw a **watchdog press as essential to democracy**.
- **Driving political change:** politics and the media shape each other — media coverage has driven the **civil rights movement, Vietnam, Watergate, 9/11, and Hurricane Katrina**, and social media powered **Black Lives Matter and MeToo** (see Chapter 9).
- **The partisan trust gap:** in 2016 about **three-quarters of both Republicans and Democrats** believed the media served as a watchdog; by 2020 only **73% of Democrats vs. just 10% of Republicans** trusted the media to report the news fairly (Brenan 2020). Trump supporters distrust "mainstream media" in favor of **Breitbart, Newsmax, and OAN** — meaning the watchdog is trusted by only one side.
- **Campaigns and elections — style over substance:** media coverage now emphasizes **human interest, personalities, spectacles, and planned events over policy background** (Kellner 2005; J. Thompson 2012). *Example:* the **1960 Kennedy–Nixon debates**, the first televised — Kennedy prepared for the cameras and looked fresh and energetic while Nixon looked sweaty and tired; **TV audiences thought Kennedy won, radio audiences thought Nixon won** — image has mattered ever since.
- **Personality politics:** **sound bites** condense politics into seconds and **celebrity politicians** (Reagan "the Great Communicator," Schwarzenegger, etc.) know how to work the camera — *Example:* Donald Trump, a reality-TV celebrity whose "outsider/authentic" image was promoted by the media, which treated him as entertainment and gave him **free airtime**.
- **The media's negative potential:** **filter bubbles** (Eli Pariser 2012) — algorithms deliver customized results based on your history and social circles, fragmenting audiences into **echo chambers**; **Russian meddling (2016, 2020)** spread false accounts and inflammatory posts; **fake news** and conspiracy theories (e.g., **QAnon**) spread by shares and retweets; and Trump flipped "fake news" to **delegitimize mainstream outlets** — **undermining the watchdog function the framers intended**.

### 10. What are opinion leaders? How do they affect our political views?
- **Opinion leaders** = **high-profile individuals whose interpretation of events influences the public** (Katz and Lazarsfeld 1955) — "we let them shape what we believe."
- *Example from the notes:* **Dan Rather** is named as a classic opinion leader.
- **Related role — pundits:** pundits (commentary + news — *e.g.*, Maddow, Hannity, Maher, Hayes, Zakaria) similarly **"mediate what we believe"**; media coverage of campaigns increasingly filters politics through such personalities rather than through policy substance (Kellner 2005; J. Thompson 2012).
- **Mechanism of influence:** opinion leaders supply the **interpretation** of events — not just the facts, but what the events *mean* — and audiences adopt those interpretations as their own beliefs, which is why we let them shape what we believe.
- **Impact on political views:** because opinion leaders and pundits are partisan figures, they feed the split seen in the partisan trust gap (by 2020, **73% of Democrats vs. just 10% of Republicans** trusted the media to report fairly) — each side following its own trusted interpreters of events reinforces rather than corrects existing views.
- **Adjacent effect — satirical news:** satirical news shows (*The Daily Show, Last Week Tonight, Full Frontal*) mirror real news formats but aren't held to journalistic standards, and studies show they **reinforce viewers' preexisting liberal or conservative attitudes as much as serious news does** (Knobloch-Westerwick and Lavis 2017) — the same reinforcement dynamic attributed to opinion leaders.

### 11. Where do people get their political information from? How does this vary by age? What impact can this have on political beliefs and political actions/behaviors?
- **Sources of political information covered in the notes:** traditional/mainstream news media (the "Fourth Estate"), **social media** (Facebook, Twitter, YouTube), **satirical news shows**, partisan outlets (**Breitbart, Newsmax, OAN**, "alt-right" and fringe outlets like Infowars), and the **opinion leaders and pundits** who interpret events within those media.
- **Age differences in political information:** the notes document large **age gaps in political participation** — *e.g.*, in 2020 turnout was **74% among those over 65 vs. 57% among 18–34-year-olds**, and in 2008 just **51% of 18–29-year-olds** voted (the Obama year) — and they repeatedly tie **young people to social media** and youth-led political activity: *Examples:* the **March for Our Lives (2018)** — student survivors of Parkland organized on **Facebook and Twitter** and drew 1.2–2 million people across 880+ demonstrations, among the largest youth-led protests since the Vietnam War; **Black Lives Matter** and **MeToo** were powered by social media. The notes' satirical-news data workshop also flags the shows' **influence on young people's politics** — young viewers often treat satire as a news source.
- **Impact on political beliefs — echo chambers:** **filter bubbles** (Eli Pariser 2012) — search/social algorithms deliver **customized results based on your history and social circles** — fragment people into separate intellectual streams and political **echo chambers** where you only encounter agreeable sources, **sowing division and enabling abuse**. Satirical news likewise **reinforces viewers' preexisting liberal or conservative attitudes** (Knobloch-Westerwick and Lavis 2017), so people's information diets tend to harden rather than change their beliefs.
- **Impact on political beliefs — distrust and misinformation:** the partisan trust gap means many (e.g., Trump supporters) reject mainstream outlets in favor of Breitbart/Newsmax/OAN; **fake news** spreads by shares and retweets, and conspiracy theories like **QAnon** flourished on platforms such as 4chan.
- **Impact on political actions/behaviors:** information sources shape real-world behavior in both directions — **mobilizing** (coverage and social media built the civil rights movement, Vietnam and Watergate coverage, Black Lives Matter, MeToo, and March for Our Lives) and **suppressing or radicalizing** (*Examples:* Russian operatives ran targeted ads encouraging Hillary supporters to "text or tweet rather than show up" — a voter-suppression tactic; QAnon conspiracy beliefs were **linked to the January 6, 2021 insurrection**). The media also shape electoral behavior through **style over substance** coverage — *e.g.*, Trump's free-airtime treatment as entertainment — and the age gap in participation (74% vs. 57%) suggests that *where* and *whether* people get political information connects to whether they act on it politically.
