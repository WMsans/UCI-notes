# Chapter 4: Socialization, Interaction, and the Self
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term                                   | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Socialization**                      | The process of **learning and internalizing the values, beliefs, and norms of our social group**, by which we become functioning members of society. A *twofold* process: society teaches individuals to become functioning members **and** individuals learn/internalize the group's values and norms.                                           |
| **Nature vs. nurture debate**          | The ongoing discussion of the respective roles of **genetics (nature) and socialization (nurture)** in determining individual behaviors and traits.                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Sociobiology**                       | A branch of science that uses **biological and evolutionary explanations for social behavior** (E. O. Wilson 1975).                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Feral children**                     | In myths and rare real-world cases, children who have had **little human contact** and may have lived in social isolation from a young age (contrast "man cub" fiction like Mowgli).                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Self**                               | The individual's **conscious, reflexive experience of a personal identity** separate and distinct from others — we can think about ourselves as if we were more than one being and see ourselves from an observer's vantage point.                                                                                                                |
| **Id**                                 | (Freud) The part of the mind composed of **basic inborn drives** that are the source of instinctive psychic energy; selfish and unrealistic, seeks **pleasure and avoids pain**.                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Ego**                                | (Freud) The **realistic aspect of the mind** that balances the forces of the id and the superego; operates on reason and mediates/integrates their demands.                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Superego**                           | (Freud) Represents the **internalized demands of society**; two components — the **conscience** (keeps us from socially undesirable behavior) and the **ego-ideal** (upholds our vision of who we should ideally be); develops through parental rewards/punishments.                                                                              |
| **Psychosexual stages of development** | (Freud) **Four distinct stages** of the development of the self between birth and adulthood; personality quirks result from being **fixated** (stuck) at any stage.                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Looking-glass self**                 | (Cooley) The notion that the self develops through our **perception of others' evaluations and appraisals of us** — "Each to each a looking-glass, / Reflects the other that doth pass."                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Preparatory stage**                  | (Mead) The **first stage** in the development of self wherein children **mimic or imitate others** (under ~age 3) without fully understanding the meaning.                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Play stage**                         | (Mead) The **second stage** wherein children pretend to play the role of the **particular or significant other** (playing "mommy," "firefighter," "doctor").                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Particular or significant other**    | The perspectives and expectations of a **particular role** that a child learns and internalizes.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Game stage**                         | (Mead) The **third stage** wherein children play organized games and take on the perspective of the **generalized other** (early school years).                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Generalized other**                  | The perspectives and expectations of a **network of others (or of society in general)** that children learn and then take into account when shaping their own behavior.                                                                                                                                                                           |
| **Dual nature of the self**            | (Mead) The idea that we experience the self as both subject and object — the **"I"** (spontaneous, active, creative, less socialized) and the **"me"** (norm-abiding, conforming, more socialized, reliant on others).                                                                                                                            |
| **Thomas theorem**                     | "If people **define situations as real, they are real in their consequences**" (Thomas and Thomas 1928) — the way we define each situation becomes its reality.                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Definition of the situation**        | An agreement with others about **"what is going on"** in a given circumstance; this consensus allows us to coordinate our actions with others and realize goals.                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Expressions of behavior**            | Small actions such as an eye roll or head nod that serve as an **interactional tool** to project our definition of the situation to others.                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Expressions given**                  | Expressions that are **intentional and usually verbal**, such as utterances (most of our speech).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Expressions given off**              | Observable expressions that can be **intended or unintended** and are usually **nonverbal** (facial expressions, mannerisms, body language, dress).                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Impression management**              | The effort to **control the impressions we make on others** so that they form a desired view of us and the situation; the use of self-presentation and performance tactics.                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Dramaturgy**                         | (Goffman) An approach in which social life is analyzed in terms of its **similarities to theatrical performance**.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **Front**                              | In the dramaturgical perspective, the **setting or scene of performances** that helps establish the definition of the situation (a classroom for teaching, a bar for drinking).                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Region**                             | The context in which the performance takes place, including **location, scenery, décor, and props**.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Personal front**                     | The performance tactics we use to present ourselves to others, including **appearance, costume, and manner** (plus gender, race, age).                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Backstage / back region**            | The places where we **rehearse and prepare** for our performances.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **Frontstage / front region**          | The places where we **deliver our performances** to an audience of others.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Social construction**                | The process by which a concept or practice is **created and maintained by participants who collectively agree that it exists** (Berger and Luckmann 1966).                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Cooling the mark out**               | (Goffman) Behaviors that help others **save face or avoid embarrassment** — often referred to as civility or tact (borrowed from con games).                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Agents of socialization**            | Social groups, institutions, and individuals — especially **the family, schools, peers, and the media** — that provide structured situations in which socialization takes place.                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Hidden curriculum**                  | Values or behaviors that students learn **indirectly** over the course of their schooling (punctuality, neatness, discipline, hard work, competition, obedience, plus class/race/gender ideologies; Jackson 1968).                                                                                                                                |
| **Resocialization**                    | The process of **replacing previously learned norms and values with new ones** as part of a transition in life.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Total institutions**                 | (Goffman 1961) Institutions in which individuals are **cut off from the rest of society** so that they can be controlled and regulated for the purpose of systematically stripping away previous roles and identities in order to create new ones (prisons, cults, mental hospitals, boarding schools, nursing homes, monasteries, the military). |
| **Status**                             | A **position in a social hierarchy** that carries a particular set of expectations.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Ascribed status**                    | A status that is **inborn**; usually difficult or impossible to change (gender, race).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Embodied status**                    | A status **generated by physical characteristics** (beauty, disability).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Achieved status**                    | A status **earned through individual effort** (occupation, hobby, skill) or imposed by others (criminal identity, mental illness, drug addiction).                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **Master status**                      | A status that **seems to override all others** in our identities; always relevant and affects all other statuses we possess (e.g., assumptions about women, Asians, doctors, alcoholics).                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Stereotyping**                       | Judging others based on **preconceived generalizations about groups or categories** of people.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Role**                               | The **set of behaviors expected of someone** because of their status; provides a "script" (Goffman 1956; Turner 1978).                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Role conflict**                      | Experienced when we occupy **two or more roles with contradictory expectations** (senator vs. mother).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Role strain**                        | Experienced when there are **contradictory expectations within one role** (parent must be present *and* provide financially).                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Role exit**                          | The process of **leaving a role** that we will no longer occupy.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Role-taking emotions**               | Emotions such as **sympathy, embarrassment, or shame** that require that we assume the perspective of another person or group and respond accordingly.                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Feeling rules**                      | (Hochschild 1975) **Socially constructed norms regarding the expression and display of emotions** — expectations about acceptable/desirable feelings in a given situation ("Boys don't cry," "No laughing at funerals").                                                                                                                          |
| **Emotional labor**                    | (Hochschild 1983) The process of **evoking, suppressing, or otherwise managing feelings** to create a publicly observable display of emotion (flight attendants, waitstaff, Uber drivers).                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Copresence**                         | **Face-to-face interaction** or being in the presence of others.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Saturated self**                     | (Gergen 1991) A postmodern idea that the self is now **developed by multiple influences chosen from a wide range of media sources** — leading to a "pastiche personality" that borrows bits and pieces of identity from whatever sources are available.                                                                                           |
| **Agency**                             | The **ability of the individual to act freely and independently**; we are spontaneous, intelligent, creative — not just passive recipients of socialization.                                                                                                                                                                                      |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: Finsta, Rinsta, and the Curated Self

- Instagram has become a primary means of **presenting the self** — an account is a claim about *who you are*; an implicit demand to document and display "all the right moments" (curate, crop, filter, caption, hashtag; post often but not too much).
- **Rinsta ("real Instagram"):** the filtered, public, heavily curated self you want others to see.
- **Finsta ("fake Instagram"):** private, locked secondary accounts with carefully screened, low follower counts (closest friends/family only). A "safe place" to act naturally — unflattering selfies, joke posts, insider references.
- **Paradox:** on finstagram people may be *less fake and more authentic* than on rinsta.
- **The chapter's question:** What is your "true self"? Where does the sense of self come from?
- **Preview:** The self is **largely a social product** — created and modified through social interaction. (Connects to the later "Interacting Online" section and [[Chapter 3]]'s culture-as-lens idea.)

> **Lesson:** Social media makes visible the everyday work of **impression management** (Goffman) — we present different selves in different settings and seek others' approval.

## 1. What Is Human Nature?

- **"That's just human nature"** is used to explain everything from violence and jealousy to love and altruism — but from a sociological perspective, **culture and society are what make us human**. We have to *learn* the meanings we give to food, housing, sex, and everything else — society is the teacher.
- You would be a radically different person born in 14th-century Japan, an Aztec peasant family, or the Norwegian royal court: different language, skills, meanings, and sense of self. (Aztec peasant girls were considered "old maids" at 15 if unmarried; McCaa 1994.)

### The nature vs. nurture debate
- **Nature side** (sociobiologists, some psychologists, natural scientists): behavioral traits explained by **genetics**.
- **Nurture side** (sociologists, social scientists): human behavior is **learned and shaped through social interaction**.
- **Both are right:**
  - Testosterone contributes to "masculine" traits (aggressiveness, competitiveness) — *but* facing a competitive challenge also **raises** testosterone. Which causes which?
  - **Anderson et al. (1999):** patients with damaged prefrontal cortex — if injured as **infants**, they struggled with moral/social reasoning; if injured as **adults**, they coped. → There is a **crucial period in brain development** when the capacity for moral reasoning is acquired. Nature provides a *biological window* through which social and moral development occurs.
- **Takeaway:** heredity gives basic **potential**; our social environment determines whether we realize or fall short of that potential. Social influences begin at birth (or before) and increase over the years — so gradually and unconsciously we don't notice what/how we are learning.

### 📡 In the Future: Genetics and Sociology
- **2003 Human Genome Project** identified all genes constituting human DNA. Geneticists discovered that **the social environment can actually change genes** → interest converged with sociology.
- **Sociobiology** (controversial from 1975): **Edward O. Wilson's** *Sociobiology: The New Synthesis* came down squarely on the **nature** side (genetic determinism) → heavily criticized by sociologists. Wilson's *On Human Nature* (1978): "a substantial fraction of human behavioral variation is based on genetic difference."
- **The Bell Curve** (Herrnstein and Murray 1994) — proposed a *genetic basis for IQ that differed by race* → heated controversy; misleading media coverage; sociobiology fell out of favor.
- **Today: "social genomics" / "sociogenomics"** (Conley and Fletcher 2017; Bliss 2018; Mills and Tropf 2020) — a new generation uniting genetics and sociology: **genes are conditioned by social experience and work *with* social/cultural factors**, not independently.
  - **Molly Martin (2008):** using twins/siblings/half-siblings data, the parent–child weight link is due not only to shared genes **but also shared lifestyle** — social factors (missed meals, TV/video-game time) are just as influential as parental obesity.
  - Educational attainment, risk-taking, alcoholism, obesity = complex interplay of genetics + social factors.
- Conley and Fletcher (2017): "The genetics revolution may be well underway, but the **social genomics revolution is just getting started**."

### Social isolation
- **Christopher Knight — the "North Pond hermit"** (Maine): lived undetected in the woods for 27 years (~mid-1980s to 2013, from age 20 to 47); spoke to only one person; committed 1,000+ burglaries; avoided capture. On isolation: *"With no audience, no one to perform for, I was just there. There was no need to define myself; I became irrelevant... I didn't even have a name. I never felt lonely."* → identity requires an audience; self is constructed through interaction.
- **Feral children** — rare cases that let social scientists study the effects of isolation (Davis 1940, 1947; Newton 2004). Babies have innate capacities but can fully develop only **through contact with others**.
  - **Genie (1970, Arcadia CA):** discovered at 13; locked in a small room from infancy, often tied to a potty chair/crib, deprived of nearly all human interaction and language; lacked speech and physical development; clawed, sniffed, spat. A team of scientists studied/treated her — rapid early progress in sign language and social skills — but the team splintered, funding was withdrawn, she suffered further abuse in foster care, lost much of her speech; case closed 1978. **Ethics of such research still debated.**
  - **Turpin family (2018, California):** 13 children held captive for years, shackled to beds ~20 hours a day, beaten/tortured; physically stunted, lacking cognitive skills and basic knowledge of the outside world.
- **Conclusion:** extreme social isolation is devastating; only through contact with others do we develop the qualities we consider "natural and normal."
- Socialization begins in infancy and is especially productive once a child understands and uses language (Ochs 1986) — but it is **lifelong**: school, work, marriage, parenthood, etc.

## 2. The Process of Socialization

- "Socializing" with friends is only part of what sociologists mean: **socialization is a twofold process** — (1) society/culture/group **teaches** individuals to become functioning members; (2) individuals **learn and internalize** the values and norms of the group.
- Works on both an **individual and a social level**: we learn our society's way of life and make it our own.
- **Two main goals:**
  1. Teaches members the **skills** necessary to satisfy basic human needs and defend against danger → ensures society continues to exist.
  2. Teaches the **norms, values, and beliefs** of the culture and provides ways to ensure members adhere to the shared way of life.
- Connects to [[Chapter 3]]: socialization is how culture (values, norms, language) gets transmitted and internalized.

## 3. Theories of the Self

- Descartes: **"I think, therefore I am"** — we possess consciousness about ourselves; consciousness is at the core of humanness.
- **Self** = our experience of a distinct, real, personal identity separate from all other people. We can be "proud of ourselves," "lose control of ourselves," "change ourselves" — we think about ourselves as if we were more than one being and see ourselves from an observer's vantage point.
- Sociologists believe the self is **created and modified through social interaction** over a lifetime — but there are several theories of *how*.

### A. Psychoanalytic Theory: Sigmund Freud
- Emphasizes **childhood and sexual development** as indelible influences on identity; society is upheld through the transformation of human instincts.
- **The unconscious mind** (*The Interpretation of Dreams*, 1900/1955): conscious awareness is only the *tip of the iceberg*; below the surface lie the subconscious and unconscious. **Unconscious energy is the source of conscious thoughts and behavior** (unconscious urge to slay rivals → conscious decision to outwork a competitive coworker).
- **Three interrelated systems of the mind:**
  - **Id** — biological drives; source of instinctive psychic energy; seeks **pleasure, avoids pain**; selfish and unrealistic ("slack off, have a few drinks").
  - **Ego** — deals with the **real world**; operates on reason; mediates/integrates the id and superego ("if I keep trying, I'm bound to get that promotion").
  - **Superego** — the **internalized demands of society**; two components: **conscience** (prevents socially undesirable behavior) and **ego-ideal** (our vision of who we should ideally be); develops from parental rewards/punishments; inhibits the id and steers the ego toward morally acceptable behavior.
- **Four psychosexual stages** (between infancy and adulthood; Freud 1905): each associated with a different **erogenous zone**; first three occur between ages 1–5 and "set the stage" for adult life (little memory of this period); final stage begins ~age 12, few complete it. Being **"fixated"** at a stage → adult hang-ups: "oral fixation" (smoking, overeating, verbal aggression), "anal retentive" (neatnik, tightwad, control freak).
- ***Civilization and Its Discontents*** (1930/2010): individual psychology helps create **social order** — we must channel subconscious drives (sex, aggression) into socially acceptable outlets: sexual energy → creative pursuits (culture, commerce, science); aggression → competitive sports, politics, video games. Civilization = agreeing to norms/sanctions that infringe on personal freedom but protect the group.
- **Extension — Nancy Chodorow (1978, 1994):** feminist psychoanalytic sociologist — patterns of **gendered parenting** and early childhood development can lead to the **reproduction of traditional gender roles**.

### B. The Looking-Glass Self: Charles Cooley (1909)
- Chicago School; a simple, elegant idea: *"Each to each a looking-glass, / Reflects the other that doth pass."* We all act like **mirrors** to each other.
- **Three steps (Yeung and Martin 2003):**
  1. **We imagine how we look to others** (friendly, funny, hardworking) — especially to **significant others** (parents, bosses, friends, partners).
  2. **We imagine other people's judgment of us** (smart enough? lazy? boring? too tall?).
  3. **We experience feelings about ourselves** based on our *perceived* judgments (pride if competent, shame if inadequate).
- ⚠️ Key point: we respond to the judgments we **believe** others make — not what they actually think; we're not always right, but **perceptions, not reality, determine our feelings about ourselves**.
- **No self without society:** there is no individual self without a corresponding "other" to provide the looking-glass image. We all come to know ourselves through relationships, real or imagined.

### C. Mind, Self, and Society: George Herbert Mead (1934)
- Expanded Cooley; laid the groundwork for **symbolic interactionism** (see [[Chapter 1#Symbolic Interactionism (America's contribution)|Chapter 1]]). Self is created through social interaction; children begin developing a sense of self around the time they learn **language**.
- **Three stages of self-development:**
  1. **Preparatory stage** (under ~3): no fully developed sense of self; difficulty distinguishing self from others; **mimicry/imitation** without understanding meaning (making faces, patty-cake).
  2. **Play stage** (after ~3): **pretending/playing at roles** — "mommy," "firefighter," "princess," "doctor" → **taking the role of the particular/significant other**; internalize that role's expectations; also serves as **anticipatory socialization** for future roles.
  3. **Game stage** (early school years): organized games with rules → must take into account the roles of **all** other players simultaneously → the perspective of the **generalized other**; children see themselves as objects, internalize the group's standards, evaluate their own behavior → beginning of understanding society's attitudes/expectations as a whole.
- **The dual nature of the self:**
  - **"I"** = subject — spontaneous, active, creative, less socialized.
  - **"Me"** = object — norm-abiding, conforming, more socialized, reliant on others.
  - Inseparable; united into a single self.

### D. Dramaturgy: Erving Goffman (1956, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*)
- Micro-level, face-to-face interaction = the building block of every other aspect of society; all meaning and all selves are **constructed through interaction**.
- **W. I. Thomas & the Thomas theorem:** "If people **define situations as real, they are real in their consequences**" (1928). Ambiguous situations → many possible meanings → the definition we act on becomes reality. *Example:* a woman slapping a man could be a fight, a joke, an attempt to revive him, or a film shoot — each definition leads to different consequences (intervene, call police, laugh, ignore, summon paramedics, ask for autograph).
- We define situations **interactionally** — projecting our definition to others: the **eye roll + nod** (expressions of behavior).
- **Expressions given** (verbal, intentional — most speech) vs. **expressions given off** (usually nonverbal, observable, may be intended or unintended — facial expressions, mannerisms, body language, dress). We trust "given off" as more authentic — but they can be **manipulated too**. It's not just *what* you say but *how* you say it.
- **Impression management:** social life as a sort of **con game** — we work at controlling the impressions others have of us, using all communicative resources; we say/do what communicates our self and refrain from what damages it.
- **Dramaturgy** — the theatrical analogy, entirely intended:
  - **Front** = the setting that helps establish meaning (classroom vs. bar). **Region** = location, scenery, décor, props (don't carry a beer into the classroom).
  - **Personal front** = appearance, manner, "costume," plus gender, race, age. *Example:* Dr. Ferris is told she "doesn't look like a professor" — the image of professors as gruff, grizzled, older men makes a younger, friendlier, female professor work harder to convince others. Students encountering professors off-campus ("What are you doing here?") — out of context, old scripts don't work.
  - **Backstage (back regions)** = rehearse/prepare; **Frontstage (front regions)** = perform for an audience. We present different selves in each (professor this morning showering/making breakfast vs. now lecturing).
  - **The self is a social construction** (Berger and Luckmann 1966) — created/invented in interaction; others participate in agreeing to the reality of that self.
- **Supporting others' selves:** "doing professor"/"doing student" — we support the selves people project. **Cooling the mark out** = letting others save face (overlooking the spinach between the professor's teeth, letting a minor mix-up pass) — a tool of civility and tact (borrowed from con games).
- **Contesting selves:** calling out the spinach in a 200-person lecture hall publicly damages the professor's identity as expert/authority → **presentation of self is about power as well as self**.
- **The "honest judge" defense:** we are not all lying — we must all present *some* self in social situations; "even an honest judge must play the role of 'honest judge'" (Meyrowitz 1985).

## 4. Agents of Socialization

- Socializing forces that provide **structured situations in which socialization takes place**. The four most predominant in American society: **the family, schools, peers, and the media** (also religion, political and economic systems).

### The Family — the single most significant agent in all societies
- The **original group** we belong to: early emotional/social bonds, language learning, first internalization of norms/values; most of our **primary socialization** (becoming a mature, responsible member of society) happens here.
- **Gender roles:** we observe what moms/dads, sisters/brothers are expected to do (mow the lawn, fold laundry) and convert these into general rules about gender (Chodorow 1978). **Parents' behavior matters more than what they say they believe** (Halpern and Perry-Jenkins 2016).
- **Race:** many Black parents (especially mothers) practice **racial socialization** (Turner 2020) — teaching children to deal with racism/discrimination.
  - Low-income Black families: appeals to **respectability politics** — urging children to avoid reinforcing negative stereotypes.
  - Middle/upper-class Black families (Dow 2016): strategies to prevent sons being perceived as "thugs" — **"experience management"** (music lessons, baseball leagues) and **"environment management"** (moving to predominantly white neighborhoods).
- Each family has its own values/beliefs; ethnic, class, religious, educational, political backgrounds all matter; families change over time (first-born vs. youngest children raised differently); some socialization is **unintentional** (a father's violent temper or a mother's depression passed down).
- Family is powerful because young children have **limited outside contact** — "the family is our world." But **location matters** (Lareau 2011): neighborhoods/communities shape childhood socialization — affecting educational outcomes, social mobility, even health (Sharkey 2018).

### Schools
- Public elementary/secondary schools established in the U.S. in the **1800s**; advocates believed schooling maintained democracy (even though Black people and women couldn't yet vote). Schools have taken on ever more responsibilities: PE, meals, discipline, child care.
- School = first significant experience away from home; a **bridge to other social groups**; children learn they will be **judged** on behavior and academic performance.
- **Hidden curriculum** (Jackson 1968): behavioral traits learned indirectly — punctuality, neatness, discipline, hard work, competition, obedience, plus ideologies about class, race, gender. *Example:* teachers reinforce **heteronormativity** as early as preschool (Gansen 2017).
- Increasing scrutiny of teachers as role models (moral standing, training, competence; Goldstein 2014); pressure for schools to handle topics formerly taught at home/church — sex, violence, drugs/alcohol, morality, citizenship.

### Peers
- **Peer group** = people about the same age with similar social characteristics (school friends, teammates, camp cabinmates).
- As children age, **peers become more important than parents** (intense/immediate effect), while the family keeps the most long-lasting influence. By adolescence, young people spend more time with peers than anyone else (Larson and Richards 1991). Peer groups provide independence from — or reaction against — adult control; peer subcultures centered on own interests (gaming, disc golf, garage bands).
- **Dark side:** peer groups can be sources of self-doubt, ridicule, rejection. The need to "fit in" can be overwhelming — some betray their own values. **Bradley and Wildman (2002):** peer pressure predicts risky behaviors (dangerous driving, unsafe sex, drug/alcohol use). **Kreager and Haynie (2011):** adolescent drinking is influenced *more powerfully by the friends of a teen's partner* than by the teen's own friends.
- **Prejudice:** adolescence is critical for developing attitudes/political values — peer groups can influence levels of prejudice (Hjerm, Eger, and Danell 2018).
- **Good side:** peer pressure can boost academic engagement/success (McCabe 2016) and positive ethnic identity (Shin, Daly, and Vera 2007).

### The Media
- A **somewhat recent** agent (TV in the 1950s; widespread internet in the last three decades), yet American adults average **>11 hours/day** interacting with media (Nielsen 2018). Some sociologists ask whether media have **usurped some functions of the family**.
- **Fiji study (A. Becker et al. 2002):** TV arrived in 1995 in a culture that "traditionally supported robust appetites and body shapes." In three years, the % of adolescent girls with an **eating disorder jumped from 12 to 29**; self-induced vomiting rose from 0 to 11%; 83% reported TV influenced how they felt about their body shape/weight.
- **13 Reasons Why (Netflix):** graphic depiction of teen suicide raised "contagion" concerns. **Bridge et al. (2019):** suicide rates among ages 10–17 surged in the month after the 2017 premiere (boys' rate up ~30%; 195 excess deaths in nine months). But **Uhls et al. (2021):** Season 3 inspired young viewers to talk with peers/parents about mental health and seek information on bullying/depression → media effects aren't only negative.
- **Takeaway:** we internalize many media-presented values/beliefs/norms — and their influence will only grow in the Information Age. Ask: *Whose messages are we listening to, and what are we being told about ourselves and one another?*

## 5. Adult Socialization

- Adults are **by no means completely socialized** — life continually presents new situations/roles with unfamiliar norms and values → **secondary socialization** (learning over the life course).
- *Examples:* college teaches professional behaviors (responsibility, punctuality), but jobs add unanticipated expectations ("Always be closing!" in a real estate office); marriage, divorce/widowhood (older daters must learn "safe sex," "splitting the check"), raising a family, moving, job loss, retirement — all require modifying attitudes and behaviors.
- **Resocialization** = replacing previously learned norms/values with different ones.
- **Total institutions** (Goffman 1961): prisons, cults, mental hospitals — and sometimes boarding schools, nursing homes, monasteries, the military. Residents are **severed from previous relations with society**; former identities systematically stripped away and reformed. *Example:* military recruits get a uniform and a new name ("cadet"), shaved heads — quickly losing signs of individuality, then building a new identity: **soldier**. Same process whether the end is good monks, punished criminals, or managed mental illness.
- Severe illness and aging also involve intensive resocialization (discarding old behaviors, e.g., not working out every day).

### 💞 In Relationships: Sister Pauline Quinn and Training Dogs in Prison
- **Sister Pauline Quinn** (born Kathy Quinn): a chronic runaway institutionalized as a child, later homeless in LA — her life turned around by **Joni, a German shepherd**; the dog was the beginning of **resocialization** — first true friend, unconditional love restored her self-esteem; institutions had left her "depersonalized," stripped of positive identity.
- **Prison Pet Partnership Program (1981):** with Dr. Leo Bustad (veterinary science, U. of Washington), Quinn proposed women inmates train shelter puppies into service/therapy dogs at the Washington State Correctional Center for Women.
- **Why it works:** inmates get substantial resocialization — new positive identities, social skills that translate outside; dog training (discipline/obedience through repetition and positive reinforcement) is a lesson for the trainers; dogs even sleep with inmates; prisons report improved morale and behavior.
- **Impact:** 50%+ of state prisons now have similar programs; military prisons train service dogs for disabled veterans; a service animal can cost $10,000 to train. Each relationship with a dog transforms the inmate's sense of self.

## 6. Statuses and Roles

- **Status** = a position in a social hierarchy that comes with a set of expectations. Formal ("professor," "president," "parent" — parental obligations written into law) or informal ("class clown," the "conscience" of a friend group). We occupy **many statuses at once**; some change over a lifetime (marital/parental status), others don't (race, ethnicity, gender).
- **Three kinds of statuses:**
  - **Ascribed** — born with, unlikely to change (gender, race).
  - **Embodied** — located in our physical selves (beauty, disability).
  - **Achieved** — earned through effort (occupation, hobby, skill) or otherwise acquired (criminal identity, mental illness, drug addiction).
- **Master status** — a status that overrides all others in our identities; carries expectations that may **blind people to other facets** of our personalities (assumptions about women, Asians, doctors, alcoholics) → **stereotyping**. We all use these expectations every day — stereotyping is problematic but "all but unavoidable."
- **Role** = the set of behaviors expected from a particular status position; provides a "script" (Goffman 1956; Turner 1978). Professors must hold office hours and get permission for leaves; class clowns must turn classroom events into jokes and sacrifice their own success for laughs.

### Multiple roles and role conflict
- We describe ourselves by personality traits ("responsible," "nurturer," "competitive") that often mirror our role expectations. Playing many roles well enhances the sense of self; juggling them isn't always easy.
- **Role conflict** = two or more roles with **contradictory expectations**. *Example:* **Senator Tammy Duckworth** — first U.S. senator to give birth while in office; Senate rules barred infants from the floor during votes, conflicting with her constitutional responsibilities (and her role as mother). Her efforts led to a rare rule change (first since 1977, when the Senate accommodated service dogs).
- **Role strain** = contradictory expectations **within one role**. Duckworth as parent had to be present and involved *and* help support the family financially (requiring her to be away in Washington).
- **Role exit** = leaving a role we no longer occupy.
- **Covid-19 case:** working mothers during lockdown became remote workers *and* homeschool supervisors → role conflict, increased anxiety/stress (Calarco et al. 2020a); **>2 million women** exited the labor force entirely (Edwards 2020).

## 7. Emotions and Personality

- Emotions feel intensely personal — yet they are **not fully determined by society, but they ARE social**: individual responses show **social patterns**.

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Cross-Cultural Responses to Grief
- All cultures mourn, but expression varies wildly:
  - **Maoris (New Zealand):** death is *not final* until all funeral rites are complete — a full **year**; the deceased are spoken to as if alive until mourning ends.
  - **Roma:** intense, public mourning — no washing/shaving/combing, no eating for 3 days, total absorption (sometimes self-harm); the dead are provided clothes, money, useful objects; mourners wear **white** (vs. Western black), funeral decorations **red**.
  - **Ashanti (Ghana):** red clothing, red clay on arms/foreheads, headbands with red peppers; gendered grief — **women must wail, men must fire guns into the air**; gunpowder used = mark of family status.
  - **Irish wakes:** long, heavily attended **parties** celebrating the dead — tears, but also singing, dancing, drinking, laughing.
- **Lesson:** emotions like grief are universal, but *how* we express them depends on society.

### The social construction of emotions
- Interaction shapes emotional response: yelling angrily at a rally with the crowd (realizing later you don't feel that strongly); stifling tears in front of the coach, shedding them after the game.
- **Role-taking emotions** (sympathy, embarrassment, shame) require taking another's point of view — you know a friend feels pain, so you feel sympathy.
- **Feeling rules** (Hochschild 1975): socially constructed norms about appropriate feelings and displays ("Boys don't cry," "No laughing at funerals") — we feel pressure to conform even when unspoken.
- **Emotional labor** (Hochschild 1983): managing/selling feelings as a job requirement. Flight attendants must be cheerful on the job no matter what they feel → their authentic emotions get **displaced**; they become **alienated from their own real feelings** due to structural pressures.

### 💼 On the Job: The Wages of Emotional Labor
- **Hochschild's *The Managed Heart* (1983):** flight attendants (almost entirely female) had to present a cheerful, calm front regardless of treatment; airlines marketed "pretty, perky, perfectly obliging stewardesses," raising gendered/sexualized expectations.
- **Louwanda Evans, *Cabin Pressure* (2011):** adds **race** to emotional labor. Black pilots (<1% of commercial pilots) and Black flight attendants (~14%) must manage their own *and passengers'* emotions around race. Black pilots constantly face assumptions of incompetence ("You are assumed to be inept"); one passenger complained, "That [N-word] better not be flying my plane"; a Black flight attendant was told by a white passenger, "I would prefer you not touch my cup."
- **The rule:** it doesn't matter what emotional laborers want or feel — the job requires smiling, complying, and hiding real feelings.
- The service economy = **"commercialization of feeling"**: retail workers, receptionists, waitstaff, even your Uber driver chasing a five-star rating (Stark 2016). Ask yourself: does your job require emotional labor? Are there gendered/racialized/sexualized aspects? Are you expected to keep your mouth shut when customers do something you don't like?

## 8. Interacting Online

- Classic sociological perspectives assume interaction in **copresence** (face-to-face, physical company) — but technology increasingly enables interaction at a distance: video conferences, online dating, texting, FaceTime/Zoom/Skype, remote robotic surgery.
- **Covid-19 accelerated this** (Nguyen et al. 2020): lockdowns pushed entire sectors remote; kids taught via Zoom; families stayed in touch via video chat apps (Duo, Houseparty).
- **Pioneers of mediated-interaction sociology:** Josh Meyrowitz (1985), Steve Jones (1997), Marc Smith & Peter Kollock (1998), Philip Howard (Jones and Howard 2003), Barry Wellman (2004).
- **Sherry Turkle** (MIT, Initiative on Technology and Self; 1997, 2005): online interaction lets us **contain and reduce risk — risk to self**. We control when/where/how we communicate; we can keep others at arm's length and perform a self that may not correspond to who we "really" are. Result: many connections (friends, fans, followers) but **less depth**; we're less willing to risk intimate bonds. In *Alone Together* (2011) and *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015), she warns that replacing face-to-face talk with tweets/texts/snaps **atrophies meaningful conversation and empathy** — a call to put down devices and talk in real copresence.
- **danah boyd — *It's Complicated* (2014):** less gloomy. Teens seek **private spaces** online (Instagram, TikTok, Twitter) to conduct personal relationships away from adults; they invite only close friends. **Contrast with adults:** grown-ups tweet/post to *expand* circles and broadcast accomplishments; teens do so only for the chosen few.
- **Postmodern view (Holstein and Gubrium 2000; Gottschalk 1993):** technology is a primary feature of postmodern life — new explanations of self-development are needed.
- **Kenneth Gergen (1991) — the saturated self:** postmodern individuals develop a **"pastiche personality"** that "borrows bits and pieces of identity from whatever sources are available" (p. 150) — the self constructed from many influences, real and virtual, unforeseen by the early symbolic interactionists.

> **Question:** Who will we become as we increasingly interact with and through digital technologies?

## 9. Closing Comments — Are We Prisoners of Socialization?

- Socialization can be **homogenizing and conservative** — pushing people toward the lowest common denominator, the mainstream. **But** no two people are ever really alike.
- We are **not passive recipients** — we are active participants with **agency** (spontaneity, intelligence, creativity; free will). Symbolic interactionism: we are always doing the work of interpreting, defining, making sense of, and responding to our social environment.
- The process is **reciprocal and multidirectional** — **you are shaping society as much as it is shaping you.**

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Socialization = twofold:** society *teaches* + we *internalize* — "learning our society's way of life and making it our own."
- **Nature vs. nurture = BOTH** — heredity gives *potential*; social environment decides whether we *realize* it. (Testosterone: causes competition AND is caused by it.)
- **Isolation proves socialization:** Knight ("no one to perform for, I became irrelevant"), Genie, the Turpin children — **no contact, no self.**
- **Freud = the mind's triangle:** **Id** (pleasure), **Ego** (reality mediator), **Superego** (conscience + ego-ideal). Fixation → adult hang-ups.
- **Cooley's 3 steps:** imagine how we look → imagine others' judgment → feel about ourselves based on *perceived* judgment. It's about what we *think* they think!
- **Mead's stages:** **Preparatory** (mimic) → **Play** (particular/significant other) → **Game** (generalized other). "I" = subject/spontaneous; "Me" = object/conforming.
- **Thomas theorem:** "If people define situations as real, they are real in their consequences."
- **Goffman = theater:** front/region/personal front, backstage vs. frontstage, **impression management**, **cooling the mark out** (saving face). "Even an honest judge must play the role of 'honest judge.'"
- **Expressions given = verbal/intended; expressions given off = nonverbal/may be unintended** (but both can be manipulated).
- **Agents of socialization (FSPM):** **F**amily (most significant) → **S**chools (hidden curriculum) → **P**eers (most intense in adolescence) → **M**edia (11+ hrs/day; Fiji study).
- **Resocialization / total institutions:** strip old identity → build new one (military recruits get uniform, new name, shaved head).
- **Statuses:** **Ascribed** (born with) / **Embodied** (physical) / **Achieved** (earned); **master status** overrides all others (→ stereotyping).
- **Role conflict = between roles; role strain = within one role** (Duckworth = both; Covid moms = conflict → 2M role exits).
- **Emotions are social:** grief looks different everywhere (Maori year, Roma white, Ashanti guns, Irish wakes); **feeling rules** govern display; **emotional labor** = selling your feelings at work (flight attendants).
- **Online:** **copresence** vs. mediated interaction; Turkle = depth lost; boyd = teens want private spaces; Gergen = **saturated self / pastiche personality**.
- **Agency:** you're not a prisoner — "you are shaping society as much as it is shaping you."
