# Chapter 6: Deviance
## Key Definitions (know these cold)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Deviance** | A **behavior, trait, or belief that departs from a norm and generates a negative reaction in a particular group** (Goode 1997). Both the norms *and* the group's reaction are necessary — nothing is deviant in itself. |
| **"Nuts and sluts" approach** | (Liazos 1972) The tendency to focus deviance research on **crime, mental illness, and sexual deviance** — the deviance of the poor and powerless — while uncritically accepting the values and norms of the powerful. |
| **Naturalism** | (Matza 1969) The stance that social scientists must **set aside their preconceived notions** to understand deviant phenomena on their own terms — appreciating the diversity and complexity of a particular social world (street gangs, drug addicts, strippers, fight clubs, outlaw bikers, the homeless, the severely disfigured). |
| **Deviant heroes** | (Wolf and Zuckerman 2012) Individuals who **violate norms — and risk the repercussions — out of an intention to create positive social change**. |
| **Social control theory** | (Hirschi 1969) A theory of crime positing that **strong social bonds** — to family and religious, civic, and other groups — **increase conformity and decrease deviance**. |
| **Structural strain theory** | (Merton 1938) Deviance results from **strain between culturally approved goals (e.g., financial success) and the legitimate means available** to achieve them. |
| **Innovators** | Individuals who **accept society's approved goals but not the approved means** to achieve them (drug dealing, embezzlement). |
| **Ritualists** | Individuals who have **given up hope of achieving society's approved goals but still operate according to approved means** — going through the motions. |
| **Retreatists** | Individuals who **renounce society's approved goals and means entirely** and live outside conventional norms (dropouts, hermits). |
| **Rebels** | Individuals who **reject society's approved goals and means** and instead create and work toward their own (sometimes revolutionary) goals using new means. |
| **Differential association theory** | (Sutherland 1939) The hypothesis that **we learn to be deviant through our associations with deviant peers** — "don't hang out with the bad kids." |
| **Labeling theory** | (Becker 1963) The idea that **deviance is a consequence of external judgments, or labels**, that modify the individual's self-concept and change the way others respond to the labeled person. |
| **Primary deviance** | In labeling theory, the **initial act or attitude that causes one to be labeled deviant** (Lemert 1951). |
| **Secondary deviance** | In labeling theory, the **subsequent deviant identity or career that develops as a result of being labeled** (Lemert 1951). |
| **Tertiary deviance** | (Kitsuse 1980) **Redefining the stigma associated with a deviant label as a positive phenomenon** — "Sure, I'm fat, but there shouldn't be anything wrong with that." |
| **Self-fulfilling prophecy** | (Merton, from the Thomas theorem) A prediction that **causes itself to come true merely by being stated**. |
| **Stereotype threat** | (Steele) A self-fulfilling prophecy in which the **fear of performing poorly — and confirming stereotypes about one's social group — causes students to perform poorly**. |
| **Stereotype promise** | (Lee and Zhou 2014) A self-fulfilling prophecy in which **positive stereotypes** (e.g., the "model minority" label applied to Asian Americans) **lead to positive performance outcomes**. |
| **Stigma** | (Goffman 1962) Any **physical or social attribute that devalues a person's or group's identity** and may exclude the devalued from normal social interaction; produces a **"spoiled identity."** Three types: **physical** (impairments), **moral** (flawed character), and **tribal** (membership in a discredited/oppressed group). |
| **Passing** | **Presenting yourself as a member of a different group** than the stigmatized group to which you belong (concealing stigmatizing information). |
| **In-group orientation** | Among stigmatized individuals, the **rejection of prevailing judgments or prejudice and the development of new standards that value their group identity**. |
| **Outsiders** | (Becker 1963) Those **labeled deviant and subsequently segregated from "normal" society**; may still pass among "normals" or have deviance become a master status. |
| **Deviance avowal** | (Turner 1972) The process by which an individual **self-identifies as deviant and initiates their own labeling process** (e.g., AA's first step: "I am an alcoholic"). |
| **Voluntary outsiders** | People who find it **preferable to be a deviant** in spite of mainstream norms — using deviant identity to justify avoiding conventional expectations ("neutralization of commitment"). |
| **Crime** | A **violation of a norm that has been codified into law**, for which you could be arrested and imprisoned. |
| **Criminology** | The **systematic scientific study of crime, criminals, and the criminal justice system**. |
| **Uniform Crime Report (UCR)** | The **FBI's official tabulation of every crime reported by more than 18,000 law enforcement agencies**; tracks the "crime index" (eight offenses). Flawed: participation is voluntary and rarely audited. |
| **Violent crime** | Crime in which violence is the objective or means: **murder, rape, aggravated assault, and robbery**. |
| **Property crime** | Crime that does not involve violence: **burglary, larceny-theft, motor vehicle theft, and arson**. |
| **Cybercrime** | Crime committed via the internet: identity theft, embezzlement, fraud, sexual predation, financial scams. |
| **White collar crime** | (Sutherland) Crime committed by a **person of respectability and high social status in the course of their occupation** (fraud, embezzlement, insider trading); policed and punished less strenuously than street crime. |
| **Cyberbullying** | The use of electronic and social media (web pages, social networking sites, email, Twitter, cell phones) to **tease, harass, threaten, or humiliate someone**. |
| **Hate crime** | A crime in which victims are **deliberately targeted because of demographic characteristics** (race, national origin, religion, gender, sexual orientation, disability status). |
| **Aging out of crime** | The trend whereby crime rates **decline with age** — those 65+ commit fewest crimes (16% of population, 1.6% of arrests). |
| **Deterrence** | An approach to punishment that relies on the **threat of harsh penalties** to discourage people from committing crimes (logic behind California's "three strikes" law). |
| **Retribution** | An approach to punishment emphasizing **retaliation or revenge** — society's right to "get even." |
| **Incapacitation** | An approach to punishment that **protects society by imprisoning or executing criminals**. |
| **Rehabilitation** | An approach to punishment that **attempts to reform criminals** so that once released they don't return to crime. |
| **Criminal justice system** | The collection of social institutions — **legislatures, police, courts, and prisons** — that create and enforce laws; reflects the inequalities of the society in which it operates. |
| **Capital punishment** | The **death penalty**. |
| **Youth control complex** | (Rios) The way a variety of institutions — **law enforcement, the judicial system, and public schools** — work together to **"criminalize, stigmatize, and punish" working-class youth, particularly youth of color**; contributes to the "school-to-prison pipeline." |
| **Prison-industrial complex** | The trend of replacing government-run prisons with **privately run, for-profit penitentiaries** that push for state subsidies and cut costs (unpaid inmate labor, reduced education/health care/food). |
| **Positive deviance** | **Actions considered deviant within a given context but later reinterpreted as appropriate or even heroic** — norms broken in the name of the good. |
| **Foreground of deviance** | (Katz 1988) The **in-the-moment experience of committing a deviant act** — the emotional "seductions" of crime (e.g., shoplifting's "sneaky thrill") that background-focused theories miss. |
| **"Cash register honesty"** | Employees who **draw the line at stealing money but freely "pilfer"** pens, Post-its, personal copies, extra time — yet would be appalled at being called deviant. |

## 📍 Chapter Opener: Marijuana, Hemp, and the Fluidity of Deviance

- **The Founders were cannabis farmers:** George Washington and Thomas Jefferson grew **hemp** for fiber (fabric, rope, paper — Jefferson drafted the Declaration of Independence on hemp paper); no evidence they used it to get high.
- **The criminalization of marijuana (1920s–30s):** as hemp competed with cotton, timber, and chemical industries, **William Randolph Hearst** and others pressured government officials to make its intoxicating by-product illegal. Hearst's media ran a propaganda campaign; the film ***Reefer Madness*** (shown in schools) linked marijuana to insanity, rape, murder, criminals, jazz musicians, and ethnic minorities. **By 1937 every state had outlawed it**, and the prohibitively high Marijuana Tax Act eliminated cannabis farming.
- **Today:** a majority of states have legalized marijuana in some form — driven by environmentalism (tree-free paper), medical use, recreational users, and new profit opportunities.
- **The lesson:** *"nothing is inherently deviant — it is the cultural, historical, and situational context that makes it so."* Definitions of deviance swing back and forth over history.
- **"How to Read This Chapter":** speeding, picking your nose in public, drinking before 21, piercings your grandmother wouldn't approve, taking pens from the office — **"you are deviant."** We are all deviants of one sort or another.

> **Lesson:** Deviance is a social construction, not a property of acts themselves. What was mainstream can become deviant and vice versa — which is why the chapter asks *who defines the rules* and *why*.

## 1. Defining Deviance

- **Definition:** deviance is a **behavior, trait, or belief** that (1) departs from a norm and (2) generates a **negative reaction in a particular group** (Goode 1997). Both elements are necessary — and the norms themselves come from culture (see [[Chapter 3]]).
- **Gradations:** some violations are mildly deviant (chewing with your mouth open); others are taboo almost beyond thought (cannibalism). Deviance must be **serious or unusual enough to spark a negative sanction (punishment)**.
- **Context example — eating with your hands:** using the wrong fork at a fancy restaurant = barely noticed; eating a whole steak dinner with your hands = your friends are disgusted. In India or Ethiopia eating with your hands is normal — but use the **right** hand (the left is bad luck and bad manners).
- **Covid mask example:** who is deviant — the mask wearer or the non–mask wearer — depends on the group's values (family that prioritizes public safety vs. friends who prioritize individual freedom; religious/political affiliation). The same act can flip from conformity to deviance between settings.
- **Five questions sociologists ask about deviance:**
  1. How are norms and rules created, and how do some become especially important?
  2. Who is subject to the rules, and how is rule breaking identified?
  3. What types of sanctions are dispensed to violators?
  4. How do rule breakers see themselves, and how do others see them?
  5. How have sociologists tried to explain rule making, rule breaking, and responses to it?
- **"Nuts and sluts" approach (Liazos 1972):** sociologists historically focused on crime, mental illness, and sexual deviance of the **poor and powerless**, accepting the powerful's norms without question.
- **David Matza's "naturalism" (1969):** study deviance **on its own terms** — appreciate the diversity and complexity of each deviant world rather than imposing moral judgments. *Example:* Leila Rupp and Verta Taylor spent three years with a dozen drag queens for *Drag Queens at the 801 Cabaret* (2003).

### 🌍 Global Perspective: Body Modification
- **Branding:** once an *involuntary mark of shame* (punishment for criminals/enslaved people in ancient Greece), now a *voluntary mark of pride* — African American fraternities brand their Greek letters; Michael Jordan and NFL's Malcolm Jenkins display brands. Now spreading to subcultures alongside tattoos, Mohawks, and piercings (Parker 1998).
- **Cross-cultural contrasts:** Suri of Ethiopia insert progressively larger **lip plates**; Padaung women of Burma stretch their necks with **brass rings**; breast augmentation is common in the U.S., butt augmentation popular in **Brazil**.
- **"Normal" body modification:** corsets with whalebone "stays" (sometimes women even had ribs removed); the hair salon — a perm breaks the disulfide bonds in your hair and reshapes them; even a haircut is body modification (luckily temporary).
- **What's "deviant" is relative:** other cultures may view Americans' obsession with hair removal — shaving, tweezing, waxing — as bizarre.
- **Lesson:** the boundaries between **beauty and deviance are fluid across time and place**.

## 2. Theories of Deviance

The three paradigms from [[Chapter 1]] — **functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism** — plus specialized theories developed for deviance specifically.

### Functionalism (What is deviance *for*?)
**Émile Durkheim** proposed several functions of deviance:
1. **Clarifying moral boundaries** — we're reminded of shared notions of right when wrongs are addressed. *Case:* **Cecil the lion** — a beloved 13-year-old lion with protected status in Zimbabwe's Hwange National Park, killed in 2015 by Minnesota dentist **Walter Palmer** (lured out of the park with an elephant carcass, skinned, decapitated; Palmer paid $50,000+ for the hunt). Palmer argued it was *legal* — but the public deemed it *immoral*.
2. **Promoting social cohesion** — outrage unites a community. The Palmer case went viral worldwide; protests outside his office (he went into hiding); his Facebook and Yelp flooded; condemned by Jane Goodall and PETA.
3. **Promoting social change** — **"deviant heroes"** (Wolf and Zuckerman 2012) violate norms and risk repercussions to create positive change. *Case:* **Colin Kaepernick** knelt during the national anthem (2016) to protest racial injustice and police brutality; lauded by some, called a traitor by others (including then-president Trump); cost him his football career but raised awareness of systemic oppression, inspired nationwide protests, and contributed to police reforms. Now recognized as a civil rights activist.

**Travis Hirschi's social control theory:** the **stronger one's social bonds** (family, religious, civic groups — the group ties from [[Chapter 5]]), the less likely one is to commit crime; bonds increase investment in and commitment to the community's values; both **internal and external forces** regulate behavior and promote conformity.

### Structural strain theory (Merton 1938)
- Bridges functionalism and conflict theory: like Durkheim, deviance is inevitable; like conflict theorists, **one's position in the social structure affects one's experience of deviance**.
- **Strain** = tension between the **goals** society says we should pursue (financial success, the "American Dream" mythology; Messner and Rosenfeld 2012) and the **means** available (not everyone can work hard at a legitimate job).
- **Merton's typology (Figure 6.1)** — combinations of accepting/rejecting goals and means:
  - **Conformist** — accepts goals *and* means.
  - **Innovator** — accepts goals, rejects means (drug dealing, embezzlement).
  - **Ritualist** — rejects goals, keeps going through the motions of means.
  - **Retreatist** — rejects both, lives outside conventional norms (dropouts, hermits).
  - **Rebel** — rejects both and advocates radical alternatives to the existing order.
- ***Black Panther* analysis:** **T'Challa** = conformist (upholds Wakanda's separatist policies through approved means); **M'Baku and the Jabari** = retreatists (mountain tribe rejecting Wakanda's norms); **Nakia** = innovator (internalizes Wakandan values but favors unconventional methods — sharing vibranium tech to uplift Black communities); **Killmonger** = rebel (wants to destroy the world with Wakanda's weapons); **Okoye** = ritualist (loyally protects Wakanda even under Killmonger, until she loses faith in his new goals and leads the overthrow). Note: the **innovator** plays the critical role in changing Wakanda's culture.

### 💞 In Relationships: Cyberbullying, Trolls, and Online Deviance
- **Cyberbullying** = using electronic/social media to tease, threaten, or humiliate. Catapulted into national consciousness after the suicide of 13-year-old **Megan Meier** (October 2006): she was befriended online by "Josh," who turned cruel; Megan hanged herself. "Josh" turned out to be **Lori Drew**, a neighbor mom retaliating for a snub of her daughter.
- **Scale:** **59% of U.S. teens** have been victims of cyberbullying (Pew; Anderson 2018).
- **Why it's worse than offline bullying:** faster, connects more people; hate pages (anonymous accounts) can be set up instantly and comments go viral in hours; it happens *anytime* including in your own home; effects are **longer-lasting** — a rumor online has almost unlimited reach even if later deleted, unlike bathroom graffiti.
- **Trolls:** internet bullies who target strangers; a 32-year-old Seattle programmer created the "Megan Had It Coming" blog after her death; trolls enjoy their abuse, continue under new usernames after being blocked; common tactics include **doxxing** (publishing private, humiliating information — photos, financial data — online; Stein 2016).
- **The legal gap:** slander, harassment, and stalking are crimes "in real life," but identity and jurisdiction questions make it hard to prosecute cyberbullies and trolls. Maybe the only answer is to *troll the trolls*?

### Conflict Theory
- **Rules are applied unequally** and punishments are unequally distributed: those at the top face different rules and sanctions than those at the bottom; **the behaviors of less powerful groups are more likely to be criminalized**.
- **Richard Quinney:** capitalism — the exploitation and oppression of the working class — makes deviant and criminal behavior nearly inescapable for workers; the ruling class makes laws that target the poor; when the poor act out, they become targets of law enforcement while the rich stay free.
- **Social control:** **informal** (customs, norms, expectations) or **formal** (laws, official regulations) — both can be exercised unequally in a hierarchical society.
- **Anti-sodomy laws:** as recently as 2003, more than a dozen states imposed heterosexuality through laws prohibiting sexual acts that didn't lead to procreation; in practice they targeted same-sex partners; **Lawrence v. Texas (2003)** invalidated them all. From a conflict perspective: the heterosexual majority exercising control over same-sex minorities.
- **The gender binary:** official documents (driver's license, passport, social security card) require M or F; only recently have some states added an "X" option, even as the nonbinary population grows; the Biden administration vowed to make it federal policy — putting the power of the state behind social change.

### Symbolic Interactionism
Focuses on how **interpersonal relationships and everyday interactions** shape definitions of deviance.

#### Differential association theory (Sutherland 1939)
- **We learn to be deviant through interactions with others who break the rules** — the theory behind "don't hang out with the bad kids." Example: an athlete on steroids influencing teammates to "dope."
- **Critiques:** not all who associate with deviants become deviant; plenty of deviants never consorted with rule breakers; and it can't explain deviance that isn't a willful act (e.g., mental illness).

#### Labeling theory (Becker 1963)
- **Deviance is not inherent in any act, belief, or condition — it's determined by social context.** A man who kills an intruder attacking his child may be labeled a **hero**; a man who kills a cashier during a robbery, a **villain** — the act (homicide) is the same.
- **Labels vary** by culture, time period, and situation ("teen mom").
- **Rosenhan, "On Being Sane in Insane Places" (1973):** eight researchers faked hearing voices to gain admission to psychiatric hospitals, then acted normally. Staff treated them as mentally ill throughout; they were discharged after **one to seven weeks** — all with schizophrenia "in remission." *"Once labeled schizophrenic, the pseudo-patient was stuck with that label."* The study was recently scrutinized for data inconsistencies (Cahalan et al. 2019), but the core point about the power of labels has been documented repeatedly (Rios 2017).
- **Labels stick via the looking-glass self** (see [[Chapter 4]]): if others react to us as deviant, we tend to internalize the label.
- **Primary → secondary deviance (Lemert 1951):** the initial labeled act leads to a deviant identity or career. *The Biggest Loser*: excess body weight (seen as deviant in U.S. culture) = primary deviance; contestants' recognition that they are "fatties" who need slimming = secondary deviance.
- **Tertiary deviance (Kitsuse 1980):** rejecting the deviance label entirely and redefining the trait as normal — *"Sure, I'm fat, but there shouldn't be anything wrong with that"* — seen among Biggest Loser contestants who regained weight and came to accept their bodies.

**Self-fulfilling prophecies and stereotypes:**
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy (Merton)** derives from the **Thomas theorem** (W. I. Thomas 1928; see [[Chapter 4]]): *"If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences."* A prediction causes itself to come true (1930s bank collapsing on a rumor of insolvency).
- **Elijah Anderson's *Streetwise* (1990):** police and community perceive Black male inner-city teenagers as a criminal element → they're more likely to be arrested and reported, must constantly defend their innocence, and are more likely to be incarcerated — a **negative cycle** that's hard to break.
- **Stereotype threat (Claude Steele 1997, 2010):** priming students to identify a negatively stereotyped group membership before an exam → they perform worse; demonstrated in high-achieving African American students and highly ranked female math students (Spencer, Steele, and Quinn 1999).
- **Stereotype promise (Lee and Zhou 2014):** the flip side — Asian American students benefit from positive stereotypes ("model minority"), getting more AP placements, higher grades, and better treatment from teachers; stereotypes become real as people act on them.

### The Emotional Attraction of Deviance
- **Background-based theories can't explain everything:** why do some people with deviance-prone backgrounds never violate rules, while others with no such background do?
- **Jack Katz, *Seductions of Crime* (1988):** focus on the **"foreground" of deviance** — the deviant's in-the-moment experience. Shoplifters seek the **"sneaky thrill"** of stealing in public while appearing normal (which is why most shoplifters aren't underprivileged — see Lindsay Lohan's attempted necklace theft). Muggers get more satisfaction from the sense of superiority over victims than from the goods, even feeling morally superior. Murder can be a "seductive" response — **"righteous slaughter"** — to overcome humiliation (e.g., killing after adultery rather than ending the relationship).
- **Lesson:** social actors are **active participants creating meaningful experiences**, not mere products of their environment.

### 💼 On the Job: Is "Cash Register Honesty" Good Enough?
- **Employee theft is massive:** ~**5% of annual revenues** lost to employee fraud (median loss $140,000/year per organization; ACFE 2012); up to one-third of U.S. corporate bankruptcies involve employee theft; **75% of employees steal at least once**, half of those repeatedly (U.S. Chamber of Commerce); only 1 in 3 potential employees is completely trustworthy (Cunningham and Jones 2010).
- **Pilfering:** paper clips, Post-its, personal copies on the office copier, personal web use, long lunch breaks — "it happens tens of thousands of times a day."
- **"Cash register honesty":** people draw the line at actual cash but not at odds and ends of less calculable value — and would be **appalled at being called deviant**, since "everyone else seems to take something."
- **Controls:** informal social control and formal surveillance (videotaping, keystroke logging, scanning/searching).

## 3. The Stigma of Deviance

- **History:** ancient Greeks branded criminals and enslaved people with hot irons — the mark was called a *stigma* (Greek for **tattoo**), an outward sign of shame. We still use the word for disgrace or failing.
- **Goffman (1962), *Stigma*:** once labeled deviant, a person acquires a **"spoiled identity."** Three types of stigma:
  1. **Physical** — physical or mental impairments.
  2. **Moral** — signs of a flawed character (alcoholism, jail time, sexual transgressions, mental illness).
  3. **Tribal** — membership in a discredited or oppressed group.
- **Stigma is contextual:** being Black or Jewish is a stigma only in a racist or antisemitic society; in an all-Black community, whites are stigmatized. Goffman noted stigmatized identities aren't necessarily just or deserved — they're specific to a group's norms, time, and place.
- **Consequences:** macro — health insurance may not cover depression treatment; micro — friends don't understand; you may work to hide that you're depressed.

### Managing deviant identities
- **Passing:** concealing stigmatizing information — light-skinned African Americans passing as white for 300+ years (especially before the civil rights movement) to access the privileges of whiteness; today: employees concealing tattoos, people hiding diabetes/depression/hearing impairments.
- **In-group orientation:** rejecting the standards that mark you deviant and proposing new ones — **PFLAG, NAD** (National Association of the Deaf), **NAAFA** (National Association to Advance Fat Acceptance), **NCTE** (National Center for Transgender Equality). Activism can be as simple as being openly, unapologetically "out." (*Shrill:* Aidy Bryant's Annie develops an in-group orientation at the Fat Babe Pool Party.)
- **Deviance avowal (Ralph Turner 1972):** some people **initiate the labeling process themselves** — deviance as a *role* rather than an isolated act. AA's first step is admitting "I am an alcoholic." Avowal enables **"neutralization of commitment"** — using the deviant identity to avoid conventional expectations (a recovering alcoholic refusing a 9-to-5 job or family gatherings because stress/attendance "always made them drink"). Result: **voluntary outsiders**.
- **Outsiders (Becker 1963):** those living outside mainstream society; deviance may become a **master status** (see [[Chapter 4]]) — punk subculture members assumed to be loud troublemakers regardless of personality.

## 4. The Study of Crime

- **Crime** = a particular type of deviance: a **violation of a norm codified into law**, punishable by arrest and imprisonment. State-backed sanctions exert more power than nonlegal norms — but the risk of jail doesn't always deter (speeding, underage drinking, office pens).
- **Criminology** = the study of crime, criminals, and the criminal justice system. Questions: Who makes the laws? Who breaks them? Who benefits from defining/enforcing them? How do people start and stop offending?
- **The Uniform Crime Report (UCR):** the FBI's tabulation of crimes reported by 18,000+ agencies. Tracks the **crime index** — eight offenses: **violent** (murder, rape, aggravated assault, robbery) and **property** (burglary, larceny, motor vehicle theft, arson). Flaws: voluntary participation, rarely audited.
- **UCR findings:**
  - Violent crime declined sharply in the last decade of the 20th century; **1991 had the highest homicide rate in U.S. history** (9.8 per 100,000; 24,700 murders); rates dropped dramatically 1991–2000 and stayed low.
  - Murder peaks in **July–August** and is higher in **southern states** (which also have the lowest median family incomes).
  - Murder is most often committed by a **friend or relative** of the victim, seldom a stranger.
  - Robbery is most frequent in urban areas among youth.
  - **Property crimes outnumber violent crimes**; the most common crime is **larceny**.
  - The Information Age added **cybercrime**.

### Crime and demographics (question the data!)
- **Class:** crime rates look higher in poor urban areas — but police **concentrate efforts there**, so arrests are higher; class may relate more to how citizens are *officially treated* than to who actually commits crime. **Sampson and Wilson (2005):** the same factors that disadvantage an area (lack of jobs, after-school child care, good schools) also encourage crime.
- **White collar crime** (Sutherland): fraud, embezzlement, insider trading — committed by the respectable and high-status; from privileged backgrounds (Shover and Wright 2001); **policed and punished less strenuously than street crime**.
  - ***Case: Elizabeth Holmes/Theranos.*** Holmes founded the blood-testing startup in 2003 at 19 (dropped out of Stanford); the "Edison" machine supposedly ran hundreds of tests on one drop of blood; valued at **$9 billion** by 2015; investors included Murdoch, DeVos, Kissinger, and the Walmart heirs; Walgreens partnership; machines didn't work, deception unraveled; **charged with "massive fraud" in 2018** and forced to step down as CEO.
- **Age:** the younger the population, the more crime. Peak arrest age: **19**; 18–24-year-olds are 9% of the population but 20% of arrests. Separate juvenile laws/courts (bench trials, no juries; some acts like curfew violations are crimes only under 18). Those **65+**: 16% of population, 1.6% of arrests — **"aging out of crime."** Caution: our stereotype of the youthful criminal may make police and public more likely to arrest young people; youth commit more *visible* crimes, while older people's crimes (embezzlement, fraud) are harder to detect.
- **Gender:** males = **88% of murder arrests, 73% of all arrests** (2019). Early "biology" explanations (women too weak/passive/unintelligent) replaced by social-structural ones. **Messerschmidt (1993):** as women gain power in the labor market (education, income), their crime rates should rise toward men's — largely supported: 2010–2019 male arrests fell 23%, female arrests only 15%.
- **Race:** African Americans are 13% of the population but **27% of arrests and 33% of prisoners**. Sociologists caution against linking race and criminality, highlighting **institutional racism** (disproportionate targeting; Kohler-Hausmann 2018) and **class variables** (Black Americans more likely to live in lower-income, heavily policed communities with fewer opportunities).
  - **Michelle Alexander, *The New Jim Crow* (2011):** contact with the criminal justice system permanently stigmatizes African American men — legally denied employment, housing, and public benefits, "much as African Americans were once forced into a segregated, second-class citizenship in the Jim Crow era."
  - **Devah Pager (2003):** the stigma of a criminal record is more damaging for Black men than white men. In matched-pair job applications, **a white man with a criminal record got more callbacks than a Black man with no record**.
- **Hate crimes:** deliberate targeting based on race, national origin, religion, gender, sexual orientation, or disability; usually added to other charges when bias is suspected; FBI counted **7,000+ in 2019 (58% racially motivated)** — and official statistics underreport (not all jurisdictions participate).
  - **Covid spike:** hate crimes overall declined 6% in 2020, but **anti-Asian attacks rose ~150%** (Center for the Study of Hate and Extremism 2021). The **Atlanta spa shootings** (Robert Aaron Long, 8 killed, 6 Asian women) were not prosecuted as a hate crime, angering Asian American leaders; no widely accepted symbol of anti-Asian bias makes proving racist motive harder, and language barriers/fear of retaliation deter reporting (Hong and Bromwich 2021).
- **Intersectionality:** class, age, gender, and race don't act in isolation. **Nikki Jones (2012)** studied inner-city Black girls in Philadelphia caught in a **respectability vs. practicality bind**: to be "respectable" they must be "good girls" and avoid violence, while meeting feminine and race-based appearance norms — but risky neighborhoods require them to look and act tough and be ready to fight. Their race, class, gender, and age force them to balance both demands.

## 5. The Criminal Justice System

- **Four philosophies of punishment:**
  1. **Deterrence** — harsh penalties discourage crime (the logic of California's **"three strikes" law**: automatic life sentence for a third felony). But sociology rarely finds such direct causal links.
  2. **Retribution** — society's right to "get even."
  3. **Incapacitation** — confine or execute criminals to protect society.
  4. **Rehabilitation** — reform criminals so they don't return to crime.
- **The system itself:** legislatures + police + courts + prisons = the criminal justice system; like any institution it **replicates the inequalities of power** in society.
- **Victor Rios** (gang member turned UC Santa Barbara sociology professor, thanks to a teacher who connected him to a mentoring program): the **"youth control complex"** — law enforcement, the judicial system, and public schools work together to "criminalize, stigmatize, and punish" working-class youth, especially youth of color. The educational system adopted law-enforcement attitudes; indiscriminate policing **creates the crime it's designed to eliminate**, feeding a **"school-to-prison pipeline"** (Rios 2009).
- **The death penalty:** **Beckett and Evans** found Black defendants **>4× more likely** to be sentenced to death than other defendants (Washington State capital cases). In 2018 the Washington State Supreme Court ruled the death penalty unconstitutional, citing the research ("imposed in an arbitrary and racially biased manner"); Illinois and California placed moratoriums or moved to abolish it. When inequities and errors exist, we must question the meaning of "justice."
- **The prison-industrial complex:** the shift from government-run to **private, for-profit prisons** that push for state subsidies and cut costs (unpaid inmate labor; less education, health care, food). Critics question this trend, especially given the prison population growth that coincided with privatization.

### 📡 In the Future: American vs. Scandinavian Prisons
- **U.S. prisons:** imposing, windowless, walled, overcrowded, violent; prisoners get little medical/rehabilitative/mental-health care; located in rural or impoverished urban areas to stay invisible; we react with fear and revulsion.
- **Scandinavian "open" prisons** (Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Finland, Iceland): organized like boarding schools, focused on **rehabilitation and reentry**, not retribution and incapacitation.
  - **Suomenlinna Island prison (Helsinki):** not walled off from the tourist town; prisoners live in dormitories, hold town jobs (maintenance, tourism) at **$6–$10/hour**, wear their own clothes, cook their own meals, have TVs/stereos, visit families, and have supportive (not adversarial) guards.
  - **Halden Prison (Norway):** education, job training, therapy; the Norwegian Correctional Service makes every inmate a **"reintegration guarantee"** — helping them find homes and jobs after release.
- **Outcomes:** U.S. incarceration rates are **10×** Scandinavian rates, and Scandinavian **recidivism is less than half** the U.S. rate.
- **Why it works:** Nordic criminal justice policy is **governed by research rather than politics** — social scientists, not legislators, decide how to house and treat prisoners. Contrast with U.S. "tough on crime" politics, fearmongering media, and private corporate interests that created an overcrowded, violent, expensive, ineffective system.

## 6. Reconsidering Deviance?

- Definitions of deviance are historically, culturally, and situationally specific — most arguments, legislative battles, and social movements are really fights over **what counts as deviant** (see the marijuana case).
- Even in Durkheim's hypothetical **"society of saints"** (1895), deviance is unavoidable.
- **Positive deviance:** norms broken **in the name of the good** — rule violators initially seen as criminals, later honored as heroes.
  - **Rosa Parks (December 1, 1955, Montgomery, AL):** refused to give up her bus seat to a white man, defying a segregation ordinance; her arrest galvanized the Black community, triggered the bus boycott and protests that ended Southern segregation. She was not an accidental symbol — an experienced activist. Awarded the **Presidential Medal of Freedom (1996)**; her 2005 funeral drew presidents, members of Congress, and luminaries of all races.
  - **My Lai (March 16, 1968):** Lt. William Calley's Charlie Company stormed the South Vietnamese village on a "search and destroy" mission and massacred hundreds of unarmed civilians (women, children, elderly) in direct violation of military law. Calley was court-martialed; his men, "following orders," were not held responsible. **Hugh Thompson, Lawrence Colburn, and Glenn Andreotta** flew their helicopter into the carnage *against orders*, airlifted dozens of survivors to safety, and threatened to shoot their fellow Americans if they harmed more villagers. The army covered up their heroism for years; they were recognized with medals in **1998** — for "the moral conviction necessary to defy authority."

## 7. Closing Comments

- Studying deviance raises **complicated issues of morality and ethics** — sensitive topics (rape, alcoholism) and vulnerable populations (undocumented immigrants, the mentally ill) demand responsibility.
- **David Matza:** eschew moral judgments in our work, however difficult.
- **Ethics:** protect the people we study from negative outcomes; the deviance label can **disempower** groups, and policy made from social science research may further injure already marginalized groups.
- **But:** the sociological perspective also creates the possibility that previously labeled and marginalized groups may **someday receive assistance and legitimacy** from the larger society. *"The sociological perspective is a powerful tool."*

## Quick Memory Hooks
- **Deviance = norm departure + negative reaction** — nothing is inherently deviant; context (culture, history, situation) makes it so. Marijuana: hemp to scourge to medicine.
- **You are deviant** — speeding, nose-picking, pens from the office: deviance is everywhere.
- **Don't study just "nuts and sluts"** (poor people's crime/mental illness/sex) — study deviance on its own terms (Matza's naturalism).
- **Durkheim's three functions:** clarify moral boundaries (Cecil the lion), promote cohesion (global outrage), promote social change (deviant heroes like Kaepernick).
- **Hirschi's social control:** strong bonds (family, church, civic groups) → conformity → less crime.
- **Merton's strain typology:** goals vs. means — **conformist** (both) / **innovator** (goals only) / **ritualist** (means only) / **retreatist** (neither) / **rebel** (both + alternatives). *Black Panther:* T'Challa = conformist, Nakia = innovator, Okoye = ritualist, M'Baku = retreatist, Killmonger = rebel.
- **Conflict theory:** rules are made by and for the powerful; the poor get criminalized (Quinney); anti-sodomy laws and the M/F gender binary as majority control.
- **Sutherland:** deviance is learned from deviant peers (differential association).
- **Becker's labeling:** labels create deviance; Rosenhan's pseudo-patients stayed "schizophrenic"; **primary → secondary deviance** (label → identity/career); **tertiary deviance** = reclaiming the label as positive ("fat acceptance").
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy:** Thomas theorem → predictions come true (bank runs, *Streetwise*); **stereotype threat** (worse performance) vs. **stereotype promise** (better performance).
- **Katz:** the *foreground* of deviance — the "sneaky thrill," the seduction of crime.
- **Pilfering = "cash register honesty":** 75% of employees steal once; theft is a cost of doing business.
- **Stigma (Goffman):** physical / moral / tribal → "spoiled identity"; manage it by **passing** (conceal), **in-group orientation** (reject the standard), or **deviance avowal** (claim it — AA style).
- **Crime = codified deviance;** measured by the **UCR** (8 offenses, flawed but useful); murder peaked in 1991, most homicides by friends/relatives, larceny is #1.
- **Demographics (be skeptical of the data):** class (policing concentration), age (peak 19; aging out), gender (88% of murders male; structure not biology), race (institutional racism; *The New Jim Crow*; Pager's audit), hate crimes (underreported; anti-Asian spike in Covid).
- **Punishment logics:** deterrence (three strikes), retribution (get even), incapacitation (lock up), rehabilitation (reform).
- **Rios:** youth control complex → school-to-prison pipeline; **Beckett & Evans:** death penalty is racially biased.
- **Scandinavian open prisons:** research-driven policy, reintegration guarantee, <½ U.S. recidivism at 1/10 the incarceration rate.
- **Positive deviance:** Rosa Parks (1955) and the My Lai trio (Thompson, Colburn, Andreotta) — criminals in their day, heroes in hindsight.
- **Bottom line:** deviance is in the eye of the norm-setter. Who defines the rules, and who gets punished — that's the sociological question. And the perspective can both disempower the labeled *and* eventually legitimize them.
