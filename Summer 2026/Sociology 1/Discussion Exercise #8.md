# The Economy as a Social Institution

The economy is the social institution that organizes how a society produces, distributes, and consumes the goods and services it needs to survive. 

Functionalists see the economy as an essential part of the social system, since every society must solve the material problem of survival. The concept of the division of labor is central to this view. As societies grow larger and more complex, work becomes increasingly specialized, and people become dependent on one another. For instance, a farmer relies on truck drivers and mechanics, just as those workers rely on the farmer. This is organic solidarity, which functionalists claims what binds modern society together. Thus, in this perspective, the economy produces social cohesion by matching people to the occupational roles that society needs and rewarding the talents required to fill them.

On the other hand, conflict theorists treat the economy as a central battleground between the owner of the means of production and the people who own nothing. The people in the modern economic system owns nothing, so they sell their labor to the owner of means of production to survive. Laborers don't have machines, lands, intelligence assets or any means of production, so they have to work for the owner. However, the wages (set by owners) are usually a lot lower than the value (or profit) of the product the workers produces. This is the process of exploitation. As a result, work under capitalism also produces alienation: workers become estranged from the products they make, from the repetitive work process itself, and from one another. In modern form, this perspective points to corporations whose size gives them power over wages, prices, and even government policy, and to an economy in which wealth concentrates at the top while the share of income going to ordinary workers stagnates. The rules of the economy are set by the powerful, and the class position a person is born into largely determines their economic life chances.

Symbolic interactionists focus on what work and money mean to the people engaged in them. Occupation is among the first things that we use to label and identify others and ourselves, for example when someone asks us "what do you do?". Job titles, work uniforms and office spaces serve as symbols that signify competence and authority. With Goffman, the workplace may be conceived of as a theatre: interviews and meetings take the form of performances wherein the participants manage the impressions that they give by means of CVs, clothing and language. The same applies to consumption. The money in and of itself has no value; it gains its value from collective belief that pieces of paper and numbers on computer screens have some value. Brands are selected because they are part of identity. This is why two similar goods are sometimes sold at vastly different prices.

The most useful thing I learned this quarter is the sociological imagination. Mills defined it as the ability to see the connection between biography and history between our personal circumstances and the larger social forces surrounding us. For instance, losing a job feels like a personal failure until you notice that millions of people are being laid off at the same time. I am glad I learned this because it is the lens through which I now read my own life. Selecting a major, having fears about the return on investment from paying for college, and being concerned about whether my first generation status will limit me are all examples of personal choices and concerns, but sociological imagination helps us to see that these are influenced by the social structure of the economy, including the types of occupations available, their salaries, and the qualifications needed to enter them.
