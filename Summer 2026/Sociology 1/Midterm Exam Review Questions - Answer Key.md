# Midterm Exam Review Questions — Answer Key

## The Sociological Imagination

### 1. What is the main concern of sociology?
- **Sociology** = the systematic/scientific study of **human society and social behavior** — from large-scale institutions and mass culture down to small groups and individual interactions (Latin *socius* + Greek *logos* = study of society).
- It studies **how humans affect society AND how society affects humans** (both directions).
- Becker: sociology is the study of people **"doing things together"** — neither society nor the individual exists in isolation; humans are essentially social beings.
- It is a **science** of **society**: it uses the scientific method (objectively verifiable sense experience) to examine the social world.

### 2. What is the purpose of sociological theories?
- **Theories** = abstract propositions / general laws of social action that **explain the social world and make predictions about the future** (in sociology, the "laws" are called theories).
- We use theory to **predict behaviors**.
- A claim only counts as scientific theory if it is **testable against empirical data** — this separates science from opinion/belief/common sense.
- Theories are constantly tested and **renewed** against new data (science = the process of renewing theories); when data no longer fits, the theory is revised or replaced.

### 3. What are the main theoretical perspectives in sociology?
- **Macro perspectives** (large-scale structures):
  - **Structural Functionalism** (Durkheim)
  - **Conflict Theory** (Marx)
  - **Weberian Theory** (rationalization/bureaucracy)
- **Micro perspective** (face-to-face interaction):
  - **Symbolic Interactionism** (Mead, Blumer, Goffman, Garfinkel)
- **Newer approaches**: Postmodernism, Midrange Theory (Merton).
- The "big three" taught as the core perspectives: **functionalism, conflict, symbolic interactionism**.

### 4. Describe the sociological imagination
- **C. Wright Mills (1959):** a **quality of the mind** that allows us to understand the relationship between our **individual circumstances and larger social forces** — the **"intersection between biography and history."**
- "The sociological imagination enables us to **grasp history and biography and the relations between the two within society**. That is its task and its promise."
- We wrongly see our problems as private matters of character/chance; they may be caused by a specific cultural-historical context.
  - *Example:* can't find a job → feels personal, but may result from outsourcing, downsizing, technology changes, migration. Credit-card debt: psychological view = lack of self-control; sociological view = cultural norms promoting lifestyles beyond means + wages not keeping up with inflation.
- **Makes the familiar strange:** see behavior as part of a **pattern** in a larger society — it's the **culture and history** landed on that person that caused the behavior.
- Three consequences: (1) makes our lives "ordinary" (you are not alone); (2) exposes **false consciousness** (seeing the "veneer" of social life); (3) understanding your own fate requires locating yourself in your time (history + biography).

### 5. Be able to list the early theorists in sociology and briefly describe their work
- **Auguste Comte (1798–1857)** — coined "sociology"; invented **"social physics"/positivism** (sense perceptions are the only valid source of knowledge); wanted the scientific study of society to guide social progress and order; 3 stages of human thinking: theological → metaphysical → positive/scientific.
- **Harriet Martineau (1802–1876)** — English journalist/political economist; traveled to the U.S. and judged democracy on its own standards (*Society in America*, 1837); **translated Comte into English (1853)**, making his ideas accessible; early feminist sociologist.
- **Herbert Spencer (1820–1903)** — established sociology in Britain/America; societies evolve like biological organisms; coined **"survival of the fittest"** → **Social Darwinism**.
- **Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)** — founder of **functionalism**; demonstrated the scientific method can study social reality; *Division of Labor in Society* (mechanical vs. organic solidarity); *Suicide* (1897) — suicide relates to **social factors** (religion, marital status, employment) and **anomie** ("normlessness," disconnection); *Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (sacred vs. profane).
- **Karl Marx (1818–1883)** — **conflict theory**; capitalism creates two classes: **bourgeoisie** (owners of the means of production) and **proletariat** (workers); alienation, ideology, false consciousness vs. class consciousness, **praxis** (practical action to change the world).
- **Max Weber (1864–1920)** — modern society characterized by efficient, goal-oriented, rule-governed **bureaucracies**; **rationalization**; the **iron cage** (rigid bureaucratic structures control our lives); **disenchantment**; **verstehen** (empathic understanding of actors' meanings).
- Micro-theorists (Chicago School): **George Herbert Mead** (mind, self, society as social processes; language crucial), **Herbert Blumer** (named symbolic interactionism; 3 tenets), **Erving Goffman** (dramaturgy — the self is created in interaction and changes by context), **Harold Garfinkel** (ethnomethodology).
- Also: **W.E.B. Du Bois** (pioneer of race studies; NAACP co-founder), **Jane Addams** (Hull House 1889; applied sociology; founder of social work).

### 6. Be able to distinguish between micro and macro sociology
- **Microsociology** — studies **face-to-face and small-group interactions** to understand how they affect larger patterns/structures of society (e.g., DeFrancisco's "Sounds of Silence" — gender/power seen in everyday marital talk).
- **Macrosociology** — studies **large-scale social structures** to determine how they affect the lives of groups and individuals (e.g., Williams' "glass ceiling"/"glass escalator").
- Photographer analogy (Newman): zoom lens = micro, wide-angle lens = macro; both photograph the same subject.
- The difference is the **scale of patterns**.
- Different assumptions: micro assumes larger structures are *shaped by* individual interactions; macro assumes structures *shape* those interactions. They form a **continuum** — most sociologists sit somewhere in between.

### 7. Discuss the similarities and differences between functionalism, conflict, and symbolic interaction theories
**Similarities:**
- All are **theories/paradigms**: sets of assumptions that form a way of understanding social reality; all explain social order, change, or interaction.
- All can be applied to the same issue and each gives a partial lens (e.g., college admissions: Table 1.1).
- Functionalism and conflict are both **macro**; symbolic interactionism is **micro**.

**Differences:**

|                         | Functionalism                                                    | Conflict                                                     | Symbolic Interactionism                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| View of society         | **Stable, orderly system** of interrelated parts; like a machine | Characterized by **social inequality, conflict, and change** | Created and maintained **through interaction and meaning** |
| Key units               | Structures/institutions (family, education, religion…)           | Classes/inequality (class, ethnicity, gender…)               | Symbols, meanings, gestures, language                      |
| Function of each units  | Generates **stability of the whole**                             | Reproduces the **interests of the powerful**                 | Meaning is negotiated and can change                       |
| Change                  | Dysfunction → change → new equilibrium                           | Driven by **class struggles/revolutions**                    | Through redefinition of meanings in interaction            |
| Key figures             | Durkheim, Parsons, Merton                                        | Marx, Frankfurt School, feminist/CRT/queer theorists         | Mead, Blumer, Goffman, Garfinkel                           |
| Assumption about actors | Parts serve the whole                                            | Interests conflict                                           | **Human agency**; actors are active/expressive             |

### 8. Discuss the strengths and weaknesses of each of the three theoretical perspectives
**Functionalism**
- ✅ **Strengths:** an attempt at a **universal social theory**; comprehensive model; provides a **sense of stability and order** (brings order to a disorderly world).
- ❌ **Weaknesses:** suggests dysfunction produces social change but **doesn't explain what or why dysfunction occurs**; provides **little insight into social processes**; conservative bias (only dysfunction creates change — can't easily explain civil rights/women's movements); static rather than dynamic; circular reasoning (an institution's persistence shouldn't count as explanation); inequality problem (if poverty persists, it "must serve a function" — Gans).

**Conflict Theory**
- ✅ **Strengths:** favors **practical action (praxis)**; Marxist ideas have contributed to **social movements** throughout history; addresses **both macro and micro issues**; does **not assume an institution's existence means it is beneficial**.
- ❌ **Weaknesses:** ignores parts of society that are stable and enduring, like **shared values** and **common beliefs**.

**Symbolic Interactionism**
- ✅ **Strengths:** allows for **human agency** (active, expressive model of the human actor); treats the individual and the social at the same level of analysis; strong research-method legacy (ethnography, conversation analysis).
- ❌ **Weaknesses:** sometimes accused of being **apolitical** (supporting the status quo); sometimes accused of being **unscientific**; very micro-focused — often limited to **social psychology** ("astructural").

### 9. Be able to apply each perspective to an example
**Example: college admissions** (Table 1.1):
- **Functionalism:** Society is a unified whole; admitted students are worthy/well-qualified, rejected students "not deserving" — there are other places in society for them. Structures (education) function together to sort people into roles.
- **Conflict:** Decisions are made on criteria other than grades/scores — donor parents, athletic/musical talent; some are denied for race, gender, sexuality. Stratification is reproduced; the system serves the powerful.
- **Symbolic Interactionism:** It's all about **self-presentation and meaning-making** — how applicants present themselves, how the committee constructs the "ideal applicant," how applicants interpret acceptances/rejections.

**Example: religion** (Table 3.1):
- **Functionalism:** Religion = a social institution that promotes **social order** by providing the morals/ethics followers embrace.
- **Conflict:** Religion **controls the masses** by creating rules; sanctions may be unequally/unfairly applied; **culture wars** reflect tension over which values dominate.
- **Symbolic Interactionism:** Religion = beliefs and rituals that are part of **interaction** among followers (reciting the Lord's Prayer, bowing toward Mecca, keeping kosher = meaningful displays).

## Methods

### 1. What are the objectives and characteristics of scientific research?
- **Science = the organization of objectively verifiable sense experience.** Objectives: describe and explain the social world with verifiable evidence, and build/test **theory** that explains and predicts.
- **Characteristics:**
  1. **Data is empirical and objective** — gathered through specific methods (systematic observation, measurement, experiments); findings must be **verifiable by others**, not one person's opinion.
  2. **Data is organized into general laws/theory** — raw observations alone aren't science.
  3. **Theory must fit the empirical facts.**
  4. **Must be testable/falsifiable.**
  5. **Can be disproven but never proven** (science is the process of renewing theories).
- Stages: (1) Question (comes with a theory) → (2) Hypothesis derived from the theory (derive variables from concepts; IV, DV, control variables) → (3) Construct a research design → (4) Analyze the data (correlations; significance ≠ importance — significance means the association is greater than might occur at random) → (5) Draw conclusions.
- Related ideals: **objectivity** (let facts speak for themselves), **replicability** (research can be repeated and verified by others).

### 2. Discuss how each of the research methods covered in class might be used to gather information/data about a social issue
- **Experimentation** (focus: cause/effect) — manipulate the independent variable, measure the dependent variable in a controlled setting, with random assignment of subjects to experimental/control groups. *Example:* Thorne's pink/blue baby experiment (gender socialization begins in infancy); Correll's "motherhood penalty" experiments (working mothers paid less/fewer promotions even with identical qualifications). Variations: classical, quasi-experimental, field experiments.
- **Field studies / observational research** (focus: realism/depth) — systematic observations of behavior as it occurs naturally; informant interviewing, **participant observation**, **unobtrusive measures**. *Example:* Edin & Kefalas' ethnography of poor single mothers in East Camden (*Promises I Can Keep*).
- **Survey research** (focus: realism/breadth) — ask a set of questions to members of a population and record responses; closed/open-ended questions, Likert scales; sampling (convenience vs. random — simple, stratified, clustered). *Example:* Guttmacher Institute survey on why American women delayed childbearing during Covid.
- **Archival analysis** (focus: availability) — analyze information **already collected by others** (surveys, diaries, media, government records); **content analysis** = interpreting and coding documents. *Example:* Stearns' *Anxious Parents* (child-rearing manuals → changing meaning of childhood); Sabrina Strings' content analysis of 18th-century media (thinness ideals rooted in anti-Blackness).
- **Meta-analysis** — statistically **combine quantitative results** from all previous studies on a question; useful for making sense of large numbers of studies on the same phenomena with divergent findings.
- **Social network analysis (SNA)** — measure/visualize the **structure of social relationships** (who people look to for information, advice, support); used for adolescent risk behaviors, information diffusion, epidemics (Covid spread; six degrees of separation — Milgram).

### 3. Be able to explain which methods provide a better fit to which kinds of issues. Contrast internal and external validity.
- **Experiments** = best for **causality** (internal validity): you can manipulate and control the environment, randomize, replicate. *Fit:* cause-effect questions (does X cause Y?). Weakness: artificial lab settings → lower external validity.
- **Surveys** = best for **large populations / breadth**: quick, economical, reliable, generalizable with probability sampling. *Fit:* describing patterns/attitudes in a big population. Weakness: lacks depth; weak on causal claims and self-report validity.
- **Ethnography/observation** = best for **depth/meaning**: rich detail, studies groups whose stories go untold, challenges taken-for-granted notions. *Fit:* "how do people experience/make sense of…?" Weakness: small samples → representativeness problem; hard to replicate.
- **Archival/existing sources** = best for **past or unobtainable data** (historical periods, data researchers could never collect). *Fit:* historical change, trends over time. Weakness: data wasn't designed for your questions.
- **Field experiments** = balance: high external validity with moderate internal validity (real-world setting + some control).
- **Internal validity** = free of contamination by extraneous influence — how well the study's causal conclusion holds *within* the study.
- **External validity** = the extent to which causal relationships in a research setting/population can be **generalized outside the study** to the real world.

### 4. Describe the two forms of bias that can intrude into social psychological studies *
- **1) Investigator/researcher (experimenter) bias** — the researcher's own expectations, values, or characteristics influence the results: choice of what to study, sample, question wording, analysis, write-up (bias can infiltrate *every* stage). Example: Mark Regnerus's New Family Structures Survey (2012), criticized for conservative/religious funding and flawed design. (Relates to Weber's "value-free" sociology — separating facts from personal values.)
- **2) Participant/subject bias** — participants react to the process of being studied and may give the researcher what they think is wanted: **demand characteristics** (guessing the hypothesis and behaving accordingly), social desirability, evaluation apprehension, dishonesty in self-reports. Classic example: **reactivity / the Hawthorne effect** — productivity rose with *every* change because subjects knew they were being studied.

### 5. How can harm to participants of social psychological studies be reduced or prevented?
- **Physical harm** — uncommon in sociology research; avoid it.
- **Psychological harm** — manipulations may include false negative feedback or very personal questions; reduce long-term harm by providing a **thorough debrief immediately following participation** (esp. after deception: explain the experiment and work through the impact of deception).
- **Breach of confidentiality** — particularly important in surveys and observational research; precautions vary by methodology; subjects are entitled to "rights of biographical anonymity," especially vulnerable populations (children, undocumented immigrants, psychiatric patients).
- **Informed consent** — respondents freely participate and understand the nature of the research: explanation of purpose/procedures, acknowledgment of foreseeable risks, description of benefits, list of available medical/psychological resources, offer to answer questions, disclosure of the **right to terminate participation at any time**.
- **Institutional safeguards** — the **Common Rule (45 CFR 46)** and university **IRBs** (institutional review boards) review research proposals to protect human subjects; can require changes or stop projects.

### 6. Discuss the ethical issues in social psychological research.
- **Informed consent** — participants must freely participate and understand the research (especially since interviews/experiments are often recorded or deceptive).
- **Protecting subjects from harm** — physical, psychological (false feedback, personal questions), and reputational harm; debriefing after participation.
- **Deception** — participants are often unaware of the project or its goals (Milgram's obedience studies, Stanford Prison Experiment) → requires strict ethical guidelines, post-participation debriefing, and justification.
- **Confidentiality / privacy** — biggest harm = breaching confidentiality; biographical anonymity; pseudonyms (Middletown revealed as Muncie, Indiana; Carolyn Ellis's village betrayal; Alice Goffman's *On the Run*).
- **Codes and laws** — ASA Code of Ethics (avoid bias, follow professional standards, protect respondents); the **Nuremberg Code** (post-WWII: informed, noncoerced consent; no unnecessary suffering) born from Nazi experiments; the **Tuskegee Syphilis Study** (1932–1972, Black men left untreated) as a U.S. atrocity; the **Common Rule** and **IRBs**.
- **Institutional review** — IRBs review proposals; controversy: boards are often all medical/physical scientists and may not understand social research.
- **Value-free ideal vs. bias** — Weber's "value-free" sociology; bias can enter every stage; market research rarely meets scientific standards.

## Culture

### 1. What is culture?
- **Culture** = the **system of symbols** people in a society use to create, store, and organize their affairs; the **entire way of life of a group of people** (material + nonmaterial elements).
- Acts as a **lens** through which one views the world; **passed from one generation to the next**; **shared, learned, not innate** — the "human equivalent of instinct in animals."
- Culture is **internalized** — ingrained in our thinking and acting; guides how we make sense of the world.
- Components: beliefs, values, norms, language, patterns of action, physical objects.

### 2. What are the components of culture?
- **Symbols** — objects or actions that by social convention stand for something else (arbitrary but consensual); includes **signs** (traffic signals, logos) and **gestures** (body language).
- **Language** — made up of **significant (shared) symbols**; a cultural universal; the basis of nonmaterial culture; **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (language structures thought).
- **Beliefs** — ideas people share about **true or false**.
- **Values** — ideas people share about **good and bad**.
- **Norms** — standards/rules and expectations people share: **folkways** (loosely enforced), **mores** (great moral significance), **taboos** (unthinkable — incest, cannibalism), **laws** (formally codified); **sanctions** enforce norms (positive rewards, negative punishments); **social control** = formal/informal mechanisms eliciting conformity.
- **Roles & statuses** — role = set of normative requirements for a category of people; social status = recognized position in a system of positions; master status = permeates all other statuses (age, gender, ethnicity, SES).
- **Social groups** — primary (small, intimate — family) vs. secondary (larger, impersonal — job).
- **Social institutions** — general patterns of social organization around basic human needs (family, education, religion, economics, polity/government, health/medicine, mass media).
- **Material culture** — physical objects to which we give social meaning; **nonmaterial culture** — ideas/ways of thinking and behaving.

### 3. How is society internalized?
- **INTERNALIZATION** = the process by which we **incorporate within our personality/self** the norms that are prevalent within our society.
- **CONSCIENCE** = the internal operation of ethical/moral principles that control the actions, thoughts, and/or feelings of an individual.
- Through **socialization** — the interactive processes through which individuals learn ways of behaving, thinking, and feeling essential for effective participation — culture (values, norms, language) gets transmitted and taken in as our own.
- We learn culture so slowly and incrementally that we're unaware of the process; it becomes "second nature" and guides decisions.

### 4. Distinguish between dominant culture, subcultures, and countercultures. Be able to provide examples of each.
- **Dominant culture** = the culture of the group whose values, norms, traditions, and perspectives are **imposed on society as a whole** — the culture of the group **in power** (dominant is *not* the same as majority). Related: **hegemony** (Gramsci) — cultural aspects of social control; the dominant group's ideas are accepted by all. *Example:* mainstream commercial radio/streaming playlists set by record-company business interests; mainstream U.S. consumer culture.
- **Subculture** = a subgroup whose perspectives and lifestyles **differ** from the dominant culture but exist **harmoniously within** the larger mainstream. *Examples:* Korean Americans, senior citizens, snowboarders, White Sox fans, firefighters, Trekkers, the indie-game community (shares gaming norms but values artistry over profit).
- **Counterculture** = a subgroup whose beliefs, norms, and values are **incompatible with / in direct opposition to** mainstream culture. *Examples:* 1960s hippies, antiwar protesters, feminists, hackers/hacktivists (Anonymous), far-right militia/"sovereign citizens," modern-day polygamists.
- Related: **culture wars** = clashes *within* mainstream society over which values/norms should win (bathroom bills, abortion, LGBTQ rights).

### 5. What is cultural variability? How do cultures change?
- **Cultural variability** = cultural differences resulting from **social change**: alterations occur over time; stable and enduring patterns make up society; includes the **generation gap**.
- Cultures usually change **slowly and incrementally**, but change can be rapid (1960s social movements, Digital Revolution).
- **Technological change** — most changes in material culture are technological; new technology (computer microchip → internet → smartphones) provides the structure through which culture is disseminated.
- **Cultural diffusion** — change when groups share material and nonmaterial culture.
- **Cultural imperialism** — imposition of one culture's beliefs/practices on another via media and consumer products.
- **Cultural leveling / isomorphism** — once-distinct cultures become increasingly similar.
- Multiculturalism, subcultures, countercultures, and culture wars all contribute to growth and change; change is usually viewed as "progress."

### 6. What is cultural diffusion? Describe isomorphism and cultural imperialism.
- **Cultural diffusion** = the dissemination of material and nonmaterial culture (tools/technology, beliefs/behavior) **from one group to another**; exposure to another culture → aspects get appropriated. *Example:* McDonald's exports the whole fast-food culture, not just hamburgers — Japan's fast-food consumption doubled in the 1980s, obesity/heart disease rose, leading to the 2008 "Metabo Law."
- **Isomorphism** (≈ **cultural leveling**) = when distinct cultures become **increasingly similar**; Walmarts drive mom-and-pop stores off Main Streets; cultures converge (blending can create hybrids — but also a blander culture of sameness). *Example:* McDonaldization — the same standardized menus and fast-service norms everywhere.
- **Cultural imperialism** = the **imposition of one culture's beliefs and practices on another culture through media and consumer products rather than military force** (Schiller 1995): film, TV, music, soft drinks, running shoes instead of guns. Western media companies command enough economic resources to dominate wherever their products go; national responses (censorship, promoting domestic production) are hard to sustain.

## Socialization and the Self

### 1. What is socialization?
- **Socialization** = the **interactive processes with others in society** through which individuals **learn the ways of behaving, thinking, and feeling** that are essential for **effective participation within society**, and that lead to the **acquisition of the Self**.
- A **twofold process**: (1) society/culture/group **teaches** individuals to become functioning members; (2) individuals **learn and internalize** the group's values and norms.
- Two main goals: teach members the **skills** to satisfy basic human needs and defend against danger; and teach the **norms, values, and beliefs** of the culture so members adhere to the shared way of life.
- Underlies the **nature vs. nurture** debate: do genetics (nature) or socialization (nurture) influence behaviors and traits? Sociology: both — heredity gives potential; the social environment determines whether we realize it (e.g., prefrontal-cortex study: a "crucial period" for moral reasoning).

### 2. How does socialization make us like all other members of society, but distinctive in other ways?
- **Like others:** socialization is **homogenizing and conservative** — it pushes people toward the mainstream/common denominator; we internalize shared norms, values, language, and roles, so we resemble other members of our society (e.g., we all learn the same language and basic norms).
- **Distinctive:** **no two people are ever really alike** — each person has a **unique combination of biopsychosocial characteristics** and a specific **location in the social structure**; unique family backgrounds, experiences, interpretations, and interactions produce a distinctive self.
- We are **not passive recipients**: we have **agency** (spontaneity, intelligence, creativity, free will) — we interpret, define, and make sense of our social environment, and the process is **reciprocal/multidirectional**: "you are shaping society as much as it is shaping you."

### 3. How did Freud view personality and the self? (pleasure principle/reality principle, three personality processes)
- Freud emphasized the **conflict between the individual and society** through the **internalization of cultural ideas** that direct human action.
- **Pleasure principle vs. reality principle:** the id operates on the pleasure principle (immediate gratification, avoid pain); the ego operates on the reality principle (reason, dealing with the real world).
- **Three personality processes:**
  1. **Id** (本我) = the **biological demands of the organism**; inborn drives, source of instinctive psychic energy; selfish and unrealistic; seeks pleasure and avoids pain.
  2. **Ego** (自我) = **learned behaviors** which seek to satisfy the demands of the id; the realistic aspect that **mediates between the id and the superego** (the "self" regulating between the id's impulsive demands and the superego's idealized rules).
  3. **Superego** (超我) = the **demands of society** — beliefs, values, norms, conscience; two components: **conscience** (prevents socially undesirable behavior) and **ego-ideal** (vision of who we should ideally be); develops through parental rewards/punishments.
- Related: four **psychosexual stages**; being "fixated" at a stage → adult hang-ups; *Civilization and Its Discontents* — we channel subconscious drives (sex, aggression) into socially acceptable outlets (creative work, sports).

### 4. Describe Cooley's looking glass self
- The self develops through our **perception of others' evaluations and appraisals of us** — "Each to each a looking-glass, / Reflects the other that doth pass."
- **Three steps:**
  1. **Imagination of our appearance to others** (how we look to them — friendly, funny, hardworking), especially significant others.
  2. **Imagination of their judgment of that appearance** (smart enough? lazy? boring?).
  3. **Self-feeling of pride or mortification** based on that *imagined* judgment.
- Key point: we respond to the judgments we **believe** others make — not what they actually think; **perceptions, not reality, determine our feelings about ourselves**. (Self-esteem = our guess of others' thoughts about us — and we usually underestimate ourselves because we think negatively.)
- **No self without society** — if we do not interact with others, we do not have a self. *Example:* we wave back because we guess the other thinks the wave means "bye."

### 5. List and briefly describe Mead's three stages in the development of the self.
1. **Preliminary (Preparatory) Stage** — **presymbolic communication**; babies don't speak, they make *noises* that we assign meanings to; we get overexcited when babies make noise, so babies repeat them; understanding meaning through **reactions**; reinforcing **symbols** through **repetition**; mimicry/imitation without full understanding.
2. **Play Stage** — **symbolic communication**; **taking the role of the other one at a time** (playing catch — switching between catcher and thrower; playing "mommy," "doctor" with dolls); taking the role of the **particular/significant other**; eventually they **think from others' perspective** without acting it out — the beginning of the self; they learn relationships differ, and develop understanding of social norms/consequences and **abstract thinking**.
3. **Game Stage** — **taking the role of several others simultaneously** (organized games with rules); requires **abstract thinking** about rules/norms; viewing others' comments toward the generalized "me" as the **Self**; viewing the internal conversation between "I" and "me" as **Thought/Mind**; the perspective of the **generalized other** (society's attitudes/expectations as a whole).
- Dual nature of the self: **"I"** = subject (spontaneous, active, creative, less socialized); **"me"** = object (norm-abiding, conforming, more socialized, reliant on others).

### 6. What is the definition of the situation?
- **Thomas theorem:** "If people **define situations as real, they are real in their consequences**" (Thomas & Thomas 1928) — the way we define a situation becomes its reality.
- **Definition of the situation** = an agreement with others about **"what is going on"** in a given circumstance; this consensus lets us **coordinate our actions** with others and realize goals.
- For a situation to be successful, people must share a common view: agree on a **frame** (the type of social occasion), agree on **situated identities** (the identities they grant one another — different situations require different identities), and agree on the roles.
- We project our definition through **expressions given** (intentional, usually verbal) and **expressions given off** (observable, usually nonverbal, intended or not — facial expressions, dress).

### 7. What is the difference between front and back regions? Provide examples.
- **Front Region (frontstage)** = settings **accessible to outsiders** where one acts **in line with normative expectations** — delivering a performance to an audience.
- **Back Region (backstage)** = settings **inaccessible to outsiders** where one **violates front-region performances** — where we rehearse, prepare, and relax.
- *Examples:* a professor lecturing in the classroom (front) vs. relaxing at home before class (back); waitstaff serving customers politely (front) vs. joking about customers in the kitchen (back); a student presenting in class (front) vs. complaining about the class with friends in the dorm (back).

### 8. What are the three types of impression management? Which would you use in different situations?
1. **Authentic self-presentation** — creating an image **consistent with our self view** (being genuine; presenting who we really are).
2. **Ideal self-presentation** — our **most appropriate public image of our ideal self** (presenting the best/most fitting version of ourselves for the occasion).
3. **Tactical self-presentation** — a public image that is **positive/favorable regardless of whether it reflects our actual self** (strategic, goal-directed).
- *Which to use:* **authentic** with close friends/family and intimate settings where we can be ourselves; **ideal** in formal or role-defined settings (job interview, first date, professional events — presenting the appropriate "best self"); **tactical** when the goal is to gain favor, advantage, or approval regardless of authenticity (impressing a boss, negotiating, winning over strangers).

### 9. What is the norm of reciprocity in self-disclosure? *
- **Self-disclosure** = revealing personal information about ourselves to another person.
- **Norm of reciprocity:** when someone discloses something personal, we feel expected/obligated to **reciprocate with disclosures of similar intimacy** — disclosure calls forth disclosure.
- This builds **mutual trust and balanced exchange** in relationships; failing to reciprocate signals distance/defensiveness, while over-disclosing without the other reciprocating violates the norm and feels inappropriate.
- Intimacy develops through gradual, reciprocal self-revelation. *(Standard social-psych concept — verify with slides.)*

### 10. Describe tactical self-presentation
- **Tactical self-presentation** = presenting a **public image that is positive/favorable regardless of whether it reflects our actual self** (the slide: "a public image that is positive/favorable regardless of whether it [matches reality]").
- It is **strategic and calculated** — we consciously shape the impression we make to achieve a goal (being liked, hired, respected, getting what we want), even when that image isn't genuine.
- Contrast: **authentic self-presentation** (consistent with our self view) and **ideal self-presentation** (the most appropriate public image of our ideal self).
- Tactics include **ingratiation** (getting others to like us), self-promotion, and other impression-management moves.

### 11. What is the tactic of ingratiation? *
- **Ingratiation** = a self-presentation/impression-management tactic aimed at **getting others to like us** — flattery, doing favors, agreeing with others' opinions, showing interest, praising them.
- Used strategically to gain **favor, approval, or compliance** from others — especially those with more power or whose good opinion matters to us.
- It's a form of tactical self-presentation: the favorable image may not reflect how we really feel, just what serves our goal. *(Standard concept — verify with slides.)*

### 12. How do aligning actions help us to save face? *
- **Aligning actions** (Stokes & Hewitt) = verbal actions/utterances that **align our conduct with cultural expectations** when our behavior might be seen as problematic or off-script.
- They work by **explaining or reframing** what we did so it seems consistent with norms, protecting our identity/face and helping others keep the interaction running smoothly.
- Types: **accounts** — excuses and justifications ("I was late because traffic was terrible"); **disclaimers** — statements before an act that ward off negative interpretations ("I'm not prejudiced, but…"); apologies.
- By aligning our behavior with shared expectations, we **restore the definition of the situation**, avoid embarrassment, and allow both ourselves and others to **save face**. *(Verify terminology with slides.)*

### 13. What is a spoiled identity?
- A **spoiled identity** occurs when impression management **fails** — the identity a person presents is **challenged, discredited, or no longer accepted**.
- Related failure modes (from lecture):
  - **Embarrassment** — public identity is challenged and/or discredited.
  - **Spoiled identities / cooling-out** — persuading a person whose performance is unsuitable to **accept an alternative (lesser) identity**.
  - **Identity degradation** — the person **cannot perform in their original role** (often due to social ritual); more severe than losing face.
- In Goffman's terms, a spoiled identity is a self that has been "discredited" in interaction — the person is pressured to accept a reduced/alternative identity.

### 14. What is stigma? What are the reactions to stigma? *
- **Stigma** (Goffman) = an **attribute that deeply discredits an individual**, reducing them from a whole person to a tainted/discounted one — a **spoiled identity** based on a socially discrediting characteristic (physical deformities/impairments, character flaws, or membership in a discredited group).
- **Reactions of others:** avoidance, rejection, discrimination, negative stereotyping, reduced opportunities and interaction; **"courtesy stigma"** — stigma by association for those connected to the stigmatized person.
- **Reactions of the stigmatized person:**
  - **Passing** — concealing the stigma when it's not visible.
  - **Covering** — minimizing its prominence/visibility.
  - **Withdrawal/isolation** — avoiding situations where the stigma might be exposed.
  - **Self-derogation** — internalizing negative appraisals, low self-esteem.
  - **In-group affiliation/activism** — seeking out others who share the stigma; reframing it as a positive identity ("badge of honor") and challenging the stigma collectively.
  *(Standard Goffman framework — verify with slides.)*

### 15. List and describe the four agents of childhood socialization
1. **Family** — the **single most significant agent**; the original group we belong to; early emotional/social bonds, language learning, first internalization of norms/values; most of **primary socialization** happens here. Effects: parenting styles (authoritative benefits children; authoritarian → poorer adjustment), maternal employment/childcare (high-quality center care → better cognitive skills), divorce (lower academic success, self-esteem, long-term health).
2. **Peers** — for older children; peer group = relative **equals**; choosing who to interact with contributes to a sense of social competence; peers are **voluntary, not ascribed**; peers become more important than parents in adolescence.
3. **School** — **designed for socialization**; public evaluation of performance; hidden curriculum (punctuality, neatness, discipline, hard work, competition, obedience); a bridge to other social groups; teaches that children will be judged on behavior and academics.
4. **Mass media** — news, TV, films, internet, magazines; high levels of exposure (>11 hrs/day); teaches **schemas and scripts for various relationships**; can shape body image and behavior (Fiji TV study: eating disorders rose from 12% to 29% in 3 years).

### 16. What are some of the techniques used to socialize children in schools?
- **Public evaluation of performance** — children learn they will be judged on behavior and academic performance.
- **Hidden curriculum** (Jackson 1968) — behavioral traits learned indirectly: punctuality, neatness, discipline, hard work, competition, obedience, plus ideologies about class, race, gender (e.g., teachers reinforce heteronormativity from preschool).
- Rules, routines, and discipline; rewards and sanctions (grades, praise, punishment).
- Teachers as **role models** (moral standing, training, competence).
- School is the first significant experience away from home — a **bridge to other social groups** and to society's norms.

### 17. How do the agents of socialization reinforce traditional gender roles?
- **Family:** children observe what moms/dads, sisters/brothers are expected to do (mow the lawn vs. fold laundry) and convert these into general rules about gender (Chodorow 1978); **parents' behavior matters more than what they say they believe** (Halpern & Perry-Jenkins 2016).
- **School:** the hidden curriculum and teachers reinforce gender expectations — heteronormativity is reinforced as early as preschool (Gansen 2017).
- **Peers:** peer groups are composed of relative equals but enforce gender norms through acceptance/rejection; gendered friendship patterns and activities.
- **Mass media:** TV/internet portray gendered characters, roles, and images; media teaches schemas and scripts for various relationships; body-image effects are gendered (Fiji study; gendered advertising).

### 18. What are some of the similarities and differences between childhood and adulthood socialization?
**Similarities:**
- Both are processes of **learning norms, values, and roles through interaction** with others.
- Both involve **internalization** and modification of the self.
- Socialization is **lifelong** — adults are by no means completely socialized.

**Differences:**
- **Childhood = primary socialization**: creates the self (创造自我); occurs mainly in the family; language acquisition; the most fundamental and long-lasting influence.
- **Adulthood = secondary socialization**: learning specific **roles** (学习角色) — work, marriage, parenthood, retirement.
- **Adult/developmental socialization** = **normative role transitions** (做该做的事): starting school, employment, marriage, childbirth, retirement — each requires modifying attitudes and behaviors.
- **Anticipatory socialization** — learning in advance the knowledge/skills/values of a future role (提前开始学习).
- **Reverse socialization** — the person expected to be socialized becomes the one socializing others (本该被教育的人变成了教育别人的人).
- **Resocialization** — replacing previously learned norms/values with new ones (去社会化 + 重塑新我): total institutions (army, prison, monasteries, addiction clinics) strip away the old identity (uniform, new name, shaved head) and build a new one.

### 19. Define and be able to provide examples for anticipatory socialization and reverse socialization.
- **Anticipatory socialization** = **learning in advance** the knowledge, skills, and values of a role we will occupy in the future.
  - *Examples:* Mead's play stage (children playing "mommy"/"doctor"); college students learning professional behaviors before entering careers; medical students rehearsing clinical roles; a couple preparing for parenthood; practicing for a job interview.
- **Reverse socialization** = when the person **expected to be socialized becomes the one who socializes others**.
  - *Examples:* children teaching their parents how to use smartphones/social media; immigrant children teaching parents the new country's language and customs; teens introducing parents to new music and slang.

### 20. What is role strain? How do people resolve it?
- **Role strain** = **contradictory expectations within one role** (e.g., a parent must be present and involved *and* provide financially; Duckworth as a parent had to be with her child *and* be in Washington).
- (Contrast: **role conflict** = contradictory expectations *between two or more roles* — e.g., senator vs. mother; Covid lockdown mothers as remote workers *and* homeschool supervisors → over 2 million women exited the labor force.)
- **How people resolve it:**
  - **Role exit** — leaving a role they no longer occupy.
  - **Role-making** — shaping the role to fit the self, rather than just role-taking (we "make" roles as we perform them).
  - **Prioritizing/compartmentalizing** expectations; negotiating with others about expectations; seeking institutional changes (Senator Duckworth pushed through a rare Senate rule change to allow babies on the floor); delegating or reducing responsibilities; resocialization into new roles.
