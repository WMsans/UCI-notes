# Answer

Civ VI's procedural rhetoric makes a claim about historical development that development occurs through territory, and that development takes place through the appropriation and utilization of land. In his definition of procedural rhetoric, Bogost states that, "procedural rhetoric refers to the art of persuasion through rule-based representations and interactions rather than the spoken word, writing, images, or moving pictures" (2007, p. ix; cf. Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al., 2024, p. 161). In Civ VI, persuasion lies in the yield values: since research and culture depend on city numbers and populations, Settler production is instantly converted to research, which results in the fact that at the beginning of the game, founding a city usually brings more gains in the tech points than constructing a Campus in an already founded city. My own game experience reflected this ideology. My opening strategy can be summarized as remaining on good terms with civilizations that are far away, while taking over a close-by valuable resource when appropriate. I do this because as the previous mentioned mechanics, taking land is much more valuable, and also much easier since the civilizations in early game doesn't have very strong weapons. When the game progresses and all civilizations had their Giant Death Robots, taking lands becomes extremely costly and no longer worthy. After then, infrastructure, districts, and Eurekas became my source of growth, and all of these cannot sustain if not enough land is taken in the early game. Expansion indeed fuels development, which is why it was practiced by empires in history, and in Civ VI this concept is reflected through other mechanics involving diminishing returns, as loyalty, amenities, housing, and Dark Ages hinder one's ability to overextend. In summary, Civ VI's procedural rhetoric argues around the importance of conquering lands, and secondly utilizing lands, for ancient civilizations.
