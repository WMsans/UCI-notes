# Prompt
Games let you do things you'd never do in real life — and then quietly hand you a story about why that was fine, or funny, or thrilling, or no big deal. This assignment is about catching Undertale in the act.

Describe a specific choice you made while playing — sparing someone, fighting someone, sparing then fighting out of sheer tedium, whatever it was. What story did you tell yourself about it in the moment? (“I was just curious,” “they had it coming,” “it's just pixels,” etc.) Then, using the device list from lecture (context, characters, plot structure, interface, dialogue, pacing, quests, cut scenes, artifacts), name which device you think shaped that self-story, and briefly explain how. # Answer

I killed Papyrus the first time we went toe-to-toe, and the story I made up about it was straightforward: he is the boss, and bosses are what keep you from advancing further. The narrative device at play here was story structure. Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al. (2020) call progression games "series of chapters, which all end with an exciting climax that is usually preceded by a difficult 'boss fight' to give a greater sense of achievement" (p. 215). That's how Snowdin does it too. He lays out puzzles for me to solve, he keeps promising me that he will capture me, and eventually, he meets me on the road near town, on my way to Waterfall. So, I had already sorted him out as a "gate" character long before we ever squared off. As Bruner (1986) states, I operated under the paradigmatic mode, categorizing him and solving the puzzle that was presented to me despite the game throwing me narrative-mode information about his motives, including spaghetti, puzzles, and Royal Guard dreams. What shook the boss paradigm was what came next. Sans no longer appeared. His station as a guard in the forest remained empty and none in the village wanted to talk about it. This phenomenon is known as an embedded narrative and it was described by Jenkins (2005). According to the textbook, the most intense narrative experiences come "in games where our actions have noticeable plot consequences" (Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al., 2020, p. 215). Thus, for me, it was this experience when I realized what had happened. As a result, I returned to the saved file and found Sans at his post as if nothing had ever occurred. I cannot make up my mind about this part of the game right now. The very save file that helped me realize that I lost something allowed me to undo my actions which means that I understood that Papyrus was a human being but still treated my mistake like I would treat failure of the puzzle.
