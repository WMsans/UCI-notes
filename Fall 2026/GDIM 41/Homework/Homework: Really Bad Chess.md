# Answer

Zach Gage's *Really Bad Chess* replaces chess's fixed, balanced opening position with a randomly generated set of pieces, and that change is **constitutive**. Salen and Zimmerman define constitutive rules as a game's "underlying formal structures that exist 'below the surface' of the rules presented to players" (2004, p. 130). In standard chess that formal layer fixes both how pieces move and exactly what material each side begins with, and the course concept of rules explains why: rules are the formal system that generates play, not just the setup players read (Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al., 2024, ch. 5). *Really Bad Chess* keeps the movement logic and the checkmate condition but discards the fixed initial state. Its algorithm sums assigned piece values and generates a board whose total matches a target rank, so the balance of the two sets shifts with the player's rank: early ranks favor the player, the middle is even, and the top ranks favor the A.I. (Suellentrop, 2016; Webster, 2016). Because it rewrites the game's mathematical starting conditions and balance — not the etiquette or instructions around play — the change is constitutive. I feel this personally: I know how to play chess but hold no openings or strategy, and the randomization erases opening theory entirely — there is no e4–e5 line when the position changes every game. Climbing to roughly the 35% mark, I have won in a handful of moves off a lucky draw (once with four queens), and when the pieces came out badly the fun came from twisting an ugly position back around.

Someone could reasonably contest this by calling the change **operational**: "you receive a random set of pieces" reads as a new setup instruction, and the real logic of chess — movement and mate — is untouched, so the constitutive game is intact. That does not change my mind. The operational rules are precisely what stays fixed, while the mathematically defined starting state and material equality, which Salen and Zimmerman place below the surface of play, are exactly what Gage rewrites.
