# Answer

The aesthetic that defines the experience of playing Civilization VI would be the challenge calibrated to remain within reach. Challenge as a aesthetic is defined as "game as obstacle course" in the textbook chapter 5. Mechanics are described by the MDA framework as "rules and code" whereas dynamics is the way in which "the game actually plays based on the mechanics," and aesthetics are the emotional reactions to the dynamics (Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al., 2024, p. 51). The mechanics in Civ VI, such as tile yield, district adjacency bonuses, and Eureka and Inspiration boosts that discount technology after accomplishing the listed tasks, create a dynamic of expanding in a compound manner. The ui/ux also contributes to this aesthetic in addition to the mechanics. The tech tree gives information about the requirements of each boost, and the placement lens shows the adjacency yield, so a quarry becomes half of a completed objective. The game always present a challenge of choosing a  resource that is nearby enough to take, instead merely challenge of expansion. When playing, I would claim a resource, spend it on a Settler who could claim more land, and the new land would contain the next deposit, one city away; I would then look up an hour later, never having made the choice to spend it. In the best light, this constitutes Sid Meier's "series of interesting choices" (p. 50); there was no right choice, only more or less sane ones. The specific moment of my gameplay I want to focus on  is when I ending a turn with a Settler on his way out to explore the unknown, a Builder a turn away from completing a mine, and a technology two turns away from being completed, all of these goals achieved simultaneously and none of them my reason for playing the game. This is why it is called an aesthetic challenge rather than discovery or expression (p. 51); discovery suggests that the map was the fun part of the game, but for me, the map was nothing more than a list of upcoming challenges.
