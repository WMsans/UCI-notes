# Answer

In Stefan Zweig's *Chess Story* ("Schachnovelle", 1942), chess is depicted as the last territory which belongs solely to a human being. It is a story where the game is not an act of survival in addition to merely sports. It was a claustrophobic but exhilarating experience of reading. In this novella, Dr. B who is held in solitary confinement by the Gestapo, steals a book of master games and in order to keep his sanity in tact he replays those games and plays himself thousands of times in his mind until this practice poisons him and he has to be taken into hospital (Zweig, 1942/2006). Through Clifford Geertz's interpretive anthropology, the game of chess in this context becomes deep play. Such form of symbolic practice which acts "as a story they tell themselves about themselves" (Geertz, 1973, p. 448), where personal experience becomes a text for reading. Therefore, in the novella chess symbolizes much more than one thing. He escapes from reality through being absorbed in the challenge of chess (Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al., 2024, ch. 6), which happens to be precisely his obsession that almost kills him. A fair critique might be made that Zweig is warning against addiction rather than glorifying freedom, especially in light of the fact that Dr. B's liberation is conditional upon his refusal to ever touch another chess piece again. Nevertheless, these two interpretations are not contradictory because the very same absorption that allows the chessboard to become a safe haven is also what transforms it into a prison, and hence chess symbolizes man’s desire to escape into the world governed by rules.
